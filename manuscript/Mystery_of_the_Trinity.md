# Chapter 1 — Icons of the Trinity

## Opening Prayer

> I bind unto myself the name, the strong name of the Trinity, by invocation of the same, the three in one, and one in three, of whom all nature hath creation, eternal Father, Spirit, Word. Praise to the Lord of my salvation. Salvation is of Christ the Lord. Amen.

## St. Patrick and the Shamrock

The impetus for this retreat, and why I picked this particular topic, was to recognize another saint who is during the month of March — one who, though he's not of the same status as Saint Joseph, gets maybe more celebration, at least in our country and in Ireland. And that is the saint, Saint Patrick. Saint Patrick was known for his evangelization of the peoples of Ireland during the 400s — after having been a slave in Ireland, having learned the language and escaping, he came back to evangelize the same place where he was held in slavery. And he was going up against a pagan religion — the religion of Druidism — a pagan religion that saw spirits in nature, a respect for trees and certainly plants, and had its own customs and rituals, high priests, wizards, and very many problems morally. And the biggest problem was that they didn't believe in God and needed to be converted. St. Patrick ached to be able to help convert this land, and he came in, and having known the language, he was able to preach to the people of Ireland — and many miracles attested to the power of God with him. And St. Patrick came in with an altogether different religion, having the miracle of the Paschal fire that could not be extinguished, and then other miracles as well — one person raised his arm against him with a sword, and his arm froze until he repented and confessed his faith in God.

And when he preached, he did not shy away from the central mystery of our faith, and that is the Trinity. We have the prayer of St. Patrick, St. Patrick's Breastplate, with which we began this conference. And we also have the story of the shamrock, how the shamrock was used to illustrate to a people who may be caught up in botanical language how something can be both one and three, that it can share the same life, be connected in the same way, but yet be distinct at the same time.

St. Patrick led with this because it is what our Lord told us to lead with. "All power in heaven and on earth has been given to me." So St. Patrick did not shy from holding up a shamrock and saying, "Three and one," to give them something to grip onto in their minds, to have an image that could later be corrected, modified, and through which they could enter into a theological understanding of the mystery at hand — but to give them something to begin with. It was so crucial for evangelization for the people of Ireland, at which St. Patrick was incredibly successful.

St. Patrick knew the language of the people of Ireland, for he had been enslaved there for six years before he escaped, and he understood the way they thought and the way they worshipped. And so he drew for them a botanical analogy, taking up a shamrock, a young clover — what exact plant it was does not matter, for we know it at least has three leaves and one stem joining all three leaves together. And it was a simple analogy, fraught with potential heresy — but just as this is one shamrock with three leaves, so it can be three persons and one God. The analogy limps. It's not the full reality of the Trinity. It's not saying that God the Father is a part of God, and God the Son is a part of God, and God the Holy Spirit is a part of God. It's not saying that — but you can see how it could be twisted in that way. But it was a way to enter into the language of those people and to help them understand, with some simplicity, what it means to be one and three. That's, for some, a helpful analogy. And when we were children, we learned about the shamrock or the clover — how we can have three in one. It suffices. From that, we build on distinctions and understandings, and theology starts to do the work, notionally, of completing what's going on within this mystery.

## Another Icon of the Trinity

I want to turn to another icon of the Trinity. An icon just means image. An icon, in particular — when we talk about icons, we think of the icons of the Eastern Catholic and the Eastern Orthodox. And in particular, I want to look at a Russian icon from Andrei Rublev of the Trinity. This is included in all of your packets, and it would be really helpful if you can take it out and look at it.

It's not usual to depict the Trinity. There are some instances in art, but typically people would shy away from depicting the Father and the Holy Spirit under any kind of personal form. And we would depict Christ, because the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. We can show Christ, and we can show what he looked like, and there's no affront there. But to show the Father and the Holy Spirit — well, this is hard to do.

Andrei Rublev wrote this icon — for icons are not painted, they are written, because the Word became flesh, and the Word is a written word, as well as a spoken word, and a read word. This is written in that same way, to communicate about the saints, or, in this case, these three angels depicted here.

## The Hospitality of Abraham

It recounts the story from Genesis 18. In Genesis 18, there are three visitors sent from God, three angels, that showed up to Abraham and Sarah's tent. Abraham and Sarah quickly, out of an abundance of hospitality, prepared food for their guests, who sat with them and ate. They were near the Oak of Mamre, and there they fed. And it was there that the angels, the three messengers of God, communicated to Abraham that Sarah was to conceive in her old age. And that's where eventually Isaac would be born.

And the scene of these three angels, in retrospect, we would look back and say they are a sort of representation of the Trinity. This is a very normal thing for us to do, having been given the revelation that God is both one and three, through Christ, the incarnate Word, and the sending forth of the Holy Spirit. We now know that God has a triune nature. And we can look back in the Old Testament, where this mystery was still hidden, and see resonances of God's Trinitarian nature.

And so we might say the same of the first chapter of Genesis, when God says, "Let us create man in our own image and likeness" [quotation wording unverified]. We who now know of God as one and three think that the "us," the plural, refers to the Trinity. And we are not wrong. Originally, it referred to the angels, the choirs of angels — "Let us create," God would speak to them. But for us, we can retroactively understand that in its fullest sense, God creates from the Trinity. Likewise, when three angels show up to Abraham and Sarah, we can say this is an Old Testament representation of the Trinity.

## Reading the Icon: One Divinity

The icon has a lot of meanings in there, and it's actually very helpful for us to look at art. It engages our imagination in a way that's very helpful, especially for a mystery that often confounds us. The mystery of the Trinity does confound us, and there are reasons for that.

But looking at this icon — if everyone can take a look at it — we see there's three angels around a table, and the other settings, other parts of it, are hard to see, but I will explain them as we go. These three angels, they all are dressed in one color that is common to all three of them, and that color is blue. The one on the left, you see a little bit of blue. The one in the middle has a lot of blue, and then the one on the right has a good bit of blue that's visible. Blue, in the iconographic tradition, represents divinity. So they all share the same divinity.

## The Holy Spirit and the Mountain

Likewise, all three of them have this scepter, this stave — I believe that's the word, stave? We'll go with that — in their left hands, keeping their right hands free for the decisions, the edicts, going forth from the power of their right hand. That is the power, the authority, they have. They all have the same power. They all share the same divinity. And they all have the same face. It's the same face, same hairstyle. I don't think there's any differences in the wings or anything else about them in their physical appearance. They are sitting in a circle — and a circle represents eternity; the circle represents, for them, this loop. So in that way, too, there is a divinity within the way they are depicted. Where we notice differences are in the colors that each one of them wears, starting with the angel on the right. See, that angel is wearing green over top of the blue, the blue tunic underneath. The green represents life. In this time of spring, there will soon be green everywhere to replace the brown. Green represents the new life. It is associated with the Holy Spirit, that breath of life that comes over the world every spring. In the East, they don't wear red for the Holy Spirit, for Pentecost. They wear green, because of the new life that comes from the Holy Spirit. So the angel on the right represents the Holy Spirit, and behind him, in the distance, that figure is that of a mountain.

A mountain is associated with the Holy Spirit, especially in the Old Testament. We recall how many good things happen on a mountain. Mountains symbolize the Holy Spirit and prophecy. And we go to the New Testament — and where do we find mountains? Mountains, we might think of Mount Tabor, where he went with Peter, James, and John, and they were all enveloped in the cloud — the cloud that symbolized the Holy Spirit — and then the Father's voice was heard. And there you have a Trinitarian exposition in the New Testament. And then of course the mountain — or really rather the hill of Calvary — where Christ gave forth the Spirit. The Holy Spirit would be connected with mountains, especially in the icon tradition.

## The Son in the Middle, the Father on the Left

The central figure in this icon, in the middle, in addition to the blue on the outside, is wearing this purplish, reddish, maybe more brownish color. And the brownish red — we have the red of — it's kind of in between. I don't know what color to call that; my color identification is poor. Some would say it's more of a brownish red, and that could symbolize the Word that went forth, that went down to the earth and walked on the dirt of this earth — he who walked here for thirty-three years — the red representing the blood of Christ poured out for our salvation, or even the purple representing the kingly power. And with that kingly power, too, it's symbolized in the gold stripe along his right shoulder. That symbolizes his kingly power.

Looking at his right hand: his fingers are extended, two fingers extended out. This was a typical posture used when raising your hand in blessing in the East. The two fingers represented the humanity and divinity of Christ — what we call the hypostatic union, where God is united with us in our human nature: true God and true man in Christ, fully human and fully divine. The other three fingers represent the Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

And then behind him, there is the tree, which makes sense in the context of the story of the Oak of Mamre. But we also think back to that original tree where original sin happened, when Eve plucked that fruit from the tree of knowledge of good and evil. And by a tree, humanity fell. We also look forward to that tree of life that is the cross, when Christ goes up on that cross and dies for us. And Christ is the new Adam, and he was the one to ascend a tree, to become the fruit of the tree of life — of the cross. So the tree behind him goes along with the identification of the middle angel as the second person of the Holy Trinity.

On the left, obviously, would have to be the Father. Now, his outer garment, it's hard to identify. In this picture, it looks pinkish, purplish, with some blue hints to it. It's original — when it was originally written, the colors were meant to be hard to identify, sort of ephemeral and beyond our ability to really name. And this represents the Father.

And behind him is a house. Now, the house of Abraham and Sarah, sure. Sure, but also we recall our Lord's words in John 14, "In my Father's house are many rooms." Or we can go back to Luke 2, "Do you not know I must be about my Father's business and my Father's house" [quotation wording unverified], that is, the temple. The temple in Jerusalem being the house of the Father, especially for Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity incarnate. So there's the Father's house there, symbolized by the house of Abraham and Sarah.

## The Postures, the Chalice, and the Empty Place

Now, the postures of all three of them: you see the Father, his head is up, but the other two, the heads are actually pointed towards — they are looking at the Father. This deference to the Father recognizes that both the Son and the Holy Spirit proceed from the Father. And that is symbolized in that. And they both go back to the Father and reference the Father — and that is the symbolism of all of us, for we are all tending to the Father in our own journeys of faith.

The whole image, if you kind of step back, you see sort of the top of an hourglass — look between the figures on the right and the left: sort of the top of a chalice. And in the middle of that sort of visual chalice, we have Christ, whose blood is poured out for us. Of course, they're all around a table, and they all are sitting on thrones — that's equal dignity there, of course, as well. It's noted also that not only are the heads of the Son and the Holy Spirit pointed towards the Father, but even the mountain is as if it was bowing towards the Father, so that the Father might be glorified in all things.

And then we have the chalice, or the bowl, in the center on the table, symbolizing the food of heaven, the bread of life, the chalice of salvation, which we partake in the New Testament. This is the heavenly bread, the bread of heaven — the bread that Moses and Aaron ate and drank when they went up onto Mount Sinai. This is the heavenly meal that is happening: the bread of the Father, that the Holy Spirit and the Son share in, and that we participate in mystically through the Holy Eucharist. And looking at the bowl — it is also a sort of a bowl shape, and that represents the sacrifices of the Old Testament, the sacrificial bowls. I pronounce "bow" and "bowl" very similarly, so I apologize. But the shape of it, though — it's actually not quite perfectly symmetrical, and that's on purpose. And so the sacrifices of the Old Testament give way to the bread of the new covenant of the Eucharist. And then, oddly, there is this rectangle with a little inner rectangle in the corner on this altar as well. And I had trouble trying to figure out what that was. I read somewhere that that rectangle represents the four corners of the world, that the Trinity is necessarily meant for evangelization, to spread the love of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, out of the four corners. But there's another meaning of that rectangle. In the altars of the East, there was a compartment in the altar where the relics of martyrs were placed.

And this is the part of the table where all three persons of the Trinity are not occupying, and we, as the viewer, are now part of this icon. We are meant, in some ways, to be invited into this feast, to share in the hospitality of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. And how do we do so? And it's very poignant: the way in which we share in that hospitality is through martyrdom, through the witness of our lives. "Unless a seed fall to the ground and dies, it remains but a seed. But if it dies, it produces much fruit" [quotation wording unverified]. Our insertion into the Holy Trinity through Christ is through a sharing in his sufferings and his death on the cross.

There are probably other parts of this image — there could be fruit drawn from it. And with our own prayer, I recommend taking this to the chapel, or in your own time of silent recollection, to look at this image. If you come up with other possible meanings, do feel free to share either with me during the times of conferences or afterwards, when we are able to talk.

## The Third Icon: The Sign of the Cross

But there's one more important image of the Trinity I want to draw attention to. And that's not the shamrock, and that's not the icon of Andrei Rublev. And that's the icon of the sign of the cross.

Catholics, we are very used to beginning prayer and ending prayer, bulk [possibly: both] ending it with the sign of the cross. We toggle prayer on and off through the sign of the cross. That's not true! You don't have to do the sign of the cross for prayer to count. For example, night prayer — we do the sign of the cross at the end of it, and that's okay. But very typically, we begin and end prayer in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. The sign of the cross consists of words: "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Amen." Those words are first uttered over us — with water poured three times over our heads — when we are baptized. Our baptism is that insertion into the Trinity where we share in the life of God. The Holy Trinity has a dwelling within us, and those words are important. Each one of them is important. "In the name of" — in the name, not names, although we name Father, Son, and Holy Spirit separately — to say they have one name, it emphasizes the unity of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and their equality in the Trinity. And it reminds us of our baptisms — which we will talk about more later — for we are baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and those are the words that made us new creations. So when we pray, we call that to mind — not sloppily, but with great intentionality — remembering that we have been baptized into this Trinity and that we have been given entrance into the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

And of course, this is not just something we made up. It is Christ's words himself, baptizing them — he says, "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." This is from our Lord in Matthew 28, his final words in Matthew's gospel before he ascends to heaven.

These words matter. Every word matters — even the "and" of thus [possibly: these]. And it's good for us, when we pray, to pray with a reverence that we might say, "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," and not to do the sloppy "Father, Son, Holy Spirit." The words matter.

## The Motions: Father and Son

Not only that, the actions matter. When we lift our hands towards our foreheads, this upward motion of our hands goes along with "In the name of the Father." That upward motion is this pilgrimage all of us are making to the embrace of God the Father. We all are ascending. We who have come forth from the Father are now meant to go towards him in our prayer. This is that simple glance directed towards heaven that St. Thérèse talks about when she says, "What is prayer but a simple glance directed towards heaven?" [quotation wording unverified] In the name of the Father.

And then our hand goes down, passing over our heart, and with that downward motion, we say, "and of the Son." The downward motion is that of Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity, descending, going forth from the Father and entering into our humanity. Entering not only into our humanity, but taking all of our sufferings onto himself, all of our misery, all of our sin — the descent of Christ into the depths of human misery, so that all human suffering is taken on him. And through his passion and death, he goes down into the depths of all human misery — he goes down into the depths of hell for us, that complete descent. He undergoes that out of love for us. He binds us to himself so we might be lifted up to the Father. And of the Son — crossing over our hearts in the process.

## The Spirit's Crossing — and the Amen

And then we go to "and of the Holy Spirit," going from left to right. In the Western tradition, it's left to right. In the Coptic tradition, it's left to right. In the Eastern tradition, it's right to left. And there are reasons why it's done differently. In the East, they go from right shoulder to left shoulder, because traditionally the good thief was on the right, and they want to recognize the good thief first. But the emphasis there is this Holy Spirit that is now among us. This is the Holy Spirit that has been sent among us. Pour it out for the forgiveness of sins. This descent of the Holy Spirit is now with us. We are living in the age of the Holy Spirit among us, and we cross over, going from shoulder to shoulder — we cross over our heart. And as we cross our hearts — the Holy Spirit — we cross our lungs, for the Holy Spirit is our breath, and we, as members of Christ's body, the Church, are animated by that Spirit. By the mystery of the pouring forth of the Holy Spirit — at the crucifixion, at the Resurrection, in the upper room, and on the feast of Pentecost — that Holy Spirit is among us, animating us. We live and move and have our being with, through, and in the Holy Spirit, and that is how we are to live morally, to live in God's grace, to live the life of the Spirit, as we love God and love one another.

The last word of the sign of the cross is no less important. Amen. We think of "amen," we're like, "What does amen mean?" "Oh, it means I believe. It means I'm committing to this." And it does mean that. When we say "Amen," we are saying, "I am staking my life on this. It will not be moved. This is my stability. I am grounded in this truth. You will not move me. No storms can disturb me, because I am putting this — this is my stability, my surety. You cannot take this from me." Your Amen is a whole gift of yourself, leaving nothing behind. And that's why the sign of the cross is there at the beginning of every prayer and at the end — for we live our whole lives enclosed within this mystery of the Holy Trinity.

This "amen" is no less important than the other words we pray, because this is the most central mystery of our faith, especially when we pray "Amen" in regards to the sign of the cross.

## Who Are We Crossing? Ourselves

And let's not forget the central image, the central icon of the sign of the cross. Who are we crossing? Ourselves. We are images of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We are made in his image and likeness. We have the Trinity in us, inscribed in our very natures, and we have the Trinity in us through baptism. We are temples of the Holy Spirit, intimacy with Christ, the inner room from which we pray to the Father in secret. We are all of these things.

And the relationality within the Trinity, the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — we are made for relationship. We are made to pour ourselves out and to receive, to give, and to receive, to reciprocate. We are inscribed in our very natures, so that when God looked upon creation all the other days, he said, "It is good" [quotation wording unverified]. He looked upon us created in his image and likeness, male and female, meant to enshrine that image of the Holy Trinity in their very bodies. He looked at us and said, "You are very good" [quotation wording unverified], for we are images of God's triune love.

What that means, how that is lived out, how this changes not just how we approach our faith but everything in our life, we will discuss in the future conferences.


# Chapter 2 — Mystery

*O my God, Trinity whom I adore, help me forget myself entirely so to establish myself in you, unmovable and peaceful as if my soul were already in eternity. May nothing be able to trouble my peace or make me leave you, O my unchanging God, but may each minute bring me more deeply into your mystery! Grant my soul peace. Make it your heaven, your beloved dwelling and the place of your rest. May I never abandon you there, but may I be there, whole and entire, completely vigilant in my faith, entirely adoring, and wholly given over to your creative action.*

*— Prayer of St. Elizabeth of the Trinity*

## The Graveyard of Heresies

The Trinity is the most central mystery in our faith, and it matters very much. It also matters what we mean by the word mystery. We think of the Trinity as something that we can't possibly understand, and it confounds us.

And I am sure that many of you have heard homilies on Trinity Sunday — the one Sunday a year when priests feel compelled to at least mention the Trinity, the central mystery of our faith — homilies that say we can't possibly speak too much about the Trinity, because if we do, we might fall into heresy. If you talk for longer than 10 minutes, you will likely say something wrong, and there are many Trinitarian heresies.

We look over the history of Christianity, especially in the early years, and — as Pope Benedict says, that is, as Joseph Ratzinger in *Introduction to Christianity* — we see "a graveyard of heresies." We want out of that graveyard.

And we don't even have our minds wrapped around what the problems were for the early Christians — except for a few things here and there, like "Arianism is wrong." So we don't want to get into those things. Or even — I mentioned the shamrock of St. Patrick, and people would say, "Wow, that's modalism, Patrick." We don't want to go anywhere near this heresy, so we conclude, "The Trinity is a mystery, we can't understand it," and we stop there. But we read in the Catechism that the Trinity is the central Christian mystery — and I actually recommend you read the full passages about the Trinity from the Catechism.

I once heard a speaker at a FOCUS conference tell about a conversation he had when he was a Protestant. His Catholic friend got him to meet up with a Jesuit priest — supposedly a good Jesuit — and the priest asked him, "What is the central mystery of our faith?" The man, since he was a Protestant, answered, trying to be a little bit edgy: "Justification by faith alone." And the priest said, "It's the Trinity." The man said, "Oh yeah — that's right. It's the love that made the sun and the stars: the love shared between Father, Son, and the Holy Spirit that spills out into all creation. And then, even though we fell, by God's mercy extended to us through the Trinity — God the Father of mercy; the death and resurrection of his Son; the Holy Spirit sent among us for the forgiveness of sins — the whole action of the Trinity comes to redeem us and bring us back to himself. The Trinity is the central faith. It's the Trinity that we will gaze on — and partake of — through Christ, in the beatific vision, where God gives us his very self through which to understand him. This is the central mystery of our faith. This is what matters more than everything else."

And every single time, we just dismiss it as: "Okay, the Trinity is something I can't understand. I'm not even going to try. It's three and one — three persons, one God. They are all one God; somehow they are connected very deeply, but they are distinct. How they are distinct, I don't know how that works. But I know Jesus was the second person of the Holy Trinity, and the Holy Spirit is the third person, and there's God the Father. But was God the Father sort of the God of the Old Testament? Was Jesus the God of the New Testament?" And no — that's wrong. That's Marcionism. Don't think that way. We start messing up with this, and we say, "Okay, that's enough. I'm done. I don't want to think about it. I can't understand it. I actually don't study theology. The idea of even reading a few pages from the Catechism is actually overwhelming to me. Therefore I'm not going to mess with it." In fact, the Trinity is just something that you study when you're on the way to being a priest. That's it. You know, in seminary you can study it. Maybe there are some extra courses. You can read St. Augustine — you read one of those books. Maybe you read St. Elizabeth of the Trinity, and you think, "Okay, she really digs the Trinity. That's cool. That's great — for her. But for me, I'm just gonna simplify it and not think about it too much."

The Trinity is a mystery. We can't understand it. It's there. It's a huge part of our faith, but we just can't get close to it at all. So we don't even try.

Although — we invoke the Trinity every sign of the cross, every Glory Be, and every time at Mass in the doxology — it's all three of them together. It's a doxology, a praise to the glory of God and of the three persons, and it expresses how God is mediated through, with, and in Christ, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, and how all things are directed to God the Father. Okay, sure — it happens all the time. But we don't let it have any meaning for us. We are inoculated from the mystery of the Trinity. It's something we hear, something we practice, but not something that we really get at all.

## Mystery Novels and Jigsaw Puzzles

When we hear the word mystery, one thing that comes to mind is mystery novels — Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, Father Brown. I know Brother Kurt likes the Sherlock Holmes — the Jeremy Brett versions, especially, which you can find on YouTube. Those are mystery stories. Mysteries, in that case, are something that need to be solved. There's something we don't know. Who committed the murder? How did it happen? What were the motivations? Who needs to be arrested? That's solved in a mystery novel.

There are pieces of information that are not there, or if they are present, they require us to deduce from the material given to arrive at the conclusion. So mysteries are usually: if there is something that we don't know and we need to know, we'll try to find that out. Or it's something where we just need to piece everything together. It's solved. Like a jigsaw puzzle, we put it together and we see the picture, and there you go. Or it's like a problem in physics: you come up with the answer through calculating, putting in the different measurements, and you get your answer. Or a problem in engineering: you need to optimize something, using the things that we have on hand — the materials and the designs — and within the design limitations, you optimize. That's a problem. And we reduce much of what we do in our life to problems that need to be solved.

## What We Mean by Christian Mystery

That way of seeing mystery is not what we mean by Christian mystery. When we think about the mystery of faith — the central mystery of our faith being the Trinity — we also might think of the Holy Eucharist. We might know that in the Eastern Catholic tradition the sacraments are called mysteries. And the Blessed Sacrament is called the mysteries, very simply.

When we say that, we don't mean, oh, a problem to be solved, that we can't know it. It's not possible to know it. We mean something that is so knowable that it overwhelms our ability to know.

## The Sun

If you were to go outside on a sunny day and try to look at the sun — are you able to see the sun? In one sense, yes, because it's so much light; in another sense, no. You can't really see the sun. You can't make out its circular form in the sky. You can't see its outline. All you see is blinding light.

So you would not say the sun is invisible. You'd say the sun is so visible that it overwhelms your ability to see. Your retina cannot handle that sun. But at the same time, the sun is useful in analogy, because the sun is that by which we see everything else.

And a mystery is likewise: as Jacques Maritain said — he was a philosopher, a Thomist philosopher in the 20th century — mysteries are "pregnant with intelligibility." They are so full of things we can know that it's too much. We are a finite intellect in the midst of something infinite, and it's overwhelming.

So what do we do? We can try to get away from it, treat it like a problem, go nowhere near it — or enter into it.

## Mysteries Implicate the Self

Another philosopher in the 20th century — his name was Gabriel Marcel — belonged to a school of philosophy called personalism. And you might recognize that as the school of philosophy that Karol Wojtyła was also involved in, on the other side of Europe.

He said that what distinguishes mysteries from problems is that mysteries implicate the self. You are involved in the mystery. When we approach something as a problem, we approach it as separate from ourselves. It is outside of us. It can be measured. It can be analyzed. And if it remains unchanged, we remain unchanged. And it can be solved.

Gabriel Marcel said that many of the problems of the 20th century — with their massive amounts of death on scales never before envisioned — came from treating mysteries as problems.

## Persons Are Not Problems

St. John Henry Newman said that each of us is "an infinite abyss of existence." That is to say, each of us is a mystery, so knowable that it overwhelms anyone's ability to know. We are even a mystery unto ourselves. When we fully know ourselves, it overwhelms us. And there is a sense in which, when we know ourselves, we know God. Self-knowledge and knowledge of God are brought together, and we might call that the virtue of humility — when we know ourselves, we know God, and when we see the discrepancy between the two, we act accordingly. But to treat another person as a problem, to use them as part of an equation, is to dismiss them at their very core.

This was the "solution" to the "Jewish problem" in 1930s and 40s Germany: to treat a whole section of society, a whole accumulation of mysteries of persons, as problems — problems to be solved. And when you're dealing with persons, the solution is death. It was an extension of living in a technical world — optimizing everything, having control over everything. Persons are not problems. They themselves are mysteries — not to be rejected, not to be cast out, not to be solved, but to be loved, to be known.

## The Two Aspects of Mystery

And we might think, well, that was a terrible time in our history. We've certainly learned from that — and hopefully we have. But we've moved now in the 21st century into the matters of the technical, the problematical. Everything is reduced to a problem. Everything can be solved now by artificial intelligence. Everything can be solved by analysis, by measurement.

And there are many that would even espouse as their way of viewing the world — their central worldview — that science is their God, because everything can be analyzed and measured, and they can be kept safe from it. This is fundamentally an attitude of fear that causes us to reduce the mysteries, the persons around us, into problems, and to deal with them on the merely technical level. And I think one of the reasons why we get so anxious all the time is that we're in a world that only lives as problems.

But to approach something as a mystery, we recognize that it is so knowable that it overwhelms us, and we are enveloped in it. The two aspects are these: first, mystery is so knowable that it overwhelms us, and second, it necessarily involves us. We are implicated in it.

We use the word "implicated" to talk about being implicated in a crime. I buy a bike from a friend at a really good deal and recognize that the friend stole the bike from someone else. I'm implicated in that crime. I'm involved in it. I'm not separate from it. I can't be detached from it as a mere external observer.

But are we then cut off entirely, irreparably? When we are about to surrender our weakness to God, we say, "Lord, I messed this up. Can you do something about it?" — and then we let it go. And then, if we reflect on that particular thing that we did — which we are foolish and shameful about — maybe a couple of years later, we look back and say, "Oh, wow — the Lord took care of that relationship, or the Lord took care of that situation." I didn't need to say that the world has ended just with that. God's mercy transforms, and even in that moment, we can see God loving us — that he's not far from us, even when we want to be far from him. Sin might be that attempt: "I'm going to take matters into my own hands and do the thing that I know I ought not to do" — but even there, we are caught up in the mystery of God's mercy. It still requires us to choose to receive that mercy, but we go back and we can see that God's goodness is there, and his fidelity is unchanging, even though ours is not. Consider the fifth station: Simon of Cyrene helps Jesus carry the cross. St. John Henry Newman says that Christ could have carried the cross by himself — he could have chosen to — but he allowed us to have a share in that work.

## Elizabeth and the Moon

I'll tell a story to illustrate this point about mystery involving the self. Several years ago, my sister's family was coming back from eastern Pennsylvania to visit my brother-in-law's family, and so I talked to my niece Elizabeth, who was five years old at that time. I just asked her, "Elizabeth, how was your trip?" She said to me, "Uncle PJ" — because she calls me Uncle PJ — "Uncle PJ, guess what? When we go in the car and we drive, the moon follows us."

And my heart sunk. I felt bad for her. And in my mind, I'm thinking, this is like one of those Santa Claus moments. Elizabeth, you're five years old. You need to know something about reality. The moon does not follow you. It is so far away that any change in our distance here at 60 or 70 miles per hour — you will not be able to notice any change in position in the sky relative to you. It only looks like it's following you because of the great distance it is away from you. And it's so unbelievably large that it seems to only be stuck in the sky. And as you travel, it looks like it's also going with you. That is not the case.

And I'm thinking, okay, how can I prove this to her? And this is happening in a very short period of time, because I'm thinking, okay, I need to prove to her this is not true. And maybe I could apply the principle of non-contradiction, or some variant of it. Why don't we take you, Elizabeth, in one car at night, and your sister, Mary Margaret, and put her in another car? Okay? And why don't you drive in separate directions, and both of you observe the moon. It can't be following both of you. So that can't be true. You must look for another explanation.

So quickly, I said to Elizabeth, "Elizabeth, that's not true. The moon doesn't follow you. It's so far away. It looks like it's following you." And she interrupted me. "No, Uncle PJ — it really follows me."

Elizabeth was right, in a sense. She approached the moon as a mystery. And its own movements were not separate from hers. She was involved in it.

For me, the moon was a problem. It could be analyzed. We could measure it. I could explain the different things about it. I could talk about why it looks like it has different shapes — that's due to the phases. I could talk about why we only see one side of it, even though it is a sphere — because it is tidally locked to the Earth. I would love to be able to explain all these things — to talk about how those shapes you see on its surface are actually impact craters from asteroids and meteors and other space debris, and that the moon actually acts as a cosmic vacuum cleaner protecting the Earth. Oh, there are so many things you could talk about with the moon that are interesting in their own way.

## Squashing Wonder

But she saw the moon as if it had a personality, as if it had a care for her. In my own fear — not wanting to think something cared for me, only to find out that it doesn't — I thought it was necessary to dissuade her from that childish notion, and in so doing, I squashed her wonder — or at least I tried to. She would not let me. The moon was a mystery to her. She was involved in it.

When we think about God — God is a mystery. He overwhelms us. There's so much to know. We can't possibly contain God. You cannot hug a mountain. Or if you try to, you'll just look silly, because your arms do not go around it. You cannot encompass the mystery of God. You cannot grasp it into your hand. It is beyond you. And no matter what you attempt, it slips through your fingers like sand on the seashore.

## Augustine's Boy

We remember the old legend of St. Augustine, walking along a beach and finding a boy, digging a hole, taking a pail and going to the ocean and dumping ocean water into that hole on the seashore. "What are you doing, boy?" "I'm pouring the ocean into this hole." "Well, you can't do that. The ocean will not fit. As much as you might try, it will not go into that. All the water you pour in will just seep into the sand and return to the ocean." And that is also the case, the boy says, when you try to understand God as one in three.

## How Far Analysis Goes

The Trinity overwhelms us. We can analyze it to an extent. We can divide it up. We can talk about its different parts. We can say that there is the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Each one of them is a subsistent relation. We can say that each one of them has their own role in coming down into this world. In some way or other, they go forth — the missions of the Trinity — to draw us into the Trinity.

We can talk about this in history. We can talk about this in theology. We can apply the dictums of philosophy to this. We can expand our mind and try to grasp as much as we can, but we still find ourselves unable to. But the most proper response to the Trinity is to understand that we are involved in it already, that we are implicated in that very mystery.

And it's not wrong to study the Trinity — in fact, there are many things to study about it, and that's why we actually have teachings about it: dogma, which is official Church teaching. Flannery O'Connor said: "Dogma is the guardian of mystery. The doctrines are spiritually significant in ways that we cannot fathom." So it's not wrong to study dogmas, and it's not wrong to treat the Trinity as a problem to be solved — but we have to also recognize that it must be a mystery that we adore.

Even knowing something means that we are participating in God, for there is not anything that is separate from God. He comes to save us. He comes to save you — not just us in general, but you. You.

## A Way of Approaching All Reality

Mystery is not just a category that we can put things in. It's a way of approaching reality. I can say this table is a mystery. I'm involved in it. I have a history with it. I have a story to tell. I have a way in which I'm connected to it. It's not just a horizontal plane elevated with finite width and length. Now this table is something that separates me from you, but it also is something that allows me to be here with you. The same thing is true of the tables in front of you. We recognize that we have a part to play in it.

I guess this lamp is a mystery. I can't see the light, but by that light, I see other things. Logically, I don't think an engineered product is, but this lamp has memories for me. It's been here since my first retreat. I replaced the light bulb in it myself during COVID — that was a hard time, and this place was for me a place of solace, and every light bulb — the ceilings and the lamps — now reminds me of God providing for me during a very difficult time. So now there's a story attached to it. Even there, an engineered product can be engaged as a story, as a mystery. Now, is it helpful to do that? Not always. If I need you to fix it — because it broke, the electrical thing, and I needed to rewire it or something — yeah, sure, I could fix it. It's not too good to be rewiring a lamp and being like, "Wow, I'm just caught up in the mystery…"

We could also look at it separately, as if I am not involved in it. I can do the measurements. I can weigh it on a scale. I can look at the materials used to make its legs and the contraption used to extend it when it is opened. I could also engage with it as mystery, as something that is brimming with the invisible.

And just as with this table, every person is potentially a mystery. There is some woman I have never met in France, and she is a mystery. But for me, my mother is a mystery — I was enclosed within her. I was given life through her. I was nourished by her. There's a connection there that involves me more so than some other random person that I have not yet met. Every person is potentially a mystery to me in some way — which is another way of saying that every person is potentially someone to be loved.

The engagement with our world, all creation, as mystery, is preparation for engaging with God — the creator of everything, the love that made the sun and the stars — to engage in God as mystery.

In the ancient world, this was not so uncommon: even grown men would look up at the sky at night, see the stars, draw pictures in the sky, and connect them to their maps of meaning. They'd see this reflected in the cosmos through the myths that they would tell, the stories that they would share with their children and with others in society. Cosmos — they engage with the sky, with the stars, as mystery, as if it involved them.

They were like my niece, Elizabeth, but the moon really does follow them. The stars really do care for us — in the sense that we are all made for relationship, and we all live in relation to everything else.

And when I look at the stars at night, I don't look up there and see constellations — I might be able to identify a few. Orion's belt is always the one that sticks out to me, and I remember that, and I have associations with Orion's belt, because that's the very recognizable one. You can see it mostly even in the city. But when I think of the stars, I know what they are. I know they are balls of plasma, many thousands of light years away — but actually, to me, that moves me more towards wonder. When I know more about what it is, it's even more interesting and amazing. I think astronomy might be one of the areas where you find fewer atheists among scientists, because you can go from the massive scale of everything — the beauty, the order of creation — when you're looking at these images from the James Webb telescope, and you're like, "This is amazing. This is incredible." And it was all hidden from our eyes for so long. It reveals the vastness, the sheer excess of creation, and it moves me towards wonder. So I think we can go from the problem — the measurable, the fact that, yeah, we can actually travel to the moon and land on it, despite what people on YouTube say — and also to wonder. I think it was amazing for those astronauts there. The first time they went around the moon — before they even landed on it — they saw an earthrise as they were orbiting the moon. They were the first human beings ever to see an earthrise. These astronauts looking back on the Earth and seeing it all as one planet moved them towards this idea of this greater unity. And that was a wonder — and a wonder that was transmitted back to the world, like, "Look, this is our blue marble." And that inspired people, and there were secular movements to have Earth Day as a result. Whatever good came out of that, I don't know, but it was at least a movement towards a wonder.

## The Proper Response

When we talk about the Trinity as mystery going forward on this retreat, we need to hold in our minds what we mean by mystery. We do not mean to ever excuse it and say we cannot access it, because that is actually the exact wrong approach to mystery. To approach mystery is to access it, to enter in, to be enveloped by it, to see that we ourselves are included and caught up in a relationship.

This is what the Trinity is. It's not so far away, so transcendent, so separated from us that we have nothing to do with it — that we can analyze it from afar, treat it with theology, apply the history of Christianity to it, and say, "Well, that's Sabellianism, that's modalism, that's pseudo-Pelagianism" — that's probably not applied to the Trinity, but that's okay. Or we can understand that we are already living in it — that the Trinity involves us — that we not only have our origin from God and his love, between Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, spilling out in an excessive, abundant way in creation, and causing us — each of us — as a thought of God, from God — from God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — in God's knowledge and his will — and that is to say, the Son and the Holy Spirit coming forth from him — and made for him. We want to say, "No. Well, somehow we're separate. Somehow everything is determined. Somehow everything is just coming forth from matter, from different material causes — maybe even randomly — in order to produce us, where we are right now." No. None of us is meaningless. None of us is random. Each one of us is willed. Because all of us come forth from the Trinity, we are made for the triune love. We are made to be inserted, through Christ, in the love of God: Father, Son, Holy Spirit. We are implicated in the Holy Trinity.

And how do we practice this? Maybe just a recognizing — just seeing that there is mystery here, that in some way the love of God is spilling out here. We hear the voice of the Father: "This is my beloved Son" — and it applies just as much to us, as other Christs, as Christians, as it does to the second person of the Holy Trinity. We are given that same Spirit through which we cry out, "Abba, Father." We are joined at the level of our beings to this love. So we cannot dismiss this: this is absolutely central to our faith, absolutely central to our prayer, central to our very selves.

And that Trinity that made the sun and the stars — the moon in the sky that follows us as we traverse long distances — that Trinity is within us. For God has come to dwell in our hearts, and he is within. We have been baptized into Christ, and we've been given this Holy Trinity as an indwelling within us — to be at home in Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — even as we ache to be in that home of the Father's house in heaven.

And the proper Christian response to mystery is to love, adore, and worship.


# Chapter 3 — Gift and Liturgy

*As a deer longs for flowing streams, so longs my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and behold the face of God? My tears have been my food day and night, while men say to me continually, "Where is your God?" These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I went with the throng, and led them in procession to the house of God, with glad shouts and songs of thanksgiving, a multitude keeping festival. Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you disquieted within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my Savior and my God. My soul is cast down within me, therefore I remember you from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar. Deep calls to deep at the thunder of your cataracts; all your waves and your billows have gone over me. Amen.*

— Psalm 42:1–7

*Come, Holy Spirit, fill our hearts. Help us know the love of God, the Father and the Son. May we be held close to you, with the prayers of the Blessed Virgin Mary and all the saints. May we be loyal and faithful children of the Church, animated by your life, and living always conscious of the gift that we are of God — Father, Son, Holy Spirit — and the gift we are meant to be to God. Amen.*

— Come, Holy Spirit

Thirst opens this chapter, and thirst will carry it to its end: deep calling to deep.

St. Paul, in the twelfth chapter of his letter to the Romans, says, "Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind." I propose that at least one way — and I won't say the only way — to be renewed by the transformation of your mind is to see mystery, to encounter realities as mysteries, to see the meaning behind that which presents itself to the senses, that which can be measured, to go from beyond the merely problematical to the actual mystery at hand — and not to be deterred by the enormity of the mysteries, but instead to enter in, to recognize that we are implicated in them. And that is a good thing, because we ourselves are good. This is part of what we call sacramentality.

## The Trinity of love that overflows

Understanding God as mystery — that he is one God, one divine nature, and three divine persons — we see that everything that God does is mysterious. There is a way in which we are implicated in it. It is well beyond our ability to understand. It overwhelms us, and we are enveloped in it. We can think of creation itself as mysterious, approaching it as a mystery, for we ourselves are created. The uncreated love created love out of his creativity, as one of the retreatants put it, and according to Joseph Ratzinger. God's love spilled out. He was infinitely perfect within himself. There was no need, in one sense, to create. For God was love within three persons — a communion of love, not some lonely figure off in another world unto himself, but fully loving. God pouring himself out, Father to Son, Son to Father, that love being the Holy Spirit. This Trinity is a Trinity of love. And that love made the sun and the stars. And that love made us.

Can I try to explain this? There is an artist of the twentieth century named M.C. Escher, and he has this depiction — well, I'll go first with the staircase. It's a really mind-bending staircase, where you can go up three levels, but it's like the dimensions are switched around. It's confusing. There's a simpler version of this, but I like this one because it was three of them. You're going up the stairs, you're up the stairs, you're off the stairs — you're always going up, in this case just dimensionally. It's challenging, and it pulls the viewer in in a weird way. On top of that, he has the M.C. Escher waterfall, with water that seems to be flowing back and forth, going up and falling back down, defying the laws of physics. No pumps are used in this kind of waterfall. You cannot make this waterfall without pumps. But this is the idea of this water constantly flowing, going up — though we do not know how — and then falling right back down.

I wanted to try to capture that with the Trinity. I remember how Father Mike explained the Trinity — he would try to draw a picture of it — as three buckets. One bucket would pour out into the other bucket, which would be pouring out into the other bucket, which is pouring out into the other bucket. So I was like, I'll try to capture that. So I made a picture. This is my attempt at — not the M.C. Escher waterfall, but the PJ Gruber Trinitarian waterfall. It's imperfect, because it seems to be too directional, but the idea is that the water flows and falls into the pool, and flows and falls into the other pool — there's a constant pouring. Now, does this image — does the Trinity do this well? I would probably do it differently.

I kind of like to do this, too. If you put your fingers together in front of you, it looks like there's a third finger that appears between them. That's not a great analogy, but I like it — how the Holy Spirit comes between [unclear] and the Father. That love is personal. This is sufficient from all eternity.

Creation is a going forth of the love of God — God making something that is not God, not so that we might be separated from God, but so that we might return to him. In Latin, there's a phrase — two words: *exitus, reditus*. *Exitus* sounds like exit — for often that is how Latin works — and then *reditus*. There is a going out, an exiting, and a return. God creates that which is not God in order that everything he creates might return to him in the way that is fitting for that creation.

## Made to respond

God made creation so that creation could respond. He threw across the sky distant galaxies and stars and nebula, and he created one planet within one solar system that was able to sustain life. And in that one planet, in that one particular place, he created male and female after his own image and likeness. God is relational in himself, and when he created us male and female, he made us relationally, made for relationship — which is to give of ourselves, not just in a way of interacting with the environment and with each other in bits and pieces, but to give of ourselves in our totality. He made us personal.

The three divine persons are, within the Trinity, subsistent relations. We are also, in a similar way but different, individual substances of a rational, relational nature — in a way different from God, but also in a way imaging him. We show forth the Trinity. Every time we make the sign of the cross over ourselves, we recognize that we ourselves are in the image and likeness of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. And in that way, we are made to respond.

Creation is the gift of God. It is a going out. And then all creation responds through us. The response of all creation through humanity, and in particular through Christ, who is true God and true man — we call that response liturgy. This is a broad, expansive concept of liturgy. Typically, when we hear liturgy, we think perhaps of the rubrics, the roles [possibly: rules], about how we are to celebrate Mass and pray the liturgy of the hours. But liturgy, more broadly speaking, is our response to God.

## "Mine"

He was looking for humanity to respond. He wanted Adam and Eve to respond, for they were made capable of that, in God's image and likeness. Adam and Eve turned in on themselves. They grasped after what was going to be given to them as a gift in due time. They made it their possession. Eve reached out, grasped that fruit. She said, "Mine." When she said "Mine," she cut herself off from communion with God and with Adam. And Adam shared in that fall. Thus cut off, humanity waited to be united. All the liturgies of the Old Testament, all the rituals and sacrifices, all the prayers and the psalms, everything pointed to a time when that would be satisfied, when we could make that full response. And it wasn't until grace entered into creation again, preveniently, in the soul of the Blessed Virgin Mary at the moment of her conception, that God could find within humanity a way to respond.

## Mary's yes

God created. He used the passive tense. "Let us create," he says. "Let there be light." *Fiat lux*, he creates. And there was light. "Let there be dry land." And there it was, dry land. And God creates one [possibly: no one] in creation to give some kind of echo of response. And it wasn't until the Blessed Virgin Mary, as a young woman, responded to God's will: "Let it be done to me according to thy word." God said — Mary said — and the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. God, who said, "Let there be light," finally got a response from a creature. *Fiat lux, fiat terra, fiat mihi secundum verbum tuum.* Mary's response — that is the liturgical response.

And when Jesus teaches us how to pray, he says, "When you pray, pray this way: Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come, thy will be done — *fiat voluntas tua* — thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven." When we pray that "Our Father," recognizing we pray that with confidence, within and through Christ, who is our insertion into the Holy Trinity, we are echoing God's creation. We are giving that full response in our totality back to God.

We have access to God the Father because Christ has given us that access through his Sacred Heart. Before that, in the rites of the ancient Jews, there was a veil that no one could enter beyond, a veil that stopped anyone from seeing into the Holy of Holies. Only the high priest could go once a year, and he would say the name of God there. But we have access, because we have Christ, and his Heart was that veil that was pierced for us.

Liturgy, as one author said, Aidan Kavanagh — he said, "The liturgy is doing the world the way the world was meant to be done." We have all these things that are not God, but they show something of God. They are teeming with his life, with his existence. And the whole world aches to give this response. Liturgy is this response. Liturgy is, in effect, a response to a question that we ourselves could have. I might say, why this chair? For sitting. Why food? For eating. Why something rather than nothing? Liturgy.

When we come together and we pray in union with Christ, as members of his mystical body, his bride, the Church, we are making that response. Our response is not lessened because we are part of a whole. We ourselves are wholes within it: we make this full response, for we are as the Church, collectively and individually. We are a spouse to Christ, and we make this prayer to God our Father. We are given the Holy Spirit, who teaches us how to pray, "Abba, Father." Liturgy is how we live now as Catholics.

All liturgy is received. Think about what distinguishes liturgy from — even from — our private prayers. When I pray, just talking to God as a friend, which is a very good thing to do, that is not liturgical. Private prayer is distinct from that. A liturgy is something we receive. There are guidelines, there are words we use, there are instructions that we follow. So it's received, and in that sense we are obedient there, in that reception. And so then — it is a gift. Liturgy is itself a gift.

Why is it necessary for a priest to pray the Hours, but not always to celebrate Holy Mass? The Hours are required — and that's part of the reason — it's the Church's discipline. The Church requires every priest to pray the Liturgy of the Hours every day. I don't know why. I just know that's the practice right now. Priests are encouraged to say Mass every day, very much so, but they're not violating a promise they made at their ordination when they don't. Also, a fun fact is that you make these promises when you're a deacon — and when you're a deacon, you can't make Mass happen, and it's really hard to get to Mass every day. And so in the past, it wouldn't have been the case that priests would say Mass every day, but it's become more common in the past five hundred years. I could just say one Mass once a week and still be good. I just wouldn't do that. That'd be weird. The practice of concelebration is more recent — for us in the West, only as recent as the past sixty years. So if I go to the old form, the traditional Latin Mass — if it's a solemn high Mass, there'd be a deacon or subdeacon, but I wouldn't be concelebrating. Only the main celebrant is doing that.

Liturgy engages all the different parts of us — the physicality of our human bodies, being present physically. We even moved away from having TV Masses. COVID was a terrible time for that, because it told everyone, "You don't actually need to be present." It's like — no, you do. It really matters, because it is liturgical. Liturgy is bodily. It means being physically present, and it means, as best as possible, being mentally present. I don't think people need to accuse themselves more than they already do about being distracted at Mass, because your mind will not be able to be focused the entire time. It's not about being focused. It's about being present — and being present and being focused are different things. So don't worry if you're not able to remain mentally focused the whole time. That's not the idea. Our minds do wander. You'd be surprised about what occurs in my mind while I'm praying the Eucharistic Prayer. It just — it doesn't matter, in some ways. I'm present. I'm not necessarily focused. Because sometimes focus is to treat it as a problem, not as the mystery itself. To be present, to respond — the Church emphasizes active participation, which is a way to say actual participation, like your heart's being joined to what's going on. Being intentional — that's all part of our response.

Receiving Holy Communion, in some ways, is not even the big point of going to Mass. The point of going to Mass is the praise and glory of God the Father — which means that if you ate a sandwich right before Mass, it's still good for you to be at Mass, even if you don't receive, because the point of Mass is to respond. And if we can receive Holy Communion, that's great, but that's not the point of why we go to church.

The liturgies of the Church — the rites of the Church that regard the sacraments — sometimes those are done within the Mass but are distinct rites; those are liturgical acts. There's a liturgy for Benediction, for example. That's a liturgical act that has roles that we follow and prayers that we pray, although there's some flexibility. I don't make up the words of the Eucharistic Prayer, for example. That would be wrong. The Church says, no, you have to use these words. You have options, but you can [possibly: only] use these words. But other prayers could be influenced by Scripture, which is also received — and Scripture is meant to be proclaimed liturgically. Not just read devotionally — Scripture is best proclaimed liturgically. The Liturgy of the Word is actually a prayer to God the Father first and foremost. Secondarily, it is educational for us. We hear what's going on in Scripture, but it's primarily a prayer.

## Baptism: inserted into the Trinity

Understanding this liturgy, we have within the Church's liturgy the seven sacraments, the first of which is baptism. Baptism is the sacrament — the mystery itself — by which we are incorporated into Christ, in which we are made beloved sons through Christ, the eternal Son of the Father. He who was adopted into our human nature — we receive adoption as sons through him. We are then truly sons, more by grace than we are sons of our biological fathers by nature. We are inserted into the Trinity. God's life is poured over us. When that water hits our head three times, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, what is enacted through material means is done immaterially, and grace is given.

We are washed clean, original sin being wiped away. We are restored, and we are elevated. Made in the image and likeness of God, but that image marred by original sin, it is now made perfect, and ready for that perfection which Christ invites us to. Baptism is our entrance into divine life. We use the word "grace" — in this case, sanctifying grace — to mean the life of the Blessed Trinity within us. And baptism opens us up to the other sacraments as well: confirmation — we are sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit — and the Holy Eucharist — we are fed with the bread of eternal life. We have the sacraments of vocation — of marriage, of priesthood — and the sacraments of healing — of confession, and anointing of the sick. In all of these sacraments, there is involved a pouring out of God, usually through material means of some kind.

The devotion of our lives — you could say that the living of a daily prayer life is an extension of our baptism, which is itself liturgy, where we're immersed and poured over with water. The deep calls unto deep there. And that is an extension of that liturgy, though in itself it's not a distinct liturgical act, just having private prayer with our Lord. But liturgies could be used in a very broad sense.

## Everything is positive

The sacramental life is how we live as Catholics. But we also have this idea of maybe more broadly sacramentality: not just the seven sacraments, but recognizing that all visible things point to the invisible, all material creation comes with the immaterial — it's a package of something beyond us. There's a mystery to behold — and every leaf that falls from a tree, every blade of grass, every hair on our heads — there is mystery, and there is gift.

Luigi Giussani, one of the great religious thinkers and reformers of the 20th century — everything is positive, to use Giussani's phrase. Everything is positive. Everything is good. And if it does not exist, it is not good. Sin is nothingness. Christ said, "Without me you can do nothing." And when we sin, we do nothing. But everything — everything that shares in God — shares in his goodness. There's nothing that is purely evil. Not even Satan himself is pure evil, for even by his existence, he shares something in God. Everything has a goodness to it, even sin. Sin is our attempt to grasp after something that is good — but not good for us right now — and instead to insert some nothingness into it. But even there, there is goodness. As one twentieth-century author put it — Bruce Marshall — "the young man who rings the bell at the brothel is unconsciously looking for God." There's always goodness, and there's no reason to ever be discouraged. There's no reason to ever despair. And everything of our own lives, everything we encounter, is teeming with that life, is teeming with that mystery, is teeming with that gift underneath it.

St. Thérèse said at the end of her life, before she was about to expire from tuberculosis — a very painful, terrible disease — that everything is grace. God's life is in everything. It is brimming with mystery. For this reason, St. Augustine could see the Trinity in everything, or St. Elizabeth of the Trinity could not help but talk about Trinity in everything she would see — for the relations between objects and persons, between person and person, everything communicated something of the Trinity. In the next talk, we will talk about how relationships image the Trinity. But everything is gift. St. Elizabeth of the Trinity — how can you not contemplate the Trinity? How can you not see it between the fruitfulness of a friendship or the delight of someone seeing you and asking, "Hey, how have you been doing since that thing happened?" Honestly, there's a Trinity right there, spilling over into my life. That's mysterious, in the true Christian mystery sense.

Is a book a mystery? Take the *Introduction to Christianity* by Joseph Ratzinger. It's overwhelming you. You don't know it yet. Is the number two a mystery? Is a number a mystery? Yes. And so it seems like if we say yes, we're simply applying mystery to anything — and we can recognize that everything could be engaged as mystery, and everything could be engaged as a problem.

This is a fundamentally different way to view all reality. This is a fundamental Christian imaginative vision. How we see the world is utterly different. We work with totalities. We work with wholes. We work with the mystery coming through, shining through the veil of the material world.

## The enchanted world and the law of the gift

We are living in an enchanted world. Not that it's magic in the sense of the occult, but it is magical and enchanted, because the material world is brimming with the invisible. We need to recover this. We need to have the sacramental vision by which we see all things. And when we do that, we will want to respond.

We are surrounded by nature, and I hope you will go outside this afternoon and see that behind every rock there is a life — a whole sort of microcosm of existence. This is a fun time of the year to overturn rocks and find the salamanders that are hiding underneath. They are different colors. Some are orange, some are like bluish. Some are debatably some shade of color that only some can figure out. But I encourage you to do that, because you see, there's so much more going on behind the surface, and the beauty is that the invisible comes through the visible. This is called, more broadly, sacramentality. The invisible comes through the visible, the immaterial through the material.

Recall John Paul II's Theology of the Body. He speaks often of the law of the gift, as it is commonly called. In this world, when we give something away, a material thing, I no longer have this. I give this folder over. It's no longer mine. I give it to this person here. Gift is a mystery. You know your intentions behind a gift, but you don't understand how the receiver is also receiving. These two things are joined together. It's too much to go into [unclear], but it's already including us. Even when we're not going to be able to get ourselves around it fully — we're not meant to get ourselves around it. But in the world of grace, when I give, I do not lose. In fact, I gain. Giving over something, we gain. Our response to all of God's creation, all of God's gifts, every one of God's blessings, is that of thanksgiving and praise.

I've come to think more and more that one of the solutions to a lot of the problems we have in this world is thanksgiving — to recognize the blessings God has poured out for us and to give that response — even that liturgical response, the Thanksgiving, the Eucharist, of our holy sacrifice of the Mass. Thanksgiving is a solution because it recognizes everything as the gift that it actually is. It's not just a way we delude ourselves as Christians, but everything truly is gift, everything truly is positive.

Everything that is, is good, and therefore God is present in everything. And everything being good, that means everything is gift — even a late arrival at a retreat. There is gift in that. To live in gift — this is a distillation of what it means to live in the Spirit. The gift of God Most High poured out for us — the love between Father and Son proceeding forth from them both, coming down to us, living among us, living within us. To live in the Spirit is to live in that gift and to make a gift of ourselves back to God.

## Two kinds of feedback

And there's something that happens when we give ourselves back, because of the law of the gift. There's something that John Paul II understood, something that John of the Cross certainly understood, and Louis de Montfort understood, in his devotion to Mary. When we give everything to Jesus through Mary, we don't lose anything of ourselves, but we gain. And we give totalities. We don't give just pieces. So it's a total consecration of ourselves. When we give so fully of ourselves, we gain of ourselves. And we give again. Now we have more to give, and then we gain more. What this is called in the world of engineering and signal processing — this is called a positive feedback loop.

There are two kinds of feedback loops. One is called a negative feedback loop. This is the safe kind. This is what — what's it called in a car, when you want to go the same speed and you put on cruise control? Cruise control is a negative feedback loop. If you start going faster than your speed, the car will slow down. If you start going slower, the car speeds up. If there's a negative change in speed, it reverses it, to get back to that speed of 70 miles per hour. It establishes equilibrium and balance.

Much of our lives, we think about having balance and equilibrium, and there's a usefulness to it. Negative feedback loops are quite good for establishing this at a certain level, and it's safe. But a positive feedback loop does not reverse the change when it is fed back into the system. And so we might think of this in a car. If the car started going faster down a hill, and if it was a positive feedback loop, it would actually rev the engine more to make the car go faster. And because the car is going even faster, it would make it go even faster than that, and eventually the car is going 180 miles per hour and it can't be controlled. That's dangerous.

Explosions — chemical explosions. You need heat in order for there to be a chemical reaction. There's a small chemical reaction, and it makes more heat, which causes more chemical reactions, and eventually this results in an explosion. Chain reactions in a nuclear bomb result in a massive explosion. This is a positive feedback loop. This is disastrous, you think, in so many different contexts. And when you have, for example, speakers and a microphone — many of us have had the experience of someone with a microphone walking in front of a speaker — the speaker projects the sound, and it gets picked up by the microphone. The microphone takes that sound and puts it in the speaker and amplifies it, and you get this loud screech. That's a positive feedback loop. It's totally undesirable in so many different contexts. In engineering, a positive feedback loop is destructive. But the opposite is true in our relationship with God.

## The positive feedback loop of grace

God gives of himself totally to us in creation and in redemption, and we respond totally with the gift of ourselves in baptism, in the life of the baptized, in the sacraments, in our devotions, our prayers — even the praying of a rosary or stations of the cross. We give ourselves back to God, and his mysteries, the mysteries of the rosary, and we gain, and God gives, and we give back, and we gain, and we gain — and this is exponential. This is the positive feedback loop of grace. This is our liturgical life to its completion. And then we end up living within the Trinity.

In the prayer of the liturgy, we make the response of the whole Church together, in unity with Christ, to the Father. We pray the prayer of the Mass. Who is the Mass directed to? The Father. That's right. We think of it as — the whole Mass — the Mass is a prayer. Who is the prayer directed to? Oh, Jesus. Everything's about Jesus. No. It's the prayer of Jesus to the Father, that we are being included in, that we are being offered now to God the Father. We make that response in the unity of the Holy Spirit — through him, with him, and in him. O God, almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, all glory and honor is yours, forever and ever. We're joining in it — an eternal gift back. We are joining in with the Son's gift back to God the Father. His thanksgiving — his Eucharist — for coming forth from God the Father from eternity, he gives himself back. That itself, within the blessed Trinity, is the Eucharist. And we participate in that same prayer. What Christ did on the cross, when he went and died and gave himself so totally to the Father — [unclear] — he has done from the heart of the blessed Trinity for all eternity, and we participate in that. In the Mass, this is the highest gift. This is the source and summit of our faith. And we receive from this very Mass — every time we go, we can receive Jesus in the Holy Eucharist. And this is the source and the summit: everything of our lives goes towards that Eucharist, and everything flows from it.

## Deep calls to deep

We are made for this infinity. We who are infinite abysses of existence cry out, and we pour ourselves out. In Greek, the word is kenosis — to pour out. St. Thérèse says, prayer [possibly: love] is to be poured out into another. Going back to the words of Psalm 42, deep calls unto deep. From the abyss of our hearts, we call out to the abyss of the Holy Trinity — an abyss calling upon an abyss, a communication going between two, between an I and a thou. All your torrents and all your waves washed over me. This is utterly catastrophic in this world. To be under a waterfall would result in a lot of death, especially in torrential waterfalls. We would drown. To be caught up in the waters of chaos and death — typically a deadly activity — is something an ancient Jew would have had a fear of. They were aquaphobic. They did not like water. The fisherman's occupation was a rare occupation among the Jews, because the water was a dangerous place to be: you could easily capsize, and a storm could catch you off guard. It was harder to survive. You can see Galilee — there were storms that would come in unexpectedly. And so this fear of water was present from the beginning — this chaos that order needed to be instilled upon, this water. But in our prayer, deep calls unto deep. This happens within the heart of the Trinity, and this happens with us. We find our meaning in receiving from the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and we call upon him, and he calls upon us. There is a communication, there is a communion between the two. In the life of the Trinity, we are meant to be washed over, continually swept up — God's pouring out of himself and his triune grace. And we say, let this happen. Let deep call out unto deep. Let me make this prayer of hope.

To hope is to affirm being, says Gabriel Marcel. To despair is to negate being, to give up on all existence. But to hope in God — this is what we are being called into, in the life of the Trinity that we live. We who are other Christs, we who are Christians, are inserted into that holy mystery of the sacred Trinity. We make that full response of ourselves. It is unceasing. It enters into the way we live our lives, even in our work — to use the world well, so we might make that response of the world to God.

This is pointing us towards that heavenly liturgy — the rivers of water that we would be partaking of, the waters of love that wash over us, the torrents and the waves that overwhelm us. This is what's happening in the holy liturgy. We're part of something so much greater — not just the mere sacramentality of the visible and the invisible world being entwined, but the love of God poured out into us through the unity of the Holy Spirit, through and in God the Son, to God the Father. And this is what gives purpose to our lives. This is why we are created. It's all to participate in the Trinity. The Rublev icon — when you look at it, it forms that chalice, and Jesus is there in the center. We, who participate in the bread of life that is Jesus' body, blood, soul, and divinity — that is our participation in the Trinity. This is what we receive. This is why this matters so much, and why you can't possibly exhaust it in one 18-minute retreat talk. There's so much going on here, and everything from our life now has meaning because of this. We who receive Jesus in the Eucharist now know that we are loved, that we are known, that we have a purpose, that we are joined to something greater. And we who struggle to find a meaning within suffering, we who have attempted to despair and have many times despaired and repented — now we can affirm Being, capital B. We can affirm the blessed Trinity, in which we are participating here and now. This should overflow for us.

In his house, there are many rooms. There is welcome for the sinner. There's a place that is set aside for us. We return to the Father — like the prodigal son — joined with Christ in the Paschal mystery, where he who has come forth from the Father has done his own pouring out to enter into creation, and to take on our sins, our sufferings — to die, to enter into hell [unclear], to rise from the dead, and to say, "I will rise and go to my father." He goes — like the prodigal son, but never having sinned — goes to God the Father. He joins us with himself, and we make that full return through Christ and his passion, death, resurrection, and ascension. And we are called into that heavenly liturgy — the worship of God in heaven — which we participate in now by following God's will for us, to enter into that most sacred celebration here and now.

## Give thanks

This is how we live the Trinitarian life in all aspects — not segmenting it off into some part that we call our faith life. We live our life — our life is inherently Trinitarian, and our life is Trinitarian through the shape of the cross that is traced over us again and again. Living this mystery means living utterly differently, and it needs to be expressed. This is the summit — everything that flows down now seeks out that lowest place. God's mercy finds that lowest place within us and in our fallen world. To live this way is to live entirely transformed — but it's not going to involve a depletion of our resources, for we are not living in the world of the problematic but the world of mystery. We have been made for mystery. We are not robots. We are not competitors with artificial intelligence. We are meant for love. And our whole existence, our whole lives, is for love. This we carry out in liturgy, pouring ourselves out. This is our return to God the Father. And what I recommend to do — this is to live this life of thanksgiving, to be thankful before God at all times, in all places. Saint Paul says, "Give thanks."

> O my God, Trinity whom I adore, help me forget myself entirely so to establish myself in you, unmovable and peaceful as if my soul were already in eternity. May nothing be able to trouble my peace or make me leave you, O my unchanging God, but may each minute bring me more deeply into your mystery! Grant my soul peace. Make it your heaven, your beloved dwelling and the place of your rest. May I never abandon you there, but may I be there, whole and entire, completely vigilant in my faith, entirely adoring, and wholly given over to your creative action.

## Two Kinds of Knowledge

There are two kinds of knowledge. The first knowledge I think we are very familiar with. I alluded to it in the earlier conferences, talking about how we deal with problems. It's the knowledge that comes to us through dissecting things that we see, things that we measure, things that we understand. All the different kinds of ologies — biology, philology, geology — all of these kinds of sciences work through understanding the parts and dissecting them, getting down deep into them, throwing our minds into these areas and absorbing as much as we can, and then putting them back together to form a cohesive whole. And this is how we go about much of our life. It is the realm of the technical, the problematical, and we dissect, we divide, we disjoin. Much of our knowledge comes this way.

St. Thomas Aquinas would quote Aristotle, who said, "Knowledge begins with the senses" — things we can touch and feel. And from that we derive things we can measure, things we experience on the superficial level, as if they were apart from ourselves, as if we were not implicated in them.

We know things by a likeness. If I see this carpet here, it produces a likeness on me. The more I study that carpet, the more I place that knowledge in me. I understand how to make this carpet, how to deal with the different patterns, how the different production facilities handle the making of a carpet, and I understand it inside and out — I get it. And my knowledge of it is built up from what I study. And so that knowledge is based on observation and on study — on making conclusions from it, best practices when it comes to what kind of thread to use and what cleaning processes make most sense. I can build up a knowledge of a carpet. That's how most knowledge works. But I could know something in a deeper way. Carpet's not the best example, where I become like the thing. This happens in relationships all the time. We might not be able to tell you every single thing about the person we love, but we can know the person. And this — the knowledge that works more with relationships — is to know the person as a whole: that infinite abyss of existence reaching out to your infinite abyss of existence, the deep calling unto deep. This is the personal knowledge, the whole knowledge.

The other kind of knowledge is the one that most accords with mystery. It is to treat things not as pieces, pieces of a puzzle, something to put together, but as a whole, in its totality. This is the kind of knowledge that we have as Christians, given to us — the mysteries of our faith, the mysteries of Christ, the events of his life, his death, his resurrection, the mysteries of the rosary, the mysteries of the teachings of the faith. Everything in theology that we understand can be engaged with as mystery, because we are recipients of it. We are participants in our faith. So we already engage with this on some level.

## Knowing from the Inside

Those that write and read poetry might be aware that there is a unitive awareness in the poetic imagination — the way in which a poet views the world and approaches things not as pieces, but as wholes. And the same might be true with the mystic. The mystic is thrown into the depths of the knowledge of God, but not in a knowledge that we can typically relate to. And this is a knowledge involving the totality of their being with the totality of God's being under some aspect.

Someone who goes through a mystical experience, I'm told, is not able to communicate what happened in that experience. St. Paul sort of sheepishly talks about how he knew a man who was lifted up into the ninth [possibly: third] heaven, whatever, some level of heaven. And he said, I don't know which — I don't know. And he can't really tell much about it, because it's hard to tell. What you know in that way can't be easily explained.

This kind of knowledge St. Thomas Aquinas calls connatural knowledge. The example he uses to explain how this knowledge works is that of the virtue of chastity. You can understand chastity by observing it and dividing it into its separate parts, understanding how the intellect and the will work together with the passions or against the passions in order to experience and to give and to receive chaste love in the appropriate context. We can study theology of the body. We can understand these things in all their various ways, and we can understand this virtue well. But someone who lives chastity starts to become like it through their living of it. And they might not decipher it and divide it down into its different parts, but someone who understands chastity from the inside has a knowledge that exceeds that of those that study from the outside.

This is called connatural knowledge by St. Thomas Aquinas, because it is knowledge that has become second nature. Virtues become part of us. All knowledge is through a likeness. If I know something — when I divide it into parts and understand the different concepts that go into a field of study — those concepts remain in me. There's a likeness imprinted into my intellect. But when we become like something, we bypass the knowledge that comes to us through concepts. And instead, we have that likeness imprinted directly on us, on our person. And that allows us to know and understand on a level wholly different. And it's to know and understand with our totality something in its totality.

## Marked with the Cross

Mystery is best engaged with this second kind of knowledge. We are imprinted with the Holy Trinity from our baptisms. We are told we are given a mark. Our baptism marks us. The shape of that mark, of that seal that's placed in our souls, is cruciform. And we reenact that mark every time we make that sign of the cross. It is imprinted on us. It is meant to become so deep that the Trinity is really that by which we understand everything else.

St. Paul says in his letter to the Romans, "Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind." The way in which our minds are renewed is how we come into a unitive awareness, a contemplation of — and most especially — relationship.

## Christ Between Us

Christ says, "Where two or three are gathered in my name, there I am in the midst of them." Christ comes in the midst of us. He comes between us — not to separate, for we often use the phrase "between us" as something that shows an obstacle, but to actually connect us.

It really wasn't until Christ that we became, again — for the first time since Adam and Eve — capable of real communion. For we have a deep communion in Christ — with Christ, but also with each other. Recall the consequences of the fall, how Adam and Eve were disjointed by their original sin. So Christ comes between us.

Are we then cut off entirely, irreparably? When we are about to surrender our weakness to God, I'm like, "Lord, I messed this up. Can you do something about it?" And then I let it go. And then, if you reflect on that particular thing that you did — which you are foolish and shameful about — and then maybe a couple of years later, you look back and you're like, "Oh, wow — the Lord took care of that relationship, or the Lord took care of that situation." I didn't need to say that the world has ended just with that. God's mercy transforms. And even in that moment, you can see God loving us — that he's not far from us, even when we want to be far from him. Sin might be that attempt: "I'm going to take matters into my own hands and do the thing that I know I ought not to do." But even there, we are caught up in the mystery of God's mercy. It still requires us to choose to receive that mercy, but we go back and we can revise — we can see that God's goodness is there. His fidelity is unchanging, even though ours is not. How we cooperate is through taking up our own crosses — the cross of repentance, the cross of healing, the cross of suffering, whatever it might be. We are to participate — to be cooperators in his work. That's the great thing about the fifth station: Simon of Cyrene helps Jesus carry the cross. St. John Henry Newman says that Christ could have carried the cross by himself — he could have chosen to — but he allowed us to have a share in that work.

## I and Thou

Martin Buber was a Jewish philosopher of the 20th century. His great work is called *I and Thou*. It was written in German, and using thou — which is, you know, the older form of you — was emphasized in the English translation to stress not just reverence towards the other — we're really talking about a singular person. When you say "I and you," you could be plural. He wants to say it between a first person singular pronoun and a second person singular pronoun. I and thou.

## The Space of the Holy Spirit

In a relationship, there is an I, a thou, a you, and there is a between. There is something that is shared — there is the total gift under the aspect, in a lot of cases, of friendship or other relationships. And in that between — this is the space of the Holy Spirit, He who is between the Father and the Son. And the charity that is in God comes down to us in the theological virtue of charity. We are meant to become more and more loving, to know more and more what love is from the inside out, and then to love others with that same love. This is expressed in friendship, in any kind of relationship.

I recall Pope Benedict's inaugural homily when he was made Pope in 2005. He said, "It is really so: the purpose of our lives is to reveal God to men. And only where God is seen does life truly begin. Only when we meet the living God in Christ do we know what life is. We are not some casual and meaningless product of evolution. Each of us is the result of a thought of God. Each of us is willed, each of us is loved, each of us is necessary. There is nothing more beautiful than to be surprised by the Gospel, by the encounter with Christ. There is nothing more beautiful than to know Him and to speak to others of our friendship with Him." We are able to really engage in friendship because God has loved us first. He has poured himself out for us when we were not deserving. It is pure gift. And because God has poured himself out, we are able to love each other.

## Every Relationship Says Something

All relationships have something that they say about the Trinity. In some way they express that primordial love — that love that was there before the sun and the stars and before we came into being, a love shared constantly, acting out fully between Father, Son, and Holy Spirit from all eternity. That love is the same love with which we now love each other, under different aspects and under different relationships.

We are meant to approach, then, the Trinity under and through these relationships that we have. Motherhood, fatherhood, sonship, even co-workers and others — all these things now have something in common with God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. I think there are more ways I'm included in Mary, just in the same way that there are more ways I'm included in my mother — my biological mother — than in some woman I have never met in France. That woman in France is a mystery. But for me, my mom is a mystery — I was enclosed within her. I was given life through her. I was nourished by her. There's a connection there that involves me more than some other random person that I have not yet met. Every person is potentially a mystery to me in some way — which is another way of saying another person is potentially someone to be loved. St. Augustine was not wrong to see the Trinity reflected in all aspects of reality, but most especially in us, who are individually and collectively images of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

## Encounter, Not Merely Understanding

In this way, we do not merely understand the Trinity. We don't just reduce it to a notion. We don't just divide it up. We do divide it up — under the aspect of theology. We do have notions of the Trinity. We understand it. We can talk about the theological underpinnings of it, and you can read the Catechism, which actually does a great job of giving an overview of a very dense topic. We're not meant to merely understand the Trinity.

Instead, we are to encounter from the depths of our being an infinite abyss of our existence.

There is a great quote — I think I'll just end with this. There are so many things that could be said about this, but it's from my uncle, who wrote a gospel reflection on Trinity Sunday. Marriage enshrines the Trinity. Motherhood expresses it. Fatherhood mirrors it. Friendship touches it. And loneliness, when it does not despair, adores it.

So maybe the way to take this with you is not to ask what the Trinity means, but to ask which of these relationships God is asking you to see as Trinitarian this week — your marriage, your motherhood, your fatherhood, a friendship, or a loneliness.

## Being itself

In theology, there's a central concept in talking about God. It's called the analogy of being. There are many who have ideas of God as a being above other beings. Many Protestants would see God as the greatest of all beings, and because he is the greatest — in fact, he's infinite, he's omnibenevolent, he's omniscient, he's omnipotent — he's all-powerful, he's all-knowing, he's all-loving — and therefore he, as the highest of all beings, gets all of our praise and worship.

I think for many of us, this might be our default conception of how God works. God is just simply the highest of all beings. We are lesser beings. We are creatures made by God, but God just stands above and beyond all these things. And to that, we can add his eternality. He's, you know, before time existed, he is, and that he always will be. There's no change in God. We can add all the characteristics to this great, really, really, really great being.

And that's God.

And, actually, there have been Protestant theologians that have argued more or less for that conception of God.

We might be thinking, that's not that wrong, right? That's a fairly normal conception of God. God is just really great.

But that is not our Catholic conception of God. God is not the highest of all beings. God is Being, with a capital B. We are beings with a lowercase b. We participate in him, and he is being. We just participate in him. We have being. He is being.

We recall the story of Moses with the burning bush. Next week you'll hear it as your first reading at Mass, where Moses, wandering in the desert, tending the flock of his father-in-law, Jethro — he's away from Egypt for a while, and probably not even practicing anything of his Israelite religion — comes up to Mount Sinai, and he sees there's this bush burning, but not being consumed. He goes to the bush, and the bush calls out to him, asking Moses to ask Pharaoh to let his people go. And Moses is not sure about it, but the burning bush — God, from that burning bush, speaks — and Moses is going to listen. But before he leaves, he says, "I need to know your name. So when others ask, 'Who sent you?' I can tell them who it was that sent me." So Moses asks the burning bush — God himself — what is his name. And God, from the burning bush, says, "I am who am." Maybe this is God just saying, "I am who I am. Don't worry about it. Just say, 'I am' sent you." Perhaps that was the original context, the original meaning. But "I am who am" — we can read back into this and say, God is saying, "I am existence itself. I am being itself." This is Exodus 3:14. Philosophers will say this means that God's essence is his existence. That's not necessarily what is being communicated to Moses — but it fits, because he does not have, he is. And from that we get the name of God. It was "I am."

Our Lord reiterates this. He has many "I am" statements, especially within the Gospel of John. "I am the gate. I am the vine. I am the way, the truth, and the life." It was on purpose that our Lord said "I am," because this harkens back to the name of God.

What does it mean for God to be existence? This means that God does not have anything. While we say he has, he is. To have means you might have not. Maybe implies there's something apart from you that is not somehow connected to you. But God does not have, he is. God does not have being. He is being. God does not have love. He is love. God does not have goodness. He is goodness. There's no potential in God. There's no having something and not having it. Whatever he has, he is.

Well, this fundamentally changes how we look at God, then, how we look at ourselves. We have goodness. God has given it to us. But we are not goodness itself. We have truth. We can actually ascertain truth with our intellects. We are made for truth. But we are not truth itself. God is.

We, as creatures of God, we participate in his being. I mentioned before that everything is gift, everything is positive, everything speaks of God. All things share in his existence, because all things have existence, and their existence participates in God's existence. Even Satan himself, even the one who has rejected God from the first moment of his being and turned his whole self away from God — he still exists. Therefore, he still shares in God. And therefore, he still has goodness. Now, it's a goodness of existence, maybe only existence, but he still has existence.

You might ask, what does it mean that we are created out of nothing — ex nihilo? How are we not God? Because we're not God. Because — this is the key thing — God is necessary. He exists, and he doesn't have being — he is being. He is existence. God's essence — who he is — and his existence are the same. We have existence. We are not existence. That's confusing. That's a lot to wrap our minds around. But because we participate in God — that's why we are not God. The fact that we were not around from all eternity is one way of saying that we could not be God.

And it's kind of misleading, then, to say God is creating out of nothing. He's creating out of the abundance of love in the Blessed Trinity. Like, is creation out of nothing? No, I mean — there was nothing, and then there was something. But that comes from God. It's almost like God's saying, "I'm going to take nothing and add something to it." And from the outside it looks that way, but it's more from God. He wanted to create us. So he did.

But then, it's not like pantheism, right — no, we all participate in God. Everything participates in God. So that a Liquid Death can [possibly: brand name — verify audio] — it exists, right? It has existence. It participates in God. It is good, because it participates in God's goodness. Everything participates in God. You might think objects don't. Yeah, they do. By being a can, it does. But you participate not just by existing, but also by your free choice to cooperate and to respond to God — and that's a participation a little bit higher.

So if you sin, you're existing less? Yeah, that's fine. You could say that. And don't exist less. Don't sin. You're existing poorly. Existing poorly.

We have goodness, and we also share in God, especially through the gift of his grace. We are made in his image and likeness, so we have a higher share than other things. Protestants would say, "Well, maybe there's a hierarchy of being. You have inanimate objects, rocks, and then you've got trees and other kinds of plants, and then animals — and higher kinds of animals — and then human beings. And then above human beings, you put angels, of course, and then God. God's the highest kind of being." But we say, no, God is being itself, and everything else shares in his being. And that's why we call it the analogy of being, because everything that has being harkens back to God, who is being. "I am who am."

## The fifth transcendental

Joseph Ratzinger, in *Introduction to Christianity*, made an interesting claim. We're familiar maybe with what we call the transcendentals: truth, beauty, goodness, unity — those four transcendentals. Unity — oneness — that's the original transcendental. God is one. We see that oneness is in a communion of three persons. Beauty gets confused, because — what is that? Is that part of goodness? Is that part of truth? Is that the appeal, the emotional appeal, of these things? Transcendentals are something that is in God. It's a characteristic of being that God has in himself and that everything else shares in, in some way. Joseph Ratzinger said, well, maybe there's a fifth transcendental, a fifth characteristic that, because we know it's in God, it also belongs to all being. What is that fifth transcendental, that characteristic that is in God? Well, we know of it only because God has revealed himself in his inner life of the Trinity. That fifth characteristic is relationality. All beings are relational.

And we share in that relationality because we are capable of relationship. We are capable of giving of ourselves and receiving from another under different aspects and different ways, but we give. We don't just communicate words or concepts. We communicate our very selves through those words and through those concepts, through those symbols, through our actions. We are relational. We share in this relationality that God has within himself.

We recall that God made us in his image and likeness — male and female he created us. To talk about the theology of the body means to talk about relationship, gift, a response. We are capable of receiving another person in their wholeness and giving back of ourselves in our wholeness, as gifts — not holding on to it with strings, pulling the gift back whenever it's no longer to our advantage to give. But when we give, we give totally, and we give freely, and we give fruitfully. This is present in every relationship — especially, we see this in marriage. We see this even in the relationship of mother or father to child, even a friendship. All these things capture something of the Trinity.

No animals — besides human beings, the rational, worded animals — can love. We can truly love. We are truly capable of self-gift, of pouring ourselves out in our entirety for another, under different aspects.

Because God is relational, because God is being itself, because what he has, he is. When we say that God — we would not say God has knowledge. We have intellect and will, right? We are able to know and we are able to love. We are able to consume truth in our intellects, and we are able to reach out towards others in love. God has knowledge and love. But remember, God does not merely have. What we say God has, God is. God's knowledge is the Son. The Father knows the Son. He is the Word of the Father, spoken from all eternity. God's knowledge is the Son. And God's love — God's love is the Holy Spirit, the breathing forth of the Father and the Son.

Knowledge and love are in the Trinity as subsistent relations. That's how we would say it theologically, because what God has, he is. And he's always knowing, and he's always loving.

And there's a going out with the knowledge. Then there's a fittingness with how the Son, the Word, only begotten Son of God the Father from all eternity, goes out into creation. God had intended to enter creation through the second person of the Holy Trinity all throughout, and to draw all things through Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity, in the Holy Spirit, back to God the Father. He planned this. For he came down out of love, in a way that fit with who he was in himself. And the Holy Spirit comes down as well, drawing us all together in one spirit, in one body, because what God has, he is.

And this way, you can see why in the Rublev's icon the heads bow towards the Father, because both the Son and the Holy Spirit come from the Father. And this happens in all eternity.

I think this is the most simple explanation of God. We look for how we are made in the image and likeness of God, because we are relational, because we're able to know and to love — not know merely in a divisive way, through sciences and different ologies, but we're able to know each other.

So that means all reality, then, has a share not only in truth and goodness — and not maybe beauty, also — but also relationality. And that makes sense, because we can only speak of a water bottle in relation to — you know, my ability to drink, my ability to have that, and I get to consume it. It has a relationality to it — a minimal relationality. There is a viewer and a person viewing. There is a relationality with all material things. Even the things that we do not yet see — the tree that falls in the forest with no one around — there is still relationality built into all of these things: that coming together of one thing and another thing. There's relationality built into all material creation, all immaterial creation — between the angels themselves, who are also persons, capable of gifts of themselves, although not through material means such as bodies like us, but through their essence and their intellect. All things share in this relationality. This also means that there is no escaping God — yes.

God is in us. In him we move. "We live and move and have our being," St. Paul says. This means, because the analogy of being, that we cannot escape God. There is no hiding from God. For Adam and Eve, there was no hiding in the Garden of Eden. For us, there is no hiding. We can't go anywhere to escape him. But also, why would we want to?

And that works the other way around. Even every thought that we have is known to God. God the Father knows the number of hairs on your head intimately. He knows you. Everything that goes on — every electrical signal discharged in your brain — he knows it. Because everything participates in him. Every thought in our minds, every concept rolling around in our intellects, God knows. We cannot escape him. God is everything for us. We live in God. And even souls that reject God — that go to the depths of hell, that have rejected him so much — they've not rejected him to the point of losing their existence. They still exist, and because they exist, they participate in God. Even we, when we sin, we still exist and participate in God.

## Intimus: the most in

St. Augustine says God is closer to us than we are to ourselves. We are mysteries unto ourselves. We don't know ourselves fully. When we know ourselves and we know God, and we see ourselves as we truly are — the mystery of our worth and our unworthiness — then there is true humility.

This means that we think we know ourselves to some extent, but there are parts of us that are hidden from us. There are memories we do not access. There are parts of our lives that we simply forget. There are things about us that we don't — you know — engage with very often. God knows everything. He is more interior to me than I am to myself. And there's a relationality there. I'm a participating being in God, but God is already closer to me. Not only is there relationality in how I live out my life — and there is a relationality between my right index finger and my left pinky toe — but there is relationality between God and me. He is closer to me than I am even to myself.

Do you know what the word *intimacy* means and where it comes from? There's a popular idea that intimacy means "into me seeing" — Christopher West says he once heard it said. And that's a helpful thing. It's wrong. It's a false etymology. And it's never meant to be the actual thing that it means, but it's a helpful way to understand into-me-see, intimacy. And we can see how, like, well — Adam knew Eve, and the biblical idea of knowing, and that Eve conceived and brought forth the son. That is an intimacy. From the intimacy of husband and wife, children come about. And so into-me-see: you might see who I really am. You might see me in my vulnerability. You might see my shame. You might see who I truly am. I might be affirmed at the level of my being. And there's a true intimacy when that is mutual, to see into each other. And there's a great truth to this. Into-me-see is a helpful way to understand intimacy. But that's not where we get the word *intimacy*. It's actually far more simple. In Latin, we have the word *in*, and then the word *interior*, which means "more in" — which we say in English "interior." That's the comparative of the word *in*. And then the superlative, *intimus* — *intimus* — which means "most in." From the word *intimus*, we derive the word *intimacy*.

God is intimate with us. He is the most in. He is closer to us than we are to ourselves.

Contemplate him in the holy Eucharist, and we become like him — for we shall see him as he really is, for we will be like him [quotation wording unverified] — as it says in one of the Eucharistic prayers of the Mass. We become like God, and our knowledge of God is not merely from study — sometimes aided by our study — but it becomes the likeness of God on our souls. We who live godly lives know God from the inside — not from the outside in, but from the inside out.

And the saints that have been purified through their long periods of suffering — God will sometimes grant to them particular blessings of knowing him in ways that cannot be communicated. One person here mentioned that they're reading *Fire Within*. *Fire Within* is a great book by Thomas Dubay, discussing the advancement in the spiritual life from a beginner — where a lot of us are — to ultimately being united with God. In that state of being united with God, there is a likeness in our soul, and even experiences of God because of that likeness, that cannot be communicated with others. It can maybe be somewhat communicated poetically. John of the Cross, who lived much of his life in the stage of unitive wholeness with God, would communicate to us through poems — and he would decipher those and lay out their meaning for the different sisters that asked him for explanations. The poetic imagination works in this sort of wholeness — not grasping after the small details, deciphered and put together, but the whole of the thing. Mystical knowledge works in this way, and our knowledge of God will work in this way as well, even if we are merely in the beginner stage.

## The friend already home

St. John Henry Newman, reflecting on Advent and this idea of waiting for Christ, gave the analogy to explain what he means by waiting or watching. It's about when you're waiting for a friend to arrive, and all of your thoughts and all your heart is fixed on that friend, and you have everything ready in your house, everything is prepared for his coming, and now you are waiting, and maybe he is delayed. But that doesn't mean you are distracted and go somewhere else. Your whole being is fixed on waiting for the coming of this friend.

And this is the attitude all of us should have in Advent, of course, but in all times. We are waiting for Christ. We are waiting for him to come again. He who is coming, always coming into this world, who has come in the incarnation, who is with us always, even at the close of the age, who is with us in the Eucharist, who is with us when two or three are gathered in his name. We are always waiting for him. He is always coming.

But to maybe alter the analogy a little bit: if you're waiting for a friend, and perhaps you do get distracted, or perhaps you need to attend to some errand, or maybe because you're preparing for his coming, you go out and you go to Giant Eagle to pick up some groceries or things there that are needed for his visit. But when you come back to your house, you are horrified. There is your friend's car parked in your driveway. You go to the front door. You go in, and you see your friend already sitting on your couch.

What would you say to such a friend? "How the heck did you get in? You are no longer my friend. You violated my privacy. You came when I was gone. That is not okay. Hey, you should wait in your car until I should have gotten back."

Or a true friend — if we've been waiting for him — we would say to him, "Welcome. I'm glad you made yourself at home."

I think this is more fitting with how God comes to us in intimacy.

When we think about the closeness of God to us — God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, all three already dwell within us. The Holy Spirit dwells within — we are a temple of the Holy Spirit. The Father hears us from our inner room. Christ's Sacred Heart beats with our hearts. God is closer to us than we are to ourselves.

In the incarnation and our configuration to Christ, we have an entrance into the love of Father, Son, Holy Spirit — the love that moves the sun and the stars and the moon. And we have access now to that love within us. He has made a home within us.

## Christ resting in the storm

We look for him in created things. We look for him in the things of the world. We try to find God and beauty that is derivative, that shares in God's beauty, yes, but is not beauty, capital B, itself. Meanwhile, he was within us, always closer to us than we are to ourselves.

St. Augustine said that when Christ ascended into heaven, he ascended from the apostles' eyes so they might turn towards their hearts and find him there, because within us, God rests. [quotation wording unverified]

We might think it's very hard for God to rest in my soul, because it seems my experience has told me otherwise. In my soul, there is little rest. There is constant anxiety. There is constant fear, maybe even anger. There's shame, there's guilt. How could God be there already?

And I think maybe mostly for our benefit — the one time that the apostles were all together on the boat, and there was a great storm that came upon them, and these experienced fishermen were afraid for their lives. They who have already weathered many storms — now, this was the big one. This was going to claim their lives. But where is Christ in this? He is there on a cushion, sleeping. How is it that when the boat is being rocked about, water is entering in, there's danger of being capsized, or the ship breaking under the waves? But Christ is there, sleeping. Christ rests in our souls. And he likes to rest in our souls when there is the most amount of chaos.

He is there, always, sustaining us in being. "Each one of us is the result of a thought of God," Pope Benedict said. If God ever ceased to think about us, we would not exist. We would have no past, no present, no future. We would be gone. God is always thinking of us. He's always sustaining us in being. He who is being itself is always holding on to us, loving us. And in us, in our souls, in our hearts, he is resting.

God is with us. Jesus is Emmanuel. God with us. And we still want to say to him, "Lord, do you not care that we are perishing?" Like we'd want to say to the friend who made himself at home, "Do you not care that I was stressed about many things?"

And even in every kind of consternation, every stress, every anxiety, every time we move to anger or resentment, every wound that we feel — the Holy Spirit, Christ the Son, the Father — they're all resting within us, like Christ on the boat in the Sea of Galilee, sleeping on a cushion in the midst of a storm. So God rests in us. He waits for us patiently, for we are his. We already live and move and have our being in him. He desires us for himself. He wants us to be made more and more like him. He waits for our silent adoration. He waits for us to just be with him — to let us know that we are known, that we are loved, that he is drawing us to himself in every time and in every place. God draws close to us. Let God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — love you from the inside out.

## Martha, Mary, and the one thing

We want to be like Martha. "Lord, I'm anxious and troubled by many things. I'm trying to do all this work, and my sister is there just resting with you. Tell her to help me with the work." And our Lord says, "No, Martha, you are anxious and troubled by many things, but one thing is necessary. And Mary has chosen the better part."

Mary, the sister of Martha, has been resting with me. She has been listening to me in the chaos of this home. She is giving me the greatest hospitality, like Abraham and Sarah, but even more so. She is showing true hospitality. Yes, I care for you, and I want you to have the same thing. I want you to have the better part, the one thing. And I want to tell you something about who I am. For God the Son reveals himself, and he speaks of the Father only, for he only has the Father to speak of, because everything he has comes from the Father. He is the Father's, and the Father is his. And then from their love comes the Holy Spirit. Martha, I want you to also know about who I am: that the one thing that is necessary — that one thing is three — three persons and one God.

And this God who created in six days and rested on the seventh, this God, this very God — God, I desire to rest in you — and your hearts and your souls. Never mind the chaos. Never mind the storms. I am with you.

## The cross: the Trinity's embrace

What does Christ speak to us of the love of God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit? By entering into our sufferings in a way so obvious to us that we miss it every time we look upon a crucifix — that he who has shouldered our burdens and taken that cross upon himself suffered every one of our sufferings. He cries out, "Father, into your hands, I commend my spirit." And he sends forth his spirit. The cross is an image of the Trinity. The cross is the Trinity embracing us.

One of you, after my first talk, discussing the sign of the cross, said, "The sign of the cross is a hug from the Holy Trinity." In this way, God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, is closer to us than we are to ourselves. Let him love you. Let him embrace you. Let him hug you. And respond by welcoming him in your hearts and letting that love spread to others.

God rests in us. He is intimate with us. Whether we like it or not — I suggest that we like it.

## Back to St. Patrick

This last of our conferences circles back to where we started, talking about St. Patrick and his evangelization of the people of Ireland. And now we end our conferences on the mystery of the Holy Trinity, once again reflecting on evangelization.

St. Patrick had blessed a fire in the 400s that no attempts by the Druid pagans could extinguish. That fire kept burning and burned brightly — a witness against the pagans, but more importantly, a witness of God's love. And that fire was meant to go forth and to spread.

These words to evangelize come from our Lord before he ascended into heaven, telling his apostles: "All power in heaven and earth — all authority in heaven and earth has been given over to me. Go, therefore, make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Behold, I am with you always, even to the close of the age."

There has been an evangelizing impulse from the beginning, from the gift of the Holy Trinity given to us. Because we are baptized into Christ, because we receive the divine life of the blessed three in one, we are impelled to go outwards.

This is something that even the Church's canon law, the official laws of the Church, recognizes: that all of us, by virtue of our baptism and confirmation, are by nature missionary. We're meant to go out, to evangelize, to spread the gospel.

This is derived from our Lord saying — the greatest commandment is to love God, and the second is like it: to love your neighbor as yourself. And the perfection of that commandment is to love one another as I have loved you. This is the spreading of the love. What has been poured into us cannot be contained. If we have now shared in the mystery of the Holy Trinity, that is not a mystery for us alone; it is meant to go out and make us evangelize all people.

Our Lord gives us this missionary imperative to make disciples of all nations. And going back to that little rectangle on Rublev's Trinity icon — this represents the four corners of the world. The message of the Trinitarian love of God is meant to go out.

## Owning the mystery

We think about evangelizing those who do not yet know Christianity, or who do not know it well, and we run into an awkward question. We want to tell them about Jesus. We want to tell them that Jesus died for their sins — that he shares with them their sufferings, though they know it not — that he is closer to them than they are to themselves, that they are meant to be included in the love of his Sacred Heart, and that he not only died for us, but rose from the dead and ascended to the Father. And this is all well and good, but to anyone hearing this, this is a problem. For — well, you're saying Jesus is God? Like, yes. But doesn't Jesus have a God? Is he just the Son of God? Are there two gods? What's going on with that?

We run into this problem of the Trinity right away, anytime we try to evangelize and catechize as part of that evangelization. And it is an awkward thing. Do we try to get around it? Do we insist there's one God and just say, "Don't worry about that — it's not that important"? That there are actually two persons here going on — actually three persons, we've got the Holy Spirit in there too — because two is awkward, but three — it kind of seems more like we're owning it — but "don't worry about it, it's a mystery"? We run into big problems, because we're trying to spread the faith, but when we try to spread the faith, we hit this problem of the Trinity pretty early on.

Some from the outside might say, "How is this altogether different from those people who believe in multiple gods? It seems that you want to have your cake and eat it too." Or maybe, "How is this different from Mormonism? For don't they have one big God, and Jesus is the God of this planet? And if we are good Mormons, then we become gods of our own planets after we die. How is this different? For don't you, as Christians, believe that you will become like God? Did not one of your early Christian saints — St. Athanasius — say, 'For the Son of God became man so that we might become God'?"

Well, this is confusing. What do we do with this? I think we need to take ownership of it. I think we need to acknowledge that the Trinitarian mystery of God is something that actually gives us greater access to God. God is not just so far from us; he is close to us. He is not just transcendent, far away; he is immanent — close to us, within us, the most within us. He is intimate with us.

And once we even just begin to understand the Trinity — even with just helpful images — the fact that some things can be one and three, the relationality between beings, between people, between persons, the image of the Trinity expressed in marriage, where husband and wife give themselves so totally to each other — there is a fruitfulness in which a third person comes forth — this helps us to see that there is a Trinitarian structure to our relationships. I think it's something we can take ownership of as we go out and evangelize.

## Other Christs

But we are meant for others to see only Jesus in us, that we might shine out with his light. Mother Teresa understood intuitively that we are to be Christ to Christ. We are to shine with his light. We are to be as Jesus to them. We are other Christs — not just because we belong to Christ and are called Christians, but because we are Christ. We belong to him, yes. Yes, but we are also him. We are the extension of Christ in this world.

We, who have been incorporated into his body through the Holy Eucharist, are his hands and his feet going out into the world, and we serve others — not somehow lesser than Christ, but as Christ himself.

Here we start to see this positive feedback loop. It is Christ serving Christ. And we who give of ourselves so fully to this work of evangelization, this work of the corporal works of mercy — we serve Christ. They are built up, and they too are transformed. For who cannot experience the love of God through us, and be transformed by it? They might go forth and also love Christ and others. This is a chain reaction. In an atomic bomb, this would result in a massive explosion of megatons of dynamite. For us, this is evangelization.

We serve Christ. And as Christ, everyone we serve encounters the gospel. They encounter Christ, and they know they are loved. The two great desires of our heart are to be known and to be loved. And they are built up and transformed. And how could they go forth not loving others with that same love?

## Nothing more beautiful

There's nothing more beautiful, Pope Benedict says, than for us to know Christ, for us to know that we are loved by him, and for us to tell others of our friendship with him. And there goes this chain reaction into the four corners of the world, this totality of our witness, of our own martyrdom, of giving ourselves so fully over to Christ that we become him, and we become his extension into this world. This is a gift never meant to be extinguished within us.

Who, having a light, puts it under a basket? No, you put it on a lampstand to shine out. "You are the light of the world," Christ says. He also says, "I am the light of the world." Both of these are true, for we are the light of the world because we are other Christ. We shine out with the same light so others might only see him. And from that, the light is spread.

Christ came that we might have life and have it abundantly. He came to set the world on fire, and how he wished it were already ablaze!

The mission of evangelization — we remember how St. Ignatius of Loyola would say to his missionaries, the Jesuits of the 16th century, as they were being sent out into the four corners of the world to spread the gospel to people who have never heard it before: "Go, set the world on fire." [quotation wording unverified] We burn with that love, that catching force of the sympathetic influence, that others might see Christ in us and be influenced by it. They might be set afire with that same love.

## The Easter flame

The way we evangelize the love of the Trinity is the same way the light is spread at the Easter Vigil, the mother of all liturgies, when the priest blesses the fire, the sacred fire. At every Easter Vigil, the priest blesses the fire and lights the Paschal candle from that fire, lifting it up and saying, "Lumen Christi" — the light of Christ. And from that one flame, there goes forth all the different tapers, all the little candles around the church. The church that was in darkness is now filled with the light. Each of us carries in ourselves the flame of Christ in our hearts, symbolized by the candle that we carry. Now it is kept blazing and is meant not to be confined to that church, but to go out from there.

At the end of every Mass, it is said: "Go forth." In some way or other — "Go forth, the Mass has ended." "Go and announce the gospel of the world" — whatever variation it is, there is a going forth.

## Missa est: "it has been sent"

In the original, in the older Mass, there was just one phrase: "Go forth, the Mass has ended." *Ite, missa est.* "Ite" means go. Go out. What happened here is not meant to be confined to here.

This is a liturgy that is meant to envelop all of creation. It is cosmic in its proportions. Christ comes so that all things might be recapitulated in him, might be united in him — all things. Christ says in the book of Revelation, "Behold, I make all things new."

This is a liturgy that is meant to go out, to envelop all of creation, and it happens through us. "Go out. The Mass has ended." *Missa est.* Well, that's actually a bad translation, or a different translation. "*Missa est*" does not mean "the Mass has ended." It means "it has been sent."

What has been sent? Sometimes people translate that — "Oh, it means be sent. Go, be sent." Some priests will explain this, not understanding Latin, that "missa est" means "be sent" — "go and be sent, be sent, go out into the world, be sent into the world." No, well, it doesn't quite mean that. The "go" means go, and "be sent" — that's the going out, that's the "go." It means something has been sent. It happened. The grammar of *missa est* means something has been sent.

What has been sent? Who has it been sent to? What has been sent is the offering of Christ, the Eucharistia — the thanksgiving, the oblation, the full gift of himself. Who has it been sent to? We might think that the Mass is offered to us. It's not offered to us. The Mass is our offering in Christ to the Father. Christ has been offered to the Father. The Mass is completed. And the Mass is not just that Jesus comes down to us in the Eucharist. The Mass is that Jesus comes, and he goes — he goes out from the Father down to us, and he goes back to the Father. What he does in salvation — in becoming incarnate and dying and rising and ascending — he does in the Mass. He has been sent back to the Father.

And we, as Christians, as other Christs, are meant to pattern our lives after that heavenly liturgy. We are meant to go forth and be sent, as Christ was sent, to the Father. It has been sent, it has been offered, and we are now to do likewise in the way we live our lives. We are other Christ. The liturgy does not end with the last words of the Mass, with the last song we sing. The liturgy goes onwards in our hearts, where we can always pray to the Father in secret and make offerings of ourselves.

And what Christ does in the Mass is not just a mystery about the second person of the Holy Trinity; it's all three persons. It's through Christ, with Christ, in Christ, to God the almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, to whom all praise and glory belongs forever and ever. That mystery is meant to be lived by us now, and going forth. Mass is not a one-time thing. It's not something that we merely do for half an hour to 45 minutes a day and just have that be that. It is how we live our lives. The source and the summit of our lives — it's also how we live our evangelization.

For God's unselfish love, his agape — that has been poured out, and from it, in its excess, in its generosity, in its abundance, all things were created. That agape poured out now has that response in us of our Eucharistia. The liturgy is the pouring out of God — his agape — and our Eucharistia, our thanksgiving in Christ, coming together. And now this is meant to spread outwards. We are to be the light to the world.

## Living it, not just teaching it

In fact, not just how we teach, but how we live, needs to be relational. We think of it mostly in terms of teaching, and we think there is right teaching and there is wrong teaching. And those who espouse right teaching — we are in that group. We are in that same tribe. That's where we're comfortable. That's where we're supported. They will say things that we will most likely agree with, and therefore we will be around those people. And those people who say false teachings, we will reject. We will separate them. They don't matter. They are problems to be solved, or problems to be ignored more often. And in this way of only focusing on teaching, we end up falling into that way of rejecting others and rejecting that mystery — not allowing that story that they have, of brokenness and error, to be completed by Christ with the mercy of God.

Do not just teach the Trinity. To live the Trinity means to love them as persons. We are tempted to have that dividing line — there's us and them. But there is no peace in thinking this way. This is the peace the world gives. As Father [possibly: Michael] often says, it's the peace that comes from the unity of everyone minus one. It's how we can scapegoat. We can victimize, set them aside, not worry about them, and have our own little group that we feel comfortable with. But that's not how we are to live.

The mystery of the Trinity does embrace all. All things make sense by it. All persons are meant to be included in it — if not just by nature, but by grace as well. So to live from the love of the Trinity means to love all those — even those that hate us — as our Lord says, "Blessed are you when you are hated and persecuted for my name." We can share that blessedness if we make sure to give of ourselves and be truly vulnerable.

## Tell everyone

How could we contain this mystery in us? If you have been changed by this retreat, if you have been transformed by the renewal of your mind, you won't let it just stay with you. If this is a secret, tell everyone about this. This is not meant to be confined. This is not meant to be held back. This is meant to be the leading point, the arrow of our evangelization.

There is nothing to be ashamed of in the Trinity, and nothing to hide away and say, "I can't understand it, neither can you, but just know that Jesus loves you." Well, yes, Jesus loves you, but he wants you to be part of him, incorporated into his body, sharing in his very being, and to be part of the Holy Trinity with, through, and in him.

We are made from love. God created everything out of love in order to draw all things to himself, and that is meant to include us. We who are embraced by the very Trinity want others to share in that embrace also. So do not hide this away. The Trinity should be spilling out of our thoughts, out of our words, out of our actions — not just out of the words — we reduce everything to the words. No, we show it forth.

I love how Catholics — because we understand mystery, because we understand that we are created in God's image and likeness and are actually sharing his being by creation, and by redemption we are given his grace; his inner life is our life as well — we don't merely tell, we show. We show it by the evident force, the catching force of our sympathetic influence, our care for others, because we see Christ in every single person we encounter, so that no one is a mere mortal, as C.S. Lewis would say, but everyone, as St. John Henry Newman says, is "an infinite abyss of existence."

We are not meant to remain empty abysses. We are meant to be filled with the fire of God's love. We are meant to share in his being. Christ chooses to do that through us. What is his favorite way, the way he loves best, to share the glory of God — Father, Son, Holy Spirit — but by us shining on those around us, those he has given for us to love.

## Floodgates

And in every single person we can see the mystery of Christ and his redemption, his yearning for us. And every single one of us, we are all mysteries of the Holy Trinity. Some of us are waiting to be activated. Some of us are dead in our sins, waiting for Christ to rise again and go to the Father. Christ has come to us in our brokenness and has forgiven us our sins — our venial sins even in the holy Eucharist, and our mortal sins through the sacrament of [possibly: confession]. All of us, though, are meant to share in that divine life.

Let us not hold that back. Let's open the floodgates of God's mercy and love into this fallen world. Let him love us. Let the waves and the billows, the torrents, all wash over us. In the chaos of this life, let us find Christ within, already resting in our souls, and be impelled by him, especially in his gift of the Holy Eucharist, to go forth.

We are now to spread [possibly: mercy] to everyone else as well, and to go forth and to welcome — just in the same way as we welcome Christ in our own hearts — welcome those that we don't want to have any part in us [unclear], those we'd rather protect ourselves from. Welcome, then, with forgiveness; welcome them with compassion, to share in their own sufferings, and then to help draw them. We might be the means by which others encounter the same Trinitarian love. And our baptisms are not meant to terminate within us. They are meant to impel us forward as well, to make disciples of all nations, so that others might be baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.

Let Christ love us. Let him love others through us. And may we all be caught up in that love that made the sun and the stars, and be joined with Christ and the Father and the Holy Spirit in the heavenly liturgy to come.


# Epilogue — Engineering Mystery

## The Engineering Worldview

Without realizing it, all of us have a way of viewing the world. We have an imaginative vision, a way of looking at things, a lens through which everything comes, and we experience it — we add labels to it, we quantify, we qualify, we calculate. And I would say that there is an engineering worldview, a way in which we look at the world as engineers. I was very much of the engineering worldview. It was a safe way of thinking for me, especially in high school.

I found out that I liked physics. Physics you can trust. Math you can trust. It does not lie to you. It does not hurt you. It doesn't try to hurt you. So I found that I could throw myself into physics problems, and it would be a way of understanding the world. I could understand it through mathematical equations. I could model it. I could wrap my mind around it. It was a way of calculating rather than reflecting and resting.

And one part of being in that engineering worldview is that I liked to optimize. Everything was an optimization problem. I desired efficiency. I would not waste effort. Why would I study for an exam more than I needed to? I could know the minimum amount I had to study to get the grade that I desired, and then I would study that amount, and then I would waste the rest of the time in dissipation. That was my strategy in high school, and for the most part it actually worked. And laziness was a huge motivator for me, because I could control the things I could control. The things I couldn't control, I didn't have to worry about, because I could go off and be an engineer.

## An Optimized Vocation

I was blessed to be from a large Catholic family. I am the eighth of fourteen children, and I grew up with thirteen siblings. Even at Franciscan, that is a large family. And when I was in high school, I had a deepened experience of the love of God for me — actually coming to a Franciscan youth conference after my junior year of high school, where the desire to pray was placed on me. The desire for holiness was something that stuck with me. The desire to do God's will was there for me.

But I had my life mostly set. I desired to be an engineer. I could use the physics that I was learning, and I could apply it to making things. I desired to make things — it was fun, exciting — and this way I could make a job out of it, and actually do a pretty good job. So my high school became about optimizing my high school experience so that when I arrived at college, I could study engineering in an even more efficient way. That meant taking AP classes with the advice of my older siblings who had done engineering before me. I could learn from them about what to do and what not to do, and I would be able to enter college with at least a semester's worth of credits under my belt.

There was really only one option for me for college, and that was the University of Pittsburgh — because why would I waste my time applying somewhere else if I didn't want to go there? I was lazy. Why would I care if some other school wanted to accept me? I only had one place I wanted to go. I remember Franciscan University reached out to me and invited me to apply, and I said, "You don't have an engineering program. If you had an engineering program, I would go there. But you don't. So I'm going to go to the University of Pittsburgh. If I was going to be a priest, I'd go to Franciscan."

You see how that worked out?

So when I went to the University of Pittsburgh, I had a desire to be an engineer. I put my identity in my work. I studied because I was told that freshman engineering, before Dr. Sanchez got involved with teaching freshmen, was grueling. And they told you, you have to make a choice. You either have friends, or you are an engineer. You can party on weekends, but there are other programs for you in the arts and sciences. Liberal arts? Yeah, do that. That way you can party. For some, there's actually maybe an incentive not to virtue but to a great fear, to keep them locked into a grueling freshman engineering program. So I threw myself into it. It was part of my identity. But also I had a desire at the same time to pray, to enter into the interior life. I knew that was lacking in me. I knew that many of my years and my youth were wasted away in utter dissipation. It was not YouTube then, but the beginnings of social media — I had a MySpace account. I was friends with Tom from MySpace, as was everyone. But the dissipation was mostly watching TV, watching movies, getting my life to just disappear, staying up late doing nothing, tiring myself out and doing nothing.

I didn't like this. I wanted the interior life, and I didn't realize what it was. I wanted to pray. When I came to the Franciscan youth conference during high school, I got a scapular, and I was like, I need to pray the rosary every day, because if I don't, it doesn't count, and I'm living a lie — which is a little bit true. I didn't pray the rosary, I don't think, at all during my senior year. I started a few times, and I was like, I just can't do it. It's too much, because it was the weight of all of this — a lot of suffering, a lot of woundedness. But I didn't get around to actually praying.

But I thought — college — I can start these habits. I had a little pocket version of the Imitation of Christ, and I snuck off to the library to read this in between classes, back before I actually had any kind of work. I started going to Mass once a week, in addition to Sunday Mass — I always went to Sunday Mass — and I stayed after and got dinner with my brother. We had deep conversations. I started to do Eucharistic Adoration. First, I was filling up the time with some book that told me what to pray, just to get me through that hour. And more and more I opened up towards silence, the resting, and the embrace of Christ. As this went on, I found a desire in me.

The priests who had influenced me so much now that I was in college — the priests of the Oratory, who I got to share life with — I got to see how they lived, I got to see how they prayed. They were normal. I wanted to be like them. And I started serving Mass, which was a ploy to get me to go to Mass more than just one extra time a week. But it worked. And I found I wanted to be on the altar — not physically on the altar, but the expression means in the sanctuary, helping, assisting. I saw that desire in me grow.

And so my sophomore year — having taken upon myself by that time a daily rosary, almost daily Mass, almost daily holy hour — I went to my uncle. He was a priest. And I said, "Hey, Uncle Mark, what do I do? I'm thinking maybe priesthood." And he said, "I have an idea for you. Not this summer, but the summer after — Trinity Sunday, that's the Sunday after Pentecost — you need to make a decision. Right now you're broadly discerning, and that makes sense for where you are in life. You're nineteen years old. But you need to decide whether you're going to go from discerning to discerning of the priesthood. And that's the time you're going to make that decision."

He was telling me this — it was December of 2008 — and he was talking about a year and a half from then. And I said, "Uncle Mark, you need to hear me out. I've been going to Mass every day, and I want to be there. I've been praying the Rosary. I've been doing the Holy Hours. I actually really desire this. And you're the first person I've told about this desire, because I kept it so hidden. Because if you let someone know about it, you might get hurt."

He said, "Oh, let's up this a little bit. Not a year and a half from now, but this summer. Come Trinity Sunday, the Sunday after Pentecost, you need to make a decision. Are you going to discern the priesthood, or are you going to just go on discerning? That's when you're going to make the decision. In the meantime, continue doing what you're doing."

That was the advice I was given. And come Trinity Sunday — a very important day — I made that decision, peacefully. I told my father. He was thrilled. "Oh, my son? A priest? Oh no — wow, that's amazing. Yes, wow, yes." And my mom said, "Oh, but you were such a good engineer." And so that summer, I dropped out of all my engineering classes, took philosophy classes, switched my major to philosophy — I was told I had enough credits that I had an engineering minor in electrical engineering — and I would graduate two years later with a degree in philosophy. And I would join the Oratory of the same priest who had influenced me during my undergrad.

And now, as a priest, I work in campus ministry to give back to the students at Pitt, CMU, and Chatham.

That engineering worldview was very important to me as a high schooler and as a college student. But that is not what it means to be transformed in Christ. This is how the world looks at things. This is how this age looks at things. We reduce ourselves to calculators, to robots, so easily replaced by artificial intelligence. We forget what it means to be human. We forget what it means to be a son or daughter of God the Father.

## What Mystery Is Not

When you hear the word mystery, the typical connotation is that a mystery is something that needs to be solved — Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes. There is something missing. There are clues that, if we add them up in the correct kind of way, we can actually come up with the answer. There is a solution out there. We must solve it. A mystery in this situation, in this application, is something that you don't know the answer to because there's something unknown. Mysteries are solved, in this understanding of the word mystery.

That is not what we mean by mystery. If you've ever taken notes, you may cross that out. As we saw at the very beginning of this book, that is not Christian mystery. A Christian mystery is not something that you don't know the answer to. Christian mystery is something that is so knowable that it overwhelms your ability to know.

We can think of the sun. If you were to try to look at the sun, you could not actually see it. Is it because it's invisible? No. It's so visible that it overwhelms your ability to see. Think of the eclipse of April 2024. You could not look at it with your naked eyes until it was entirely occluded by the moon. Or if you tried to look at it through a lens that was not approved, but you thought it was better than the other lenses — you would actually damage your eye, leaving a little bit of a shaky part in the middle of your right eye. Don't ask me how I know. I thought a welding lens was sufficient. Engineering worldview: not good. You actually need to do your research and get the ones that actually work. It was a bad mistake. It worked for the eclipse in 2017.

But you can't see the sun, typically. Yet the sun is that by which you see everything else. Mysteries illuminate. They overwhelm. We are involved in them.

## The Moon and the Child

I'll talk about another celestial body: the moon.

Several years ago, I was visiting my sister and her four-year-old daughter, Elizabeth. They had just returned from a trip from Eastern Pennsylvania to visit my niece's grandmother. So I excitedly asked her, "Elizabeth, how was your trip?" And she says to me, "Uncle PJ, guess what? When we go with the car, the moon follows us."

My heart sank, because — Elizabeth, that's not how it works. You have to understand that the moon is so far away that you will not notice any difference in the angle at which you're seeing the moon, because it's so far away and it's so huge. You think it's closer to you, and as you're driving, it looks like it's following along with you. But it's not.

In fact, we can even prove this, and I quickly concocted in my mind how we can apply the principle of non-contradiction, in some way, to this. Put you, Elizabeth, in one car, and put your sister, Mary Margaret — very good Catholic names — in the other car, and drive different directions. You both observe the moon. You'll see that it's following both of you, and that's gotta be impossible. So we have to look for another explanation. Now, parallax is gonna be a little bit too complicated for a four-year-old. But I thought, maybe this is the time for me, as her uncle, to correct her. She is utterly wrong. The moon does not actually follow you. It's an inanimate object that doesn't care about you.

So I said to her, "Elizabeth, that's not true. It only looks like it's following you."

She interrupts and says, "No, Uncle PJ, it really follows me."

And then I was like — oh. I was trying to crush her wonder. I was trying to crush her childlikeness. Had the moon any care for little four-year-old Elizabeth? No. No, it doesn't.

You look up in the sky — those things don't matter to you. Those are things to be analyzed and measured. We know the distances. We know the sizes. We know the effects of the atmosphere as the moon goes over the horizon. We never see the other side of the moon, because it's tidally locked. We can study all of these things.

But for a child, the moon involved her. She was implicated in it. It's as if the moon cared for her and went with her. Physically, she was wrong. But in the greater sense — mysteriously — she was right. We might think of the ancients, how they looked up at the stars, and grown men would sketch out, connecting the dots from one star to another. They would draw pictures in the sky — constellations — that reminded them of the myths that were so important to them, the stories that had meaning. The stars involve us. The moon cares about us. There is a man in the moon, as it were.

## The Viewer Is Part of It

I looked at it in the technical frame of mind — the engineering worldview: to be measured, calculated, analyzed, kept at a distance — because, you know what, keeping things at a distance is safe. And I know I won't be wrong, because I can trust the science. I would never have to make that mistake of thinking that someone cared about me when they didn't.

Gabriel Marcel was a philosopher in the twentieth century. He was of the school called personalism — and of that school, in a different area of the world, was also Karol Wojtyła. Gabriel Marcel said that mystery, properly speaking, involves the viewer. We are part of it. The world of the problematic is something that is separate. He distinguished problem from mystery. Typically, mystery novels — mystery novels are problems. Mysteries themselves are lived.

You can think of a principle in physics, especially in quantum physics. When you go to measure something — this doesn't matter on the macro level, but it matters on the micro and the nano level — the means by which you measure it actually affect the thing you're measuring. You can't get an accurate, precise measurement, because the probe you're using comes into contact with it. The same thing is true with quantum physics. And if you had a qubit — you know about quantum computers — a qubit is something that is a zero or a one or anywhere in between. But once you measure it, it defaults to a zero or a one. The act of measuring it affects it. It seems that on the quantum physics level, we understand that we are part of the experiment. We are not separate from it. In this way, it's somewhat mysterious — if only by analogy.

## A Solar Flare of Love

God is a mystery. That's not because we can't understand God. There's so much about God that it overwhelms us. We are enveloped in it, because we ourselves participate in God. This is actually a basic thing about our understanding of metaphysics, of being. The being that we have is a participation in God, who is capital-B Being. God does not have being; he is being. And so your being is a participation in his. And you can't separate yourself from that. You can study it — you can study it theologically or philosophically. You can do that from a distance, actually relatively safely. But you could also experience it as mystery, where you enter into it.

Not only is God a mystery — the Trinity is a mystery. And you think, the Trinity — oh, well, we can't understand it. No. There's so much to understand about the Trinity. It overwhelms our ability to think. We can't possibly grasp it. You try to stare into the Trinity, and it's like staring into the sun. We can separate it into its parts, but we're always going to be overwhelmed by it. You can't fully embrace it, because you can't fully put your arms around a mountain. You can't fully take in the Trinity. We can't study it — theology does study it, and theology has its uses. What is the proper response to the Trinity? Worship.

Seeing that we are included in this mystery — that it was like a solar flare out of the love of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — and that even all things were created. The excess, the abundance of God's love spilled out, strewn across the sky — the stars, the distant galaxies, all the details of the earth — the excess of God's creation. And out of this, God looked for someone who would share in his image and likeness, who could respond — who we are as human beings — who could participate in God. We could reflect him. We could give of ourselves. We could receive others. There would be persons that were embodied. So we are included in the mystery of creation. We can study creation from a distance, but we are studying ourselves in the process.

And this Holy Trinity is not something that is merely study. Rather, marriage enshrines it. Motherhood embraces it. Fatherhood mirrors it. Friendship touches it. Loneliness does not give in to despair — adores it.

## The Invisible World and the Sacraments

The Trinity is lived — to glorify God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — because we are included in that mystery. The proper response to mystery is to enter, to delve deeply, to be embraced by it. This is the fundamentally Christian worldview. We understand mystery. We see that mere matter is not mere matter. The things that we see around us are teeming with the life hidden from us. The visible world — that which we can analyze, calculate, measure — communicates the invisible world.

St. John Henry Newman — when he was a child, he said that he thought the invisible world was more real to him than the visible world: that the visible world was like a shroud placed over us, and that the invisible world was where he truly felt at home — the world of God, the world of angels. As children, we see this more intuitively. And we learn to forget about the invisible world.

This takes on another dimension for us as Catholics, because when we take mere matter, it becomes transformed — sacramentally, of course, in the seven sacraments; preeminently in the sacrament of the Holy Eucharist: mere bread and wine transubstantiated into the body and blood, soul and divinity of Christ. The visible communicates the invisible. The matter communicates the immaterial. And this becomes a vehicle of grace. The life of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — his indwelling — comes to us powerfully through the sacraments.

So when you are baptized, and the waters pour over your head, over the threefold invocation — "I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" — what is symbolized by that action happens. You are transformed. You are a beloved daughter, a beloved son, from that moment onwards, of God the Father. You live your whole life now, embraced by God the Father.

## Intimacy — The Most In

So you can have, as you go on through life, this confidence that St. Thérèse of Lisieux too had. She said, in her Last Conversations, that even if she were to commit the worst sin, she could throw herself into the arms of God, her Father, and be confident that he would catch her. This means, then, to live differently, because we live in that mystery of the love of God for us.

One way to enter into the mystery is silence. God the Father spoke one word from all eternity — St. John of the Cross says — and that word was his Son, and that word was uttered in silence. And if we are to receive that word, we are to receive it in silence.

It's in the silence of the heart that Christ instructs us to go. He says: when you pray, do not be like the hypocrites who like to stand on street corners and marketplaces. Rather, when you pray, go into your inner room, close the door, and your Father who hears in secret will reward you. Of course, Christ can be referring to your actual inner room — the place that is cut off from everyone else — but he's also referring to the inner room of your heart. And you go into that place, and God the Father hears you.

Do you know the etymology of the word intimacy? Who's ever heard that "intimacy" means "into me see"? Has anyone heard that before? Okay — that's not true. But it's a nice way to kind of explain it, like a little tool. "Intimacy means into me see. You can see into me." Okay, fair enough. That's not bad. But that's not what it is.

It's a Latin word — *intimus*. This is a superlative. This is grammar, so this is hard for engineers to understand. Engineers are notoriously bad writers. A superlative is, you know, good, better, best. Best is the superlative. Better is the comparative. And good is the normal word. So: in, interior, comparative, superlative — *intimus*, the most in. Intimacy means that when we have intimacy with God, he is most interior to ourselves — the truth we met earlier in the chapter on intimacy.

St. Augustine said he looked for God outside of himself, looking to fill himself up with the things of this world. But as everyone [possibly: at last], he looked into his heart, and he found God there. "Late have I loved you, Beauty ever ancient, ever new — late have I loved you! You have made us for yourselves, O Lord, and our hearts are restless till they rest in thee." God envelops us with the mystery of his love. His Trinitarian love spills out to us. We are included in that love. He is more intimate. He is closer to me than I am to myself.

Every year on the feast day of St. Augustine, if you go on Instagram — which I don't ever recommend — you will find that some Catholic person likes to quote this one thing that St. Augustine apparently said: "I looked into my deepest wound, and there I saw your glory, and it dazzled me" [quotation wording unverified]. God rests in those deepest, darkest places in our hearts — those places we want to forget about, places where I've been hurt, where I feel like I have not received love. God is already there. As much as we invite him in, as much as we try to make him show up, he's already loving us.

## God Has Already Loved You

And this is love — not that we have loved God, St. John says — not that we have proved to God that we loved him, that we've gone out of our way and made sure that we loved him first. No. "And this is love: not that we have loved God, but that he has loved us and sent his Son as expiation for our sins." God is already loving us. He has taken the initiative. He's not waiting around for us to prove ourselves.

Part of the engineering worldview is that we've got to work. We've got to have success in order to make any kind of progress. I mean, the problem with school is you've got to get your good grades. You've got to get through this class. You've got to do this project. You go into your work, and you've got to do what your boss expects of you. You've got to live up to some standard of performance. And that way you might be rewarded with some kind of raise. That makes sense. That's the basic reward system that we have. It's the worldview that we have of this age — to not be conformed to this age, but to be transformed by the renewal of your mind. For God has already loved you. You don't have to do anything to prove it. How we live that out, then, is that we receive that love, and we respond.

## The Enchanted World: Liturgy, Gift, and Poverty

Part of living in mystery, part of seeing the world as it truly is — the invisible world underneath everything that is visible — is living in an enchanted world that has a magic behind it. There are forces beyond what we can see.

When we live in this way as Christians — not near [possibly: mere] magic, but the love of God pouring out from that chair there, from that watch, from those blinds, from everything — you can see God's love pouring out. This transforms how we view the world. It reminds us — everything reminds us — of the excess of God's love, of the abundance of God's love. So now when we look up at the night sky, we don't just see stars that get studied under the science of astronomy. Now we can see God's love for us and the generosity of his creation. And it's not wrong to think that the stars care about us in some way. It's not wrong to think that the moon actually follows us, because it does. And in a grander way of going about it, God's creation is for us. It is his gift to us. It is an icon yet to be transformed. For Christ came to draw all things to himself, to recapitulate all things in Christ, and to offer himself to God the Father.

One way in which we do this is what we call liturgy. Liturgy — when you hear that word, especially spoken by a priest wearing a cassock, it sounds like: reverence, and pristine movements, and signs of the cross that are crisp, and hands that, as a priest, don't go beyond your shoulders. I don't know about your experience of liturgy at Franciscan. It might be different from that. You might think of someone talking about liturgy as something that is just — you've got to exactly follow the rules. That's not liturgy as rubrics. Liturgy — as David Fagerberg put it — is doing the world the way the world was meant to be done. It's actually an answer. Why food? To eat. Why that chair? To sit. Why something rather than nothing? Liturgy.

We are meant to respond to the gift, to the mystery of God. We are meant to respond to his love for us in creating us. We are meant to respond to him in redeeming us. We are meant to respond — with, through, and in Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity — to God the Father in the Holy Spirit. And the way we do that is through the body of Christ — the body of Christ, his bride, the Church. And when we follow the rules, when we do Mass, we hear those readings that are themselves a prayer offered to God. And when we give ourselves over to God in the Offertory, we give ourselves over to him in Holy Communion — who gives himself so totally over to us — we are caught up in a higher cycle of giving.

Have you studied any systems engineering yet? Do you know feedback loops — positive and negative feedback loops? A negative feedback loop is typically the more desirable one. The speed limit — you know: you go a little bit higher going downhill, the car will slow down; if it slows down too much, it speeds up, to stay at a constant speed. Negative feedback loops are safe. But in the spiritual life, we actually want to be caught up in a positive feedback loop — the loop we met earlier in this book as the positive feedback loop of grace. We want the output to be fed back into the input and to increase things exponentially. In the liturgy, we are offering ourselves to God, who offers himself to us.

Now, we know at least enough theology of the body to know about the law of the gift, as it is commonly called. Engineers at Franciscan get to learn a little bit about theology of the body. The law of the gift means that when you give something away, you don't lose it. You actually gain. It's actually a spiritual life hack.

For if you give of yourself, you end up gaining everything of yourself. It is only in a sincere gift of oneself, the Second Vatican Council says, that man finds himself. So if you give yourself away, so totally over to God, you gain. You feed that output back into the input. You give everything of yourself over to God, and you've already gained so much that you give more back to God — whether that's in the expressions of devotion, whether it's a willingness to suffer, union with Christ crucified, whether it's that friend you bring along the way — you give more back to God. And this continues over and over again. Every day when you offer yourself to God, you make that little act of self-offering — giving your day, giving your work, giving your thoughts, your words, your actions, over to God, whether through a morning offering or the prayer of the Church, or a desire to go to Mass. You're giving yourself back to God.

What typically happens is we hold back. We don't want to give ourselves. We think it's not safe. It might get out of control. Faith, hope, and love — the greatest of these is love. These three are theological virtues: because they go towards God, they do not admit of excess. Whereas in something like courage, you might say too much courage is foolhardiness. But too much faith — there is no such thing. Too much hope — there is no such thing. Too much love — there is no such thing.

We can get caught up in the mystery of God's love again and again, and completely offer ourselves in our totality, in our capacity to give of ourselves — not in mere contracts and little bits of ourselves, siphoning off just the smallest amounts in order to be safe — when we give our whole selves in our poverty. It's like the woman, the widow, who gave only two small coins in the treasury. And our Lord, on the eve of his passion, said: that woman has given more than everyone else. Everyone else gave of their excess. She gave from her poverty — her whole livelihood. And we give, we give poorly.

One more thing that challenges the engineering worldview: you've heard the expression, "Whatever is worth doing is worth doing well." But some of you are familiar with G.K. Chesterton. He said, "If a thing is worth doing, it is worth doing badly." I would correct Chesterton here. Anything worth doing is worth doing poorly. For if it's worth doing, it's worth doing. It doesn't matter how you do it. We end up getting ourselves caught up in that aphorism — "whatever is worth doing is worth doing well" — and we don't even do it.

I go back to myself when I was a senior in high school, and I wanted to pray. I had opportunities to pray so much, but instead I listened to music. Instead I would just sit on a couch. Instead I would just fill up my time with whatever I could, in order to not have to face myself. But maybe just praying poorly would have been the move to do at that time. Whatever's worth doing is worth doing poorly.

And in fact, in this setting of gift and mystery, whatever is worth doing is worth doing out of your poverty. Give of yourself so totally that you become poor in the process. The lovely emphasis we have as Franciscans on the poverty of St. Francis — Franciscan spirituality — means that poverty is essential to giving. You make yourself poor, and in making yourself poor, you gain everything.

## The Feed and the Feasts

We have a God who is a loving Father who won't deprive you of what you're meant to have, to [possibly: of] what you're meant to be. One thing that gets in the way of this habit of living in mystery — going back to silence and recollection — is what the author of The Soul of the Apostolate calls dissipation. We ought to have the custody of our hearts, to keep out those things that are going to distract us from God. Dissipations are rampant nowadays, because there are so many ways that we can satisfy a dopamine addiction. We can just go on to social media, get caught up in TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, and see what's on our social media feed.

You ever wonder why it's called a feed? What kind of creatures consume a feed? Animals — barnyard animals. Here is your feed.

We are human. We are not animals. We do not have feed. We have feasts. We are not meant to be reduced to that level. In the Greek myth of the Odyssey, Odysseus' men at one point were seduced by Circe, and Circe's kiss made them become pigs. Many of us living this life of dissipation have become like pigs. We're caught up in a feed. We doomscroll instead of wonder.

It's safe, though. It has its own sort of negative feedback, because I can do that until I'm tired enough to fall asleep. I do that until I can just get through these next ten minutes when I have nothing else to do. I will scroll in its nice negative feedback loop.

You're not called to that mediocrity. You're not called to be comfortable. As Pope Benedict has said, you are not made for comfort — you are made for greatness. This challenges us as engineering students. This challenges us as any kind of student, or any kind of person living in this age.

Dissipation is the enemy of recollection. Dissipation prevents us from experiencing reality as mystery. It gives us a false, counterfeit sense of control. And yet it controls us. We are enslaved. We are reduced. We have screens. Screens block out the mystery. The abstract veils — they clothe mystery; they accentuate the details of it; they show what's behind the veil. We do not look at the veils. We're stuck looking at screens. We are blocked. This is a great enemy of the interior life.

## Watching for the Christ Already Within

The interior life begins with this knowledge: that God is closer to us than we are to ourselves. And everything that is driving us to obstruct, to screen out, and to dissipate, does not need to be there, because we are already loved. God is actually our Father. He actually cares for us. He has sent Christ, his Son, so that all of us can be sons and daughters in Christ the Son — so we can be joined with him in his offering of himself to God the Father — so we can be ourselves teeming with life eternal, even here and now. This interior life happens in our interiority, in the recognition that God is already within us.

One of the attitudes we have to have as Christians always — it comes up every year in Advent — is an attitude of watching, of waiting for the coming of Christ. So we watch for him. And St. John Henry Newman — himself an Oratorian priest, like myself — said that watching is that feeling you have when you're expecting a friend to come to your house, and for some reason he's delayed, but all of your thoughts are fixed on him, and you're just waiting for him to be there, and you're desiring him to come. That is this attitude of watching.

But I would say that it made deeper sense. Maybe it's more analogous to — well, we're waiting for our friend to show up, and then we come back to our house, and we find that he's already there. His car is parked in the driveway. And we go inside, and he's already sitting on our couch.

What do you say to such a friend? Welcome. I see you made yourself at home.

Christ is already dwelling within us. Welcome him. Speak to him. Be silent with him. Gaze on his face with wonder. Look into his eyes. See how he looks at you. He cares for you. And your sufferings are not far from his sufferings. For his mysteries are your mysteries. Your sufferings are his sufferings, and there is no suffering, no wound, that you have undergone that he has not taken on himself. He has loved you so much. He's sorry when you feel like you can't trust anyone. He's sorry when you feel like you have to aim for counterfeit things. But he just invites you. The interior life is a response to the invitation that Christ constantly gives us.

## Rest

"Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest."

This is where the book ends — not with another thing to optimize, but with an invitation. It is the same rest we met in the chapter on intimacy: Christ sleeps in the boat even as the storm rages, and he is already home.
