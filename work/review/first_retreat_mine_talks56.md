**T5-S10. The carpet: two kinds of knowledge made concrete.**
*Insertion:* C04 — §"Two Kinds of Knowledge" (carpet + persons passages as enrichment; the framework itself is a duplicate, see §2).
> There are really two different kinds of knowledge that we could have. **T6-S15. The awkward question — take ownership of the Trinity in evangelization.**
*Insertion:* C06 — new section between "Back to St. Patrick" and "Christ in the poor" (suggested heading: "Owning the mystery").
> We think about evangelizing those that do not yet know Christianity, or do not know it well. We run into an awkward question. We want to tell them about Jesus. We want to tell them that Jesus died for their sins — that he shares with them their sufferings, though they know it not — that he is closer to them than they are to themselves, that they are meant to be included in the love of his Sacred Heart, and that he not only died for us, but he rose from the dead, and he ascended to the Father. And this is all well and good, but to anyone hearing this, this is a problem. For — well, you're saying Jesus is God? Like, yes. But doesn't Jesus have a God? Is he just the Son of God? Are there two gods? What's going on with that? We run into this problem of the Trinity right away — anytime we try to evangelize, and catechize as part of that evangelization. And it is an awkward thing. Do we try to get around that? Do we insist there's one God and just say, "Don't worry about that — it's not that important"? That there are actually two persons here going on — actually three persons, we've got the Holy Spirit in there too — because two is awkward, but three — it kind of seems more like we're owning it — but "don't worry about it, it's a mystery"? We run into big problems, because we're trying to spread the faith, but when we try to spread the faith, we hit this problem of the Trinity pretty much early on. Some from the outside might say, "How is this altogether different from those people that believe in multiple gods? It seems that you want to have your cake and eat it too." Or maybe, "How is this different from Mormonism? For don't they have one big God, and Jesus is the God of this planet? And if we are good Mormons, then we become gods of our own planets after we die. How is this different? For don't you, as Christians, believe that you will become like God? Did not one of your early Christian saints — Saint [Irenaeus — ASR "Inaus"; audio check: the maxim is classically Athanasius, De Incarnatione 54] — say that God became man, so that man might become God?" Well, this is confusing. What do we do with this? I think we need to take ownership of it. I think we need to acknowledge that the Trinitarian mystery of God is something that now actually gives us greater access to God. God is not just so far from us. He is close to us. He is not just transcendent, far away. He is immanent — close to us, within us, the most within us. He is intimate with us.

**T6-S17. Teach vs. live — us-and-them, "the unity of everyone minus one", blessed when hated.**
*Insertion:* C06 — new section before or merged with "Tell everyone" (suggested heading: "Living it, not just teaching it").
> In fact, not just how we teach, but how we live, needs to be necessarily relational. We think of it mostly in terms of teaching, and we think there is right teaching and there's wrong teaching. And those that espouse right teaching — we are in that group. We are in that same tribe. That's where we're comfortable. That's where we're supported. **T6-S18. Missa est extras — priests misreading Latin; the Mass as going-out-and-return; the doxology; source and summit.**
*Insertion:* C06 — §"Missa est: 'it has been sent'".
> In Latin, "Ite" means go, and "missa est" — "missa est," in Latin, it means it has been sent. Some priests will explain this, not understanding Latin, that "missa est" means "be sent" — "go and be sent, be sent, go out into the world, be sent into the world." And it's like — well, no, that's not actually what it means. The "go" means go. And "be sent" — that's the going out, that's the "go." "Missa" means it has been sent. What has been sent? We look at what it is actually referring to. [unclear: "What is that preposition referring to?"] The offering of Christ to the Father. He has been sent — it has been sent to the Father. The Mass is completed. The Mass is not just that Jesus comes down to us in the Eucharist. The Mass is that Jesus comes, and he goes — he goes out from the Father down to us, and he goes back to the Father. What he does in salvation — in becoming incarnate and dying and rising and ascending — he does in the Mass. He has been sent back to the Father. We are to pattern our going forth, then, after what Christ does in the Mass — which is not just a mystery about the second person of the Holy Trinity; it's all three persons. It's through Christ, with Christ, in Christ, to God the almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, to whom all praise and glory belongs forever and ever. That mystery is meant to be lived by us now, and going forth. Mass is not a one-time thing. It's not something that we merely do for half an hour to 45 minutes a day and just have that be that. It is how we live our lives. The source and the summit of our lives — it's also how we live our evangelization.
