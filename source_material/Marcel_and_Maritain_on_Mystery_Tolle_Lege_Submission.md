 Marcel and Maritain on Mystery
	Analytic philosophy generally approaches philosophy as a means of slow incremental progress in advancing solutions upon which all can agree. Taking root in the early twentieth century in the English-speaking world, this school of philosophy generally attaches little value to metaphysics and often doubts the logical necessity of being. They aim for a scientific certainty built on measurable results which should yield consensus among different philosophers. Today, they are splintered and fragmented but still retain a rigorous scientific approach to philosophical problems which can come across as cold and uninteresting. 
Partly in response to this growing analytic trend, two French Catholic philosophers from different backgrounds, Gabriel Marcel and Jacques Maritain, put forward two different conceptions of mystery in the middle of the twentieth century. For Marcel, a mystery implicates and encompasses the subject; for Maritain, a mystery involves an overpowering abundance of intelligibility. Marcel, preferring to avoid the limitations of particular systems of philosophy, emphasizes the importance of self-knowledge through a type of participation in being, whereas Maritain, operating from a fundamentally Thomistic framework, places a greater emphasis on conceptual knowledge in understanding being. Even with contrasting approaches, their explanations of the means of encountering mystery are more similar than conflicting, and they ultimately converge on an identical diagnosis of the difficulties associated with analytic philosophy as infringements of problem into mystery. Where the two philosophers meet on mystery, they form a rich starting point for the engagement of the mystery of God.
	For Marcel, a problem is something that “is placed in front of me, blocking my way.” A problem deals with something outside of oneself: one cannot be part of a problem. There are theoretical problems, arising from various techniques; and technical problems, which “can not be solved without a measure of pre-established theoretical knowledge.” These problems collapse on each other in a continuous cycle, never to be truly solved by technical means. In a world of the problematic, the best we can hope for is a clarification of the various functions and forcing them to work together safely. One studies the problematic through what Marcel calls ‘primary reflection,’ which engages and solves problems on the conceptual level. Primary reflection “explains, orders, clarifies, relates, abstracts, and objectifies. This is the level of science, of objectivization, of causality, of a subject-object dichotomy, of bound-ness to finitude.” Primary reflection has its proper role in the world of the problematic, and there are serious repercussions when it is wrongly applied to matters beyond those boundaries.
	Marcel takes issue with the prevalent “spirit of abstraction,” which attempts to engage the most pressing philosophical dilemmas as problems. The spirit of abstraction is the “contempt for the concrete conditions of abstract thinking,” being “profoundly linked to a tendency to conceptual dissociation.” In the spirit of abstraction, people are stereotyped and pigeonholed into particular categories, in order to “lose all awareness of the individual reality of the being whom I may be led to destroy.” In this way, an enemy is reduced to an impersonal abstraction. This type of derogatory reduction “has its basis in resentment, that is to say, in passion, and at bottom it corresponds to a violent attack directed against a sort of integrity of the real.” Marcel identifies the de-personalizing tendency at the heart of the world wars of his time as the result of treating people as problems, disconnecting their personhood from their conceptual abstraction through a stubborn persistence in the methods of the problematic. As long as we maintain a faith solely in techniques, we cannot penetrate the mystery of being, and we risk becoming slaves to the world of the problematic.
	Marcel prescribes the engagement with mystery as a real solution to the spirit of abstraction. Beyond the tendency to conceptual reduction, the ontological need of the human person cannot be adequately explained in terms of functions or reduced to something external. ‘Being’ itself is a mystery, and “a mystery is a problem which encroaches upon its own data, invading them, as it were, and thereby transcending itself as a simple problem.” Mysteries involve the subject in the object and “can be neither analysed nor reconstituted out of precedent elements.” Any attempt to approach mystery conceptually will inevitably degrade the mystery into a problem, creating a division between the subject and the object; thus, a mystery cannot be reduced to an abstract concept. Coextensive with the metatechnical, mystery cannot be accessed by techniques. Mystery can rather only be accessed by abandoning the particulars of one’s experiences and entering fully into the depths of oneself. One arrives at the necessary detachment from experience through recollection, which is “the act whereby I re-collect myself as a unity.” 
According to Marcel, one can only apprehend the mystery of being through recollection. Recollection of the ontological mystery entails a real separation of one’s life from one’s being, such that “the paradox of that actual mystery whereby the I into which I withdraw ceases, for as much, to belong to itself.” Thus, recollection entails that the subject loses himself to the mystery at hand. Insofar as recollection is self-conscious, it is what Marcel calls ‘secondary reflection,’ in which “I ask myself how and from what starting point I was able to proceed in my initial reflection, which itself postulated the ontological, but without knowing it.” By reflecting upon oneself and one’s primary reflections, one draws attention to the realm of being initially postulated in primary reflection, albeit unknowingly. In secondary reflection, one can either smother one’s own ontological need through despair, betrayal, or suicide, or one can cling to being through hope. In the face of dismal probabilities, hope prophetically asserts that reality must be on one’s side, that one is not alone in willing the good. In the secondary reflection of hope, one thus accesses the mystery of being. As a precursor to hope, despair acts as “the springboard to the loftiest affirmation,” recognizing the insufficiency of the world of the problematic; hope asserts that there is something more, breaking out of the prison of abstractions and entering into the world of mystery. 
The mystery of love further exemplifies how mystery is above and beyond the problematic. One can try to reduce love to the problematical by explaining it by means of criteria or abstract concepts. For the one who loves, however, those abstract concepts are meaningless; what matters is to be absorbed in love. Love grips at the deepest level of one’s being, since one is implicated in the mystery of love. This self-implication is not merely the content of thought, “for content is, when all is said and done, derived from experience; whereas, it is only by a way of liberation and detachment from experience that we can possibly rise to the level of the meta-problematical and of mystery.” In love, one is detached from self-focused desires and fears and caught up in wonder of the other. 
	Marcel cites Maritain as a significant influence in his thinking on mystery, saying that it was through him “that I was led to make the fundamental distinction” between problem and mystery, and introducing him to the “mystery of knowledge which is of the ontological order.” Despite serving as Marcel’s original inspiration, Maritain approaches mystery quite differently than Marcel, although ironically adopting his terminology: “making use of terminology borrowed from a contemporary French philosopher, M. Gabriel Marcel, though I am employing it in a completely different sense, we may say that every scientific question presents a double aspect, the one a Mystery, the other a Problem.” Marcel would not have mystery and problem intermingle in a single object in the way that Maritain presents it; from the outset, Maritain expresses an alternative conception of mystery. 
	For Maritain, every scientific question “is a mystery and at the same time a problem, a mystery in regard to the thing, the object as it exists outside the mind, a problem in regard to our formulae.” A mystery is not completely knowable, either because it is too actual and “pregnant with intelligibility,” or not actual enough. The latter includes things like “becoming, potency and above all matter” and more or less corresponds to the common use of mystery as unknowable as a result of missing information. The former type of mystery cannot be wholly known due to an abundance of intelligibility, analogous to the sun which is so bright it cannot be seen. Reality belongs to this type of mystery. Rather than stressing the involvement of the subject in mystery, Maritain allows for the reduction of mystery in the act of understanding: “the objectivity of the understanding is itself supremely mysterious and the object of knowledge is ‘Mystery’ reduced to a state of intelligibility in act and of intellection in act.” The object of understanding is not the concept itself, but being; the intellect is not cut off from mysteries but actually “becomes what is other than itself.” Unlike Marcel’s view, the concept is merely that by which being is known; it does not impede access but is in fact necessary for the intellect to infiltrate the mystery of being. Marcel associates the concept with what is merely notional and divorced from the real, whereas Maritain sees the concept as integral to the perception of reality. For this reason, Maritain uses concept differently than Marcel, applying it to both mystery and problem.
	A problem for Maritain is a logical difficulty that has no ontological content: “the ‘problem’ may be described as a notional complex created by our intellect, which at first appears inextricable and which must be disentangled.” Unlike Marcel’s definition, this definition of problem automatically excludes any intrusion of being, and a pure problem is hard to come by “because there is always some degree of being.” Even a mathematical equation, a prime example of a problem for both philosophers, contains some being according to Maritain. Since both the problem and mystery are entangled in every act of the intellect, we can identify each one based on whether mystery or problem predominates. Where problem predominates, when “knowledge is least ontological,” one progresses in understanding by substituting fresh solutions for incomplete solutions, mentally moving rectilinearly to solve the problem. Where mystery predominates, “when knowledge is most ontological,” one progresses in understanding by a deepening intensity in a single approach, assaulting “the same object, with increasing force and penetration.” This deepening assault is what Maritain elsewhere calls the eidetic intuition of being, in which “concept and judgment unite essence to existence in the mind’s primordial affirmation of the real.” Eidetic intuition relies on a previous intuition that is prior to the concept; this prior intuition of being is “an intense and highly personal experience that Maritain compared to an intellectual shock.” Without this personal experience of being, a person cannot do metaphysics and, thus, cannot engage the ontological mystery. 
	Maritain addresses the issue of the modern misunderstanding of mystery through the prism of three intellectual thirsts: the thirst for science, the thirst for created wisdom, and the thirst for uncreated wisdom. The intellectual thirst for science belongs to the world of the problematic; the latter two belong to mystery. The thirst for created wisdom corresponds to philosophy and is simultaneously quenched and increased in the knowledge of the ontological mystery; the thirst for uncreated wisdom is the desire for God and is completely satisfied in the beatific vision. For Maritain, there is disorder when people start “treating the things of eternal life, the vision of God, as an object of the first thirst” such as when philosophers treat eternal beatitude as a source of boredom or an infinite array of passing pleasures. We also find radical disorder when people treat “philosophy, metaphysics, wisdom – a category of knowledge in which reverence for the mystery of being is the highest factor – as an object of the first thirst.” The thirst for the ontological mystery cannot be quenched in the rectilinear progress of mere scientific thought, for “its progress is progress by an adhesion of the mind to its object and a union with it increasingly profound, progress as it were by a growing intimacy.” Such attempts to treat philosophy as a series of solutions for problems unmoor mystery from being, and, according to Maritain, “a philosophy unaware of mystery would not be a philosophy.” Thus, both Maritain and Marcel would concede that the strictly analytic approach to philosophy is a confusion of the more ontological with the less ontological.
	Thus, Maritain and Marcel arrive at the same issue: the dissolution of philosophy in the modern world through the neglect and denial of mystery. Marcel and Maritain vary in their understandings of problem and definitions of mystery, but ultimately fall on the same side of the divide against the prevailing philosophies of their day. They diverge most in their approaches. Marcel, as a phenomenological existentialist, stresses personal implication, and Maritain, as an existential Thomist, stresses the formal unity of the knower and the known. In the end, the difference between Maritain and Marcel amounts to their different starting points on this particular point. Despite their different philosophical starting points, Maritain’s mystery of super-abundant intelligibility converges with Marcel’s mystery of personal implication, since the super-abundant necessarily implicates the self. 
To illustrate the benefit of these overlapping conceptions, I offer the following illuminating discussion. Once, my five-year old niece attempted to explain to me, “Did you know that, when I go places, the moon follows me? It really does!” Notwithstanding her eyes being filled with wonder, my immediate response was to try to explain to her that since the moon is so far away, it only looks like it is following her. Before I could explain my idea for an experiment that would disprove her childish notion, she interrupted, “No, the moon really follows me.” With such opposition, I thus abandoned my attempt to scientifically disprove her childish perception. For my five-year old niece, the moon was a mystery; it really did follow her. The moon was so great in its size and distance from her that, rather than disconnecting her, it implicated her in its path. However mistaken her understanding of perspective, she approached it with wonder, and would not let that wonder be extinguished. To me, the moon was a problem. It could be measured and placed conceptually at a distance. I knew that its movements and phases are ordered to a different pattern than my sporadic movements. Although technically more correct, my problematic approach prevented me from being gripped by the mystery of the moon and sharing in my niece’s wonder.
	In a greater way, intellectually engaging God merely as a distant problem suffers from similar difficulties. When we speak of God, especially in the context of the Church’s liturgy, we tend to use adjectives that highlight God’s otherness from creation: words like “ineffable,” “infinite,” or “almighty.” These adjectives highlight God’s otherness from us, but also seem to imply an expanse which can only be crossed with fear and trembling. Likewise, the “God of the philosophers” as First Cause and Unmoved Mover do not immediately connote a God who is intimately involved with creation. However, God’s greatness and transcendence need not preclude God’s nearness and immanence. God’s super-abundance does not demand that we give up knowing Him nor does it demand that we set Him at a distance and explain Him away. In approaching of the mystery of God with wonder, we immerse ourselves with the God who is so large and so other from created things that He has room for us. Our mere existence shares in God’s being and the very fact of our creatureliness points to the Creator. Even more could be said of the image and likeness that we share with Him. However, more than mere proof of His closeness, God entered into His creation in the Incarnation. Paradoxically holding both approaches to mystery in tension, we can approach God who transcends our limited comprehension but in whom we ourselves are implicated. In mystery, transcendence and immanence meet; God is so beyond our finite grasp that He encompasses us in His infinite embrace.
