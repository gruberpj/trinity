# Chapter 5 — Intimacy

> *Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new! too late I loved Thee! And behold, Thou wert within, and I abroad, and there I searched for Thee; deformed I, plunging amid those fair forms which Thou hadst made. Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee. Things held me far from Thee, which, unless they were in Thee, were not at all. Thou calledst, and shoutedst, and burstest my deafness. Thou flashedst, shonest, and scatteredst my blindness. Thou breathedst odours, and I drew in breath and panted for Thee. I tasted, and hunger and thirst. Thou touchedst me, and I burned for Thy peace.*

— St. Augustine, Confessions X.27, trans. E. B. Pusey

## Being itself

In theology, there is a central concept in talking about God. It is called the analogy of being. There are many who have ideas of God as a being above other beings. Many Protestants would see God as the greatest of all beings, and because he is the greatest — in fact, he is infinite, he is omnibenevolent, he is omniscient, he is omnipotent; he is all-powerful, he is all-knowing, he is all-loving — and therefore he, as the highest of all beings, gets all of our praise and worship.

For many of us, this might be our default conception of how God works. God is just simply the highest of all beings. We are lesser beings. We are creatures made by God, but God just stands above and beyond all these things. And to that, we can add his eternality. He is, before time existed, he is, and he always will be. There is no change in God.

And that is God.

And, actually, there have been Protestant theologians who have argued more or less for that conception of God.

We might be thinking, that is not that wrong, right? That is a fairly normal conception of God. God is just really great.

But that is not our Catholic conception of God. God is not the highest of all beings. God is Being, with a capital B. We are beings with a lowercase b. We just participate in him. We have being. He is being.

We recall the story of Moses with the burning bush. Moses, wandering in the desert and tending the flock of his father-in-law Jethro, sees on Mount Sinai this bush burning but not being consumed. He goes to the bush, and the bush calls out to him, asking Moses to ask Pharaoh to let his people go. And Moses is not sure about it, but the burning bush — God, from that burning bush, speaks — and Moses is going to listen. But before he leaves, he says, "I need to know your name. So when others ask, 'Who sent you?' I can tell them who it was that sent me." And God, from the burning bush, says, "I AM WHO I AM." Maybe this is God just saying, "I am who I am. Don't worry about it. Just say, 'I am' sent you." Perhaps that was the original context, the original meaning. But "I AM WHO I AM" — we can read back into this and say, God is saying, "I am existence itself. I am being itself." This is Exodus 3:14. Philosophers will say this means that God's essence is his existence. That is not necessarily what is being communicated to Moses — but it fits, because he does not have, he is. And from that we get the name of God. It was "I am."

Our Lord reiterates this. He has many "I am" statements, especially within the Gospel of John. "I am the door. I am the vine. I am the way, and the truth, and the life." It was on purpose that our Lord said "I am," because this harkens back to the name of God.

What does it mean for God to be existence? This means that God does not have anything. While we say he has, he is. To have means you might not have. To have maybe implies there is something apart from you that is not somehow connected to you. But God does not have, he is. God does not have being. He is being. God does not have love. He is love. God does not have goodness. He is goodness. Whatever he has, he is.

This fundamentally changes how we look at God, then, how we look at ourselves. We have goodness. God has given it to us. But we are not goodness itself. We have truth. We can actually ascertain truth with our intellects. We are made for truth. But we are not truth itself. God is.

We, as creatures of God, participate in his being. I mentioned before that everything is gift, everything is positive, everything speaks of God. Even Satan himself, even the one who has rejected God from the first moment of his being and turned his whole self away from God — he still exists. Therefore, he still shares in God. And therefore, he still has goodness. Now, it is a goodness of existence, maybe only existence, but he still has existence.

You might ask, what does it mean that we are created out of nothing — ex nihilo? How are we not God? Because we are not God. Because — this is the key thing — God is necessary. He is existence. God's essence — who he is — and his existence are the same. We have existence. We are not existence. That is a lot to wrap our minds around. But because we participate in God — that is why we are not God. The fact that we were not around from all eternity is one way of saying that we could not be God.

And it is somewhat misleading, then, to say God is creating out of nothing. He is creating out of the abundance of love in the Blessed Trinity. Is creation out of nothing? No — there was nothing, and then there was something. But that comes from God. It is almost like God is saying, "I'm going to take nothing and add something to it." And from the outside it looks that way, but it is more from God. He wanted to create us. So he did.

But then, it is not like pantheism, right — no, we all participate in God. Everything participates in God. You might think objects do not. Yes, they do. By merely existing, it does. But you participate not just by existing, but also by your free choice to cooperate and to respond to God — and that is a participation a little bit higher.

So if you sin, you are existing less? Yes, that is fine. You could say that. And do not exist less. Do not sin. You are existing poorly.

We have goodness, and we also share in God, especially through the gift of his grace. We are made in his image and likeness, so we have a higher share than other things. Protestants would say, "Well, maybe there's a hierarchy of being. God's the highest kind of being." But we say, no, God is being itself, and everything else shares in his being. And that is why we call it the analogy of being, because everything that has being harkens back to God, who is being. "I AM WHO I AM."

## The fifth transcendental

Joseph Ratzinger, in *Introduction to Christianity*, made an interesting claim. We are familiar maybe with what we call the transcendentals: truth, beauty, goodness, unity — those four transcendentals. Unity — oneness — that is the original transcendental. God is one. We see that oneness is in a communion of three persons. Transcendentals are something that is in God. It is a characteristic of being that God has in himself and that everything else shares in, in some way. Joseph Ratzinger puts forward the idea that there is a fifth transcendental, a fifth characteristic that, because we know it is in God, it also belongs to all being. What is that fifth transcendental, that characteristic that is in God? We know of it only because God has revealed himself in his inner life of the Trinity. That fifth characteristic is relationality. All beings are relational.

And we share in that relationality because we are capable of relationship. We are capable of giving of ourselves and receiving from another under different aspects and different ways, but we give. We do not just communicate words or concepts. We communicate our very selves through those words and through those concepts, through those symbols, through our actions.

We recall that God made us in his image and likeness — male and female he created us. To talk about the theology of the body means to talk about relationship, gift, a response. We are capable of receiving another person in their wholeness and giving back of ourselves in our wholeness, as gifts — not holding on to it with strings, pulling the gift back whenever it is no longer to our advantage to give. But when we give, we give totally, and we give freely, and we give fruitfully. This is present in every relationship.

No animals — besides human beings, the rational, worded animals — can love. We can truly love. We are truly capable of self-gift, of pouring ourselves out in our entirety for another, under different aspects.

Because God is relational, because God is being itself, because what he has, he is. We would not say God has knowledge. We have intellect and will, right? We are able to know and we are able to love. We are able to consume truth in our intellects, and we are able to reach out towards others in love. God has knowledge and love. But remember, God does not merely have. What we say God has, God is. God's knowledge is the Son. The Father knows the Son. He is the Word of the Father, spoken from all eternity. And God's love — God's love is the Holy Spirit, the breathing forth of the Father and the Son.

Knowledge and love are in the Trinity as subsistent relations. That is how we would say it theologically, because what God has, he is. And he is always knowing, and he is always loving.

And there is a going out with the knowledge. Then there is a fittingness with how the Son, the Word, only begotten Son of God the Father from all eternity, goes out into creation. God had intended to enter creation through the second person of the Holy Trinity all throughout, and to draw all things through Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity, in the Holy Spirit, back to God the Father. He planned this. For he came down out of love, in a way that fit with who he was in himself. And the Holy Spirit comes down as well, drawing us all together in one spirit, in one body, because what God has, he is.

This is the most simple explanation of God. We look for how we are made in the image and likeness of God, because we are relational, because we are able to know and to love — not know merely in a divisive way, through sciences and different ologies, but we are able to know each other.

So that means all reality, then, has a share not only in truth, goodness, and beauty, but also relationality. And that makes sense, because we can only speak of a water bottle in relation to my ability to drink, my ability to have that, and I get to consume it. There is a relationality with all material things. Even the things that we do not yet see — the tree that falls in the forest with no one around — there is still relationality built into all of these things: that coming together of one thing and another thing. There is relationality built into all material creation, all immaterial creation. All things share in this relationality. This also means that there is no escaping God — yes.

God is in us. In him we move. "We live and move and have our being," St. Paul says. This means, because of the analogy of being, that we cannot escape God. There is no hiding from God. For Adam and Eve, there was no hiding in the Garden of Eden. For us, there is no hiding. We cannot go anywhere to escape him. But also, why would we want to?

And that works the other way around. Even every thought that we have is known to God. God the Father knows the number of hairs on your head intimately. He knows you. Everything that goes on — every electrical signal discharged in your brain — he knows it. Because everything participates in him. We cannot escape him. God is everything for us. We live in God.

## Intimus: the most in

St. Augustine says God is closer to us than we are to ourselves. We are mysteries unto ourselves. We do not know ourselves fully. When we know ourselves and we know God, and we see ourselves as we truly are — the mystery of our worth and our unworthiness — then there is true humility.

This means that we think we know ourselves to some extent, but there are parts of us that are hidden from us. There are things about us that we do not engage with very often. God knows everything. I am a participating being in God, but God is already closer to me. Not only is there relationality in how I live out my life — and there is a relationality between my right index finger and my left pinky toe — but there is relationality between God and me. He is closer to me than I am even to myself.

Do you know what the word *intimacy* means and where it comes from? There is a popular idea that intimacy means "into me seeing" — Christopher West says he once heard it said. And that is a helpful thing. It is wrong. It is a *false etymology*. And it is never meant to be the actual thing that it means, but it is a helpful way to understand into-me-see, intimacy. And we can see how Adam knew Eve, and the biblical idea of knowing, and that Eve conceived and brought forth the son. That is an intimacy. From the intimacy of husband and wife, children come about. And so into-me-see: you might see who I really am. You might see me in my vulnerability. You might see my shame. You might see who I truly am. I might be affirmed at the level of my being. And there is a true intimacy when that is mutual, to see into each other. And there is a great truth to this. Into-me-see is a helpful way to understand intimacy. But that is not where we get the word *intimacy*. It is actually far more simple. In Latin, we have the word *in*, and then the word *interior*, which means "more in" — which we say in English "interior." That is the comparative of the word *in*. And then the superlative, *intimus* — *intimus* — which means "most in." From the word *intimus*, we derive the word *intimacy*.

God is intimate with us. He is the most in. He is closer to us than we are to ourselves.

Contemplate him in the holy Eucharist, and we become like him — for we shall see him as he really is. We become like God, and our knowledge of God is not merely from study — sometimes aided by our study — but it becomes the likeness of God on our souls. We who live godly lives know God from the inside — not from the outside in, but from the inside out.

And the saints who have been purified through their long periods of suffering — God will sometimes grant to them particular blessings of knowing him in ways that cannot be communicated. *Fire Within* is a great book by Thomas Dubay, discussing the advancement in the spiritual life from a beginner — where a lot of us are — to ultimately being united with God. In that state of being united with God, there is a likeness in our soul. It can maybe be somewhat communicated poetically. John of the Cross, who lived much of his life in the stage of unitive wholeness with God, would communicate to us through poems. The poetic imagination works in this sort of wholeness — not grasping after the small details, deciphered and put together, but the whole of the thing. Mystical knowledge works in this way, and our knowledge of God will work in this way as well, even if we are merely in the beginner stage.

## The friend already home

St. John Henry Newman, reflecting on Advent and this idea of waiting for Christ, gave the analogy to explain what he means by waiting or watching. It is about when you are waiting for a friend to arrive, and all of your thoughts and all your heart is fixed on that friend, and you have everything ready in your house, everything is prepared for his coming, and now you are waiting, and maybe he is delayed. But that does not mean you are distracted and go somewhere else. Your whole being is fixed on waiting for the coming of this friend.

And this is the attitude all of us should have in Advent, of course, but in all times. We are waiting for Christ. We are waiting for him to come again. He who is coming, always coming into this world, who has come in the incarnation, who is with us always, even at the close of the age, who is with us in the Eucharist, who is with us when two or three are gathered in his name. We are always waiting for him. He is always coming.

But to maybe alter the analogy a little bit: if you are waiting for a friend, and perhaps you do get distracted, or perhaps you need to attend to some errand, or maybe because you are preparing for his coming, you go out and you go to Giant Eagle to pick up some groceries or things there that are needed for his visit. But when you come back to your house, you are horrified. There is your friend's car parked in your driveway. You go to the front door. You go in, and you see your friend already sitting on your couch.

What would you say to such a friend? "How the heck did you get in? You are no longer my friend. You violated my privacy. You came when I was gone. That is not okay. Hey, you should wait in your car until I should have gotten back."

Or a true friend — if we have been waiting for him — we would say to him, "Welcome. I'm glad you made yourself at home."

This is more fitting with how God comes to us in intimacy.

When we think about the closeness of God to us — God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, all three already dwell within us. The Holy Spirit dwells within — we are a temple of the Holy Spirit. The Father hears us from our inner room. Christ's Sacred Heart beats with our hearts. God is closer to us than we are to ourselves.

In the incarnation and our configuration to Christ, we have an entrance into the love of Father, Son, Holy Spirit. And we have access now to that love within us. He has made a home within us.

## Christ resting in the storm

We look for him in created things. We try to find God and beauty that is derivative, that shares in God's beauty, yes, but is not beauty, capital B, itself. Meanwhile, he was within us, always closer to us than we are to ourselves.

St. Augustine said that when Christ ascended into heaven, he ascended from the apostles' eyes so they might turn towards their hearts and find him there, because within us, God rests.

We might think it is very hard for God to rest in my soul, because it seems my experience has told me otherwise. In my soul, there is little rest. There is constant anxiety. There is constant fear, maybe even anger. There is shame, there is guilt. How could God be there already?

And maybe mostly for our benefit — the one time that the apostles were all together on the boat, and there was a great storm that came upon them, and these experienced fishermen were afraid for their lives. They who had already weathered many storms — now, this was the big one. This was going to claim their lives. But where is Christ in this? He is there on a cushion, sleeping. How is it that when the boat is being rocked about, water is entering in, there is danger of being capsized, or the ship breaking under the waves, Christ is there, sleeping? Christ rests in our souls. And he likes to rest in our souls when there is the most amount of chaos.

He is there, always, sustaining us in being. "Each one of us is the result of a thought of God," Pope Benedict said. If God ever ceased to think about us, we would not exist. We would have no past, no present, no future. He who is being itself is always holding on to us, loving us. And in us, in our souls, in our hearts, he is resting.

God is with us. Jesus is Emmanuel. God with us. And we still want to say to him, "Teacher, do you not care if we perish?" Like we would want to say to the friend who made himself at home, "Do you not care that I was stressed about many things?"

And even in every kind of consternation, every stress, every anxiety, every time we move to anger or resentment, every wound that we feel — the Holy Spirit, Christ the Son, the Father — they are all resting within us, like Christ on the boat in the Sea of Galilee, sleeping on a cushion in the midst of a storm. So God rests in us. He waits for us patiently, for we are his. We already live and move and have our being in him. He desires us for himself. He wants us to be made more and more like him. He waits for our silent adoration. He waits for us to just be with him — to let us know that we are known, that we are loved, that he is drawing us to himself in every time and in every place. God draws close to us. Let God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — love you from the inside out.

## Martha, Mary, and the one thing

We want to be like Martha. "Lord, I'm anxious and troubled by many things. I'm trying to do all this work, and my sister is there just resting with you. Tell her to help me with the work." And our Lord says, "No, Martha, you are anxious and troubled by many things, but one thing is necessary. And Mary has chosen the better part."

Mary, the sister of Martha, has been resting with me. She has been listening to me in the chaos of this home. She is giving me the greatest hospitality, like Abraham and Sarah, but even more so. Yes, I care for you, and I want you to have the same thing. I want you to have the better part, the one thing. And I want to tell you something about who I am. For God the Son reveals himself, and he speaks of the Father only, for he only has the Father to speak of, because everything he has comes from the Father. He is the Father's, and the Father is his. And then from their love comes the Holy Spirit. Martha, I want you to also know about who I am: that the one thing that is necessary — that one thing is three — three persons and one God.

And this God — who created in six days and rested on the seventh — desires to rest in you, in your hearts and your souls. Never mind the chaos. Never mind the storms. I am with you.

## The cross: the Trinity's embrace

What does Christ speak to us of the love of God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit? By entering into our sufferings in a way so obvious to us that we miss it every time we look upon a crucifix — that he who has shouldered our burdens and taken that cross upon himself suffered every one of our sufferings. He cries out, "Father, into your hands, I commit my spirit." And he sends forth his spirit. The cross is an image of the Trinity. The cross is the Trinity embracing us.

One of you, after my first talk, discussing the sign of the cross, said, "The sign of the cross is a hug from the Holy Trinity." In this way, God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, is closer to us than we are to ourselves. Let him love you. Let him embrace you. Let him hug you. And respond by welcoming him in your hearts and letting that love spread to others.
