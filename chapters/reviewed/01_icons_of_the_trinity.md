# Chapter 1 — Icons of the Trinity

> *I bind unto myself the name, the strong name of the Trinity, by invocation of the same, the three in one, and one in three, of whom all nature hath creation, eternal Father, Spirit, Word. Praise to the Lord of my salvation. Salvation is of Christ the Lord. Amen.*

— St. Patrick's Breastplate, trans. Cecil Frances Alexander

## St. Patrick and the Shamrock

St. Patrick evangelized Ireland in the fifth century. Years earlier, he had been enslaved there; during his captivity he learned the language and eventually escaped. Later he returned as a missionary to the same land where he had been previously held captive. There, he encountered a pagan culture infected by Druidism, which had its own religious customs and rituals, a reverence for the natural world, and strong beliefs concerning the spiritual powers within it. Having known the language, he was able to preach to the people of Ireland — and many miracles attested to the power of God with him.

When St. Patrick preached, he did not shy away from the central mystery of our faith, and that is the Trinity. Tradition has handed down to us the story of the shamrock. The shamrock was a botanical analogy used to illustrate to a people who may be caught up in the worship of nature how something can be both one and three, that it can share the same life, be connected in the same way, but yet be distinct at the same time. It was a simple analogy, fraught with potential heresy — but just as this is one shamrock with three leaves, so there are three persons in one God. The analogy limps as it does not capture the full reality of the Trinity. We must not take it to mean that God the Father is a part of God, and God the Son is a part of God, and God the Holy Spirit is a part of God. It is however a way to enter into the language of those people and to help them understand, with simplicity, what it means to be one and three. Let us recall how this analogy sufficed for us when we were children — how the shamrock showed we can have three in one. From that, we build on distinctions and understandings, and theology starts to do the work, notionally, of completing what is going on within the reality of this mystery.

St. Patrick led with the Trinity because it is what our Lord told us to lead with. "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go, therefore, make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:18-19). St. Patrick did not shy from holding up a shamrock and saying, "Three in one," to give their imaginations something to grip onto, to give an image that could later be corrected, modified, and through which they could enter into a theological understanding of the mystery at hand. He gave them something to begin the renewal of their minds.

## Another Icon of the Trinity

Christian art has generally been hesitant to depict the Trinity. We depict Christ, because “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). We can show what He looked like, and there is no affront there to any pious sentiments. To show the Father or the Holy Spirit under a personal form, however, is a challenge, both theologically and artistically.

Traditionally, an icon is said to be “written” rather than painted. The expression distinguishes an icon from ordinary religious artwork; like a sacred text, it bears witness through a language of received images, colors, and gestures.

Andrei Rublev wrote the icon of the Trinity, included on the cover of the book — I recommend consulting it frequently during the next few pages. Rublev circumvents the problem of depicting the Father and the Holy Spirit personally by showing, at face value, the Genesis account where three angelic visitors were sent from God to Abraham and Sarah. Out of an abundance of hospitality, they quickly prepared food for their guests. Abraham was under the Oak of Mamre when the angels communicated to him that Sarah was to conceive in her old age (Genesis 18:1-15).

The scene of these three angels, in retrospect, is a representation of the Trinity. Even here in the Old Testament, where this mystery was still hidden, we see resonances of God's Trinitarian nature. We might say the same of the first chapter of Genesis, when God says, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (Genesis 1:26). We who now know of God as one and three think that the "us," the plural, refers to the Trinity. Of course, we are not wrong. Originally, it acted as a plural of majesty or referred to the heavenly assembly of angels, but for us, we can retroactively understand that in its fullest sense, God creates from the Trinity.

## Reading the Icon: One Divinity

Rublev’s icon packs a lot of meaning, engaging our imagination in a way that is very helpful, especially for a mystery that often confounds us. I should say up front: iconographers and theologians do not all agree on every detail of this reading — which angel corresponds to which Person, for instance, has been argued over for centuries. What I am giving you is the reading I find most illuminating, but not the only one on record. Here I benefited greatly from the analysis of Fr. James Dominic Brent OP in his book The Father’s House.

Three angels sit around a table, each clothed in blue — barely visible beneath the garment of the figure on the left, prominent upon the central figure, and clearly displayed by the figure on the right. Blue, in the iconographic tradition, represents divinity. Its presence on all three therefore indicates their one shared divinity.

Likewise they all have the same face and no noticeable differences in their physical appearance. All three of them have a scepter in their left hands, while their right hands are free to bless or command. They all have the same power, same authority, same divinity. Together they form a circle, representing eternity.

## The Holy Spirit on the Right

Where we notice differences are in the colors that each one of them wears, starting with the angel on the right. That angel is wearing green over top of the blue, the blue tunic underneath. Green represents the new life. We call new tree growth green wood, or we might think of the greenness of late spring, when the world has once again come to life. Green in the eastern tradition is associated with the Holy Spirit, that breath of life that comes over the world every spring. In the East, they do not wear red for the Holy Spirit, for Pentecost — they wear green. So the angel on the right represents the Holy Spirit, and behind him in the distance is a mountain.

A mountain is associated with the Holy Spirit, especially in the Old Testament where we recall Moses on Mount Sinai or Elijah on Mount Carmel. Mountains symbolize the Holy Spirit and prophecy. In the New Testament, mountains maintain that significance. On Mount Tabor, Christ goes up with Peter, James, and John, and they were all enveloped in the cloud (Luke 9:28-36) — the cloud that symbolized the Holy Spirit — and then the Father's voice was heard. Our thoughts turn also to the mountain, or rather the hill, of Calvary, where Christ gave forth the Spirit. Behind the angel in green, the mountain gathers together associations of revelation, prophecy, and divine presence.

## The Son in the Middle

Under his blue garment, the central figure wears a robe whose color lies somewhere between brown, red, and purple. The earthy brown hue recalls the Word’s descent into our humanity; its red hue suggests the blood poured out for our salvation; its purple hue evokes his kingship, which is further attested to by the band of gold along his shoulder.

Looking at his right hand: two fingers are extended. This was a typical posture used when raising your hand in blessing in the East. The two fingers represented the humanity and divinity of Christ — what we call the hypostatic union, where God is united with us in our human nature: true God and true man in Christ, fully human and fully divine. The other three fingers represent the Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Behind him, there is the tree, the Oak of Mamre. We may also think back to the tree where original sin happened, when Eve plucked that fruit from the tree of knowledge of good and evil. By a tree, humanity fell. We also look forward to the tree of life that is the cross, when Christ goes up on that cross and dies for us. Christ is the new Adam, and he was the one to ascend a tree, to become the fruit of the tree of life on the cross. The tree corroborates the identification of the middle angel as the second person of the Holy Trinity.

## The Father on the Left

The angel on the left represents the Father. His outer garment resists simple identification of color. It appears by turns pink, purple, blue, and almost translucent. That elusiveness of color is fitting — the garment seems to withdraw from our attempts to name it, suggesting the mystery of the unseen Father.

Behind him is a house, the house of Abraham and Sarah. Also recall our Lord's words in John 14, "In my Father's house are many rooms" (John 14:2). Or we can go back to Luke 2, "Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house" (Luke 2:49), that is, the temple. In both sayings, the house signifies the dwelling of the Father. The house of Abraham and Sarah here recalls not only their hospitality, but the communion into which Christ welcomes us.

## The Postures and the Chalice

Let us examine more closely their postures. The Father’s head is up, but the other two are actually looking at the Father. This shows a deference to the Father, recognizing that the Son is begotten of the Father and the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, and they both go back to the Father and reverence the Father. Likewise for all of us, we are all from the Father’s love and tending to the Father in our own journeys of faith.

Not only are the heads of the Son and the Holy Spirit pointed towards the Father, but even the mountain and the tree are “bowing” towards the Father, so that the Father might be glorified in all things.

The contours of the two outer figures form the outline of a great chalice. In the middle of that visual chalice, we have Christ, whose blood is poured out for us.

The bowl upon the table deepens this Eucharistic meaning. In the bowl is the head of a calf. The bowl with its animal head recalls the covenant meals of the Old Testament and points forward to the New Covenant, in which we are invited to share in the heavenly banquet of the Eucharist. Its shape is actually not quite perfectly symmetrical, and that is likely on purpose. The incompletion of the sacrifices of the Old Testament points to the bread of the new covenant of the Eucharist.

## The Rectangle

Notice too the rectangle with a little inner rectangle on the front of this altar. Perhaps the rectangle represents the four corners of the world, that the Trinity is necessarily meant for evangelization, to spread the love of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, out of the four corners.

Speculatively, there is another meaning of that rectangle. In the altars of the East, there was a compartment in the altar where the relics of martyrs were placed. This is the part of the table that all three persons of the Trinity are not occupying, and we, as the viewer, are now part of this icon. We are invited to this feast, to share in the hospitality of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. How do we do so? The way in which we share in that hospitality is through martyrdom, through the witness of our lives. "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). Our insertion into the Holy Trinity through Christ is through a sharing in his sufferings and his death on the cross.

With our own prayer, I recommend taking this to the chapel — or your own inner room in your own time of silent recollection — to pray with Rublev’s icon.

## The Third Icon: The Sign of the Cross

Rublev’s icon stands before our eyes, the shamrock before our memory, but the final icon considered here is traced on our bodies: the sign of the cross.

As Catholics, we are very used to beginning and ending prayer with the sign of the cross, as a sort of toggle switch for prayer. Prayer does not “count” only when enclosed within the sign of the cross. Yet the Church’s custom of beginning and ending prayer in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit invites us to recover the meaning of words and gestures that familiarity can easily obscure.

## In the Name

The sign of the cross consists of words: "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Amen." Those words are first uttered over us — with water poured three times over our heads — when we are baptized. The Holy Trinity has a dwelling within us, and those particular words are important. Christ says "in the name," not "in the names.” Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are named distinctly, yet they possess one divine name. Even the grammar of the sign of the cross holds together the distinction of the Persons and the unity of God. In our baptisms, these are the words through which we become a “new creation” (2 Corinthians 5:17). When we pray, we invoke those same words — not sloppily, but with great intentionality. Every word matters — even the “and of the's!” It is good for us to pray with a reverence as we invoke the central mystery of our faith and our entrance into that mystery through baptism. In the name.

## Of the Father

Not only are the words important, the actions matter. When we lift our hands towards our foreheads, this upward motion of our hands goes along with "In the name of the Father." That upward motion is this pilgrimage all of us are making to the embrace of God the Father. We who have come forth from the Father are now meant to go towards him in our prayer. This is that simple glance directed towards heaven that St. Thérèse talks about when, in the words the Catechism quotes from her, "prayer is a surge of the heart; it is a simple look turned toward heaven, it is a cry of recognition and of love, embracing both trial and joy" (Catechism of the Catholic Church, no. 2558). Of the Father.

## And of the Son

The hand then descends, passing over the heart: "and of the Son." The downward movement recalls Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity, descending, going forth from the Father and entering into our humanity in the incarnation. He not only enters into our humanity, but takes all our sufferings onto himself, all of our misery, all of our sin — the descent of Christ into the depths of human misery. He undergoes this descent out of love for us. He binds us to himself so we might be lifted up to the Father. And of the Son.

## And of the Holy Spirit

The hand then crosses the body: "and of the Holy Spirit." In the Western tradition, we move from left shoulder to right shoulder. In the East, they go from right shoulder to left shoulder, because, as one tradition explains, the good thief was on the right. In either tradition, the accompanying motion emphasizes that the Holy Spirit is now among us. This is the Holy Spirit that has been sent among us, poured out for the forgiveness of our sins (John 20:22-23). We are living in the age of the Holy Spirit among us, and we cross over our heart, going from shoulder to shoulder. As we cross our hearts, we cross our lungs, for the Holy Spirit is our breath, and we, as members of Christ's body, the Church, are animated by that Spirit. By the mystery of the pouring forth of the Holy Spirit — at the crucifixion, at the Resurrection, in the upper room, and on the feast of Pentecost — that Holy Spirit is among us, animating us. We live and move and have our being in God (Acts 17:28), with, through, and in the Holy Spirit. Therefore, we are to live morally, to live in God's grace, to live the life of the Spirit, as we love God and love one another. And of the Holy Spirit.

## Amen

The last word of the sign of the cross is no less important. Amen. The word comes from a Hebrew root associated with firmness, faithfulness, security, and trust. It is like a peg driven into secure ground, holding fast whatever has been entrusted to it. I imagine the altogether common experience of a dad tightening a ratchet strap, giving a firm tug, and declaring, "That’s not going anywhere." In saying Amen, we give our assent to something solid enough to bear the weight of our lives. We affirm: this is true; this is firm and trustworthy; let it be so. Our Amen is a whole gift of self, leaving nothing behind. Our prayer begins and ends with the sign of the cross because our whole life is enclosed within this mystery of the Holy Trinity, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.

## Who Are We Crossing?

Let us not forget the central image, the central icon of the sign of the cross. Who are we crossing? Ourselves. We are images of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We are made in his image and likeness. We have the Trinity in us, inscribed in our very beings, by grace, through baptism. We are temples of the Holy Spirit, sharing an intimacy with Christ in the inner room from which we pray to the Father in secret (Matthew 6:6).

In this image of our very selves, we notice the relationality within the Trinity, the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We are made for relationship. We are made to pour ourselves out and to receive, to give and to receive. We are inscribed with the Trinity in our very natures. When God looked upon unfinished creation all the other days, he saw "it was good" (Genesis 1:4-25). He looked upon us — created in his image and likeness, male and female meant to enshrine that image of the Holy Trinity in our very bodies — and saw that “it was very good” (Genesis 1:31). Our lives are icons of God's triune love.

What that means, how that is lived out, how this changes not just how we approach our faith but everything in our life, we will discuss in the chapters that follow.
