# Epilogue — Engineering Mystery

## The Engineering Worldview

Without realizing it, all of us have a way of viewing the world. We have an imaginative vision, a way of looking at things, a lens through which everything comes, and we experience it — we add labels to it, we quantify, we qualify, we calculate. And I would say that there is an engineering worldview, a way in which we look at the world as engineers. I was very much of the engineering worldview. It was a safe way of thinking for me, especially in high school.

I found out that I liked physics. Physics you can trust. Math you can trust. It does not lie to you. It does not hurt you. So I found that I could throw myself into physics problems, and it would be a way of understanding the world. I could understand it through mathematical equations. It was a way of calculating rather than reflecting and resting.

And one part of being in that engineering worldview is that I liked to optimize. Everything was an optimization problem. I desired efficiency. I would not waste effort. Why would I study for an exam more than I needed to? I could know the minimum amount I had to study to get the grade that I desired, and then I would study that amount, and then I would waste the rest of the time in dissipation. That was my strategy in high school, and for the most part it actually worked. And laziness was a huge motivator for me, because I could control the things I could control. The things I could not control, I did not have to worry about, because I could go off and be an engineer.

## An Optimized Vocation

I was blessed to be from a large Catholic family. I am the eighth of fourteen children. Even at Franciscan, that is a large family. And when I was in high school, I had a deepened experience of the love of God for me — actually coming to a Franciscan youth conference after my junior year of high school, where the desire to pray was placed on me. The desire to do God's will was there for me.

But I had my life mostly set. I desired to be an engineer. I could use the physics that I was learning, and I could apply it to making things. So my high school became about optimizing my high school experience so that when I arrived at college, I could study engineering in an even more efficient way. That meant taking AP classes with the advice of my older siblings who had done engineering before me. I could learn from them about what to do and what not to do, and I would be able to enter college with at least a semester's worth of credits under my belt.

There was really only one option for me for college, and that was the University of Pittsburgh — because why would I waste my time applying somewhere else if I did not want to go there? I was lazy. Why would I care if some other school wanted to accept me? I only had one place I wanted to go. I remember Franciscan University reached out to me and invited me to apply, and I said, "You don't have an engineering program. If you had an engineering program, I would go there. But you don't. So I'm going to go to the University of Pittsburgh. If I was going to be a priest, I'd go to Franciscan."

You see how that worked out?

So when I went to the University of Pittsburgh, I had a desire to be an engineer. I put my identity in my work. I studied because I was told that freshman engineering, before Dr. Sanchez got involved with teaching freshmen, was grueling. And they told you, you have to make a choice. You either have friends, or you are an engineer. You can party on weekends, but there are other programs for you in the arts and sciences. Liberal arts? Yes, do that. That way you can party. So I threw myself into it. But also I had a desire at the same time to pray, to enter into the interior life. I knew that was lacking in me. I knew that many of my years and my youth were wasted away in utter dissipation. It was not YouTube then, but the beginnings of social media — I had a MySpace account. I was friends with Tom from MySpace, as was everyone. But the dissipation was mostly watching TV, watching movies, getting my life to just disappear, staying up late doing nothing.

I did not like this. I wanted the interior life, and I did not realize what it was. I wanted to pray. When I came to the Franciscan youth conference during high school, I got a scapular, and I thought, I need to pray the rosary every day, because if I do not, it does not count, and I am living a lie — which is a little bit true. I did not pray the rosary, I do not think, at all during my senior year. I started a few times, and I thought, I just cannot do it. It is too much, because it was the weight of all of this — a lot of suffering, a lot of woundedness. But I did not get around to actually praying.

But I thought — college — I can start these habits. I had a little pocket version of the Imitation of Christ, and I snuck off to the library to read this in between classes, back before I actually had any kind of work. I started going to Mass once a week, in addition to Sunday Mass — I always went to Sunday Mass — and I stayed after and got dinner with my brother. We had deep conversations. I started to do Eucharistic Adoration. First, I was filling up the time with some book that told me what to pray, just to get me through that hour. And more and more I opened up towards silence, the resting, and the embrace of Christ. As this went on, I found a desire in me.

The priests who had influenced me so much now that I was in college — the priests of the Oratory, who I got to share life with — I got to see how they lived, I got to see how they prayed. They were normal. I wanted to be like them. And I started serving Mass, which was a ploy to get me to go to Mass more than just one extra time a week. But it worked. And I found I wanted to be on the altar. I saw that desire in me grow.

And so my sophomore year — having taken upon myself by that time a daily rosary, almost daily Mass, almost daily holy hour — I went to my uncle. He was a priest. And I said, "Hey, Uncle Mark, what do I do? I'm thinking maybe priesthood." And he said, "I have an idea for you. Not this summer, but the summer after — Trinity Sunday, that's the Sunday after Pentecost — you need to make a decision. Right now you're broadly discerning, and that makes sense for where you are in life. You're nineteen years old. But you need to decide whether you're going to go from discerning to discerning of the priesthood. And that's the time you're going to make that decision."

He was telling me this — it was December of 2008 — and he was talking about a year and a half from then. And I said, "Uncle Mark, you need to hear me out. I've been going to Mass every day, and I want to be there. I've been praying the Rosary. I've been doing the Holy Hours. I actually really desire this. And you're the first person I've told about this desire, because I kept it so hidden. Because if you let someone know about it, you might get hurt."

He said, "Oh, let's up this a little bit. Not a year and a half from now, but this summer. Come Trinity Sunday, the Sunday after Pentecost, you need to make a decision. Are you going to discern the priesthood, or are you going to just go on discerning? That's when you're going to make the decision. In the meantime, continue doing what you're doing."

That was the advice I was given. And come Trinity Sunday — a very important day — I made that decision, peacefully. I told my father. He was thrilled. "Oh, my son? A priest? Oh no — wow, that's amazing. Yes, wow, yes." And my mom said, "Oh, but you were such a good engineer." And so that summer, I dropped out of all my engineering classes, took philosophy classes, switched my major to philosophy — I was told I had enough credits that I had an engineering minor in electrical engineering — and I would graduate two years later with a degree in philosophy. And I would join the Oratory of the same priest who had influenced me during my undergrad.

And now, as a priest, I work in campus ministry to give back to the students at Pitt, CMU, and Chatham.

That engineering worldview was very important to me as a high schooler and as a college student. But that is not what it means to be transformed in Christ. This is how the world looks at things. This is how this age looks at things. We reduce ourselves to calculators, to robots, so easily replaced by artificial intelligence. We forget what it means to be human. We forget what it means to be a son or daughter of God the Father.

## What Mystery Is Not

When you hear the word mystery, the typical connotation is that a mystery is something that needs to be solved — Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes. Mysteries are solved, in this understanding of the word mystery.

That is not what we mean by mystery. As we saw at the very beginning of this book, that is not Christian mystery. Christian mystery is something that is so knowable that it overwhelms your ability to know.

We can think of the sun. It is so visible that it overwhelms your ability to see. Think of the eclipse of April 2024. You could not look at it with your naked eyes until it was entirely occluded by the moon. Or if you tried to look at it through a lens that was not approved, but you thought it was better than the other lenses — you would actually damage your eye, leaving a little bit of a shaky part in the middle of your right eye. Do not ask me how I know. I thought a welding lens was sufficient. Engineering worldview: not good.

Yet the sun is that by which you see everything else. Mysteries illuminate. They overwhelm. We are involved in them.

## The Moon and the Child

I will talk about another celestial body: the moon.

Several years ago, I was visiting my sister and her four-year-old daughter, Elizabeth. They had just returned from a trip from Eastern Pennsylvania to visit my niece's grandmother. So I excitedly asked her, "Elizabeth, how was your trip?" And she says to me, "Uncle PJ, guess what? When we go with the car, the moon follows us."

My heart sank, because — Elizabeth, that is not how it works. You have to understand that the moon is so far away that you will not notice any difference in the angle at which you are seeing the moon, because it is so far away and it is so huge. You think it is closer to you, and as you are driving, it looks like it is following along with you. But it is not.

In fact, we can even prove this, and I quickly concocted in my mind how we can apply the principle of non-contradiction, in some way, to this. Put you, Elizabeth, in one car, and put your sister, Mary Margaret — very good Catholic names — in the other car, and drive different directions. You both observe the moon. You will see that it is following both of you, and that has to be impossible. So we have to look for another explanation. Now, parallax is going to be a little bit too complicated for a four-year-old. But I thought, maybe this is the time for me, as her uncle, to correct her. She is utterly wrong. The moon does not actually follow you. It is an inanimate object that does not care about you.

So I said to her, "Elizabeth, that's not true. It only looks like it's following you."

She interrupts and says, "No, Uncle PJ, it really follows me."

And then I thought — oh. I was trying to crush her wonder. I was trying to crush her childlikeness. Had the moon any care for little four-year-old Elizabeth? No. No, it does not.

You look up in the sky — those things do not matter to you. Those are things to be analyzed and measured. We never see the other side of the moon, because it is tidally locked. We can study all of these things.

But for a child, the moon involved her. She was implicated in it. It is as if the moon cared for her and went with her. Physically, she was wrong. But in the greater sense — mysteriously — she was right. The stars involve us. The moon cares about us. There is a man in the moon, as it were.

## The Viewer Is Part of It

I looked at it in the technical frame of mind — the engineering worldview: to be measured, calculated, analyzed, kept at a distance — because keeping things at a distance is safe. And I know I will not be wrong, because I can trust the science. I would never have to make that mistake of thinking that someone cared about me when they did not.

Gabriel Marcel was a philosopher in the twentieth century. He was of the school called personalism. Gabriel Marcel said that mystery, properly speaking, involves the viewer. We are part of it. The world of the problematic is something that is separate. He distinguished problem from mystery. Typically, mystery novels — mystery novels are problems. Mysteries themselves are lived.

You can think of a principle in physics, especially in quantum physics. When you go to measure something — this does not matter on the macro level, but it matters on the micro and the nano level — the means by which you measure it actually affect the thing you are measuring. You cannot get an accurate, precise measurement, because the probe you are using comes into contact with it. And if you had a qubit — you know about quantum computers — a qubit is something that is a zero or a one or anywhere in between. But once you measure it, it defaults to a zero or a one. The act of measuring it affects it. It seems that on the quantum physics level, we understand that we are part of the experiment. We are not separate from it. In this way, it is somewhat mysterious — if only by analogy.

## A Solar Flare of Love

God is a mystery. That is not because we cannot understand God. There is so much about God that it overwhelms us. We are enveloped in it, because we ourselves participate in God. The being that we have is a participation in God, who is capital-B Being. God does not have being; he is being. And you cannot separate yourself from that. You can study it — you can study it theologically or philosophically. But you could also experience it as mystery, where you enter into it.

Not only is God a mystery — the Trinity is a mystery. There is so much to understand about the Trinity. It overwhelms our ability to think. We cannot possibly grasp it. You try to stare into the Trinity, and it is like staring into the sun. We can separate it into its parts, but we are always going to be overwhelmed by it. We cannot study it — theology does study it, and theology has its uses. What is the proper response to the Trinity? Worship.

We are included in this mystery — this mystery that is like a solar flare out of the love of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Out of all things that were created — the excess, the abundance of God's love spilled out and strewn across the sky — God looked for someone who would share in his image and likeness, who could respond, who could reflect him, who could make a total gift of themselves, who could receive others. We are thus included in the mystery of creation, implicated in its mystery. When we study creation, we are studying ourselves in the process. And we are examining the echo of the Trinity.

And this Holy Trinity is not something that is merely studied. Rather, marriage enshrines it. Motherhood embraces it. Fatherhood mirrors it. Friendship touches it. Loneliness, when it does not despair, adores it.

## The Invisible World and the Sacraments

The Trinity is lived — to glorify God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — because we are included in that mystery. This is the fundamentally Christian worldview. We understand mystery. We see that mere matter is not mere matter. The things that we see around us are teeming with the life hidden from us. The visible world — that which we can analyze, calculate, measure — communicates the invisible world.

St. John Henry Newman — when he was a child, he said that he thought the invisible world was more real to him than the visible world: that the visible world was like a shroud placed over us, and that the invisible world was where he truly felt at home — the world of God, the world of angels. As children, we see this more intuitively. And we learn to forget about the invisible world.

This takes on another dimension for us as Catholics, because when we take mere matter, it becomes transformed — sacramentally, of course, in the seven sacraments; preeminently in the sacrament of the Holy Eucharist: mere bread and wine transubstantiated into the body and blood, soul and divinity of Christ. And this becomes a vehicle of grace. The life of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — his indwelling — comes to us powerfully through the sacraments.

So when you are baptized, and the waters pour over your head, over the threefold invocation — "I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" — what is symbolized by that action happens. You are transformed. You are a beloved daughter, a beloved son, from that moment onwards, of God the Father. You live your whole life now, embraced by God the Father.

## Intimacy — The Most In

So you can have, as you go on through life, this confidence that St. Thérèse of Lisieux too had. She said, in her Last Conversations, that even if she were to commit the worst sin, she could throw herself into the arms of God, her Father, and be confident that he would catch her. This means, then, to live differently, because we live in that mystery of the love of God for us.

One way to enter into the mystery is silence. God the Father spoke one word from all eternity — St. John of the Cross says — and that word was his Son, and that word was uttered in silence. And if we are to receive that word, we are to receive it in silence.

It is in the silence of the heart that Christ instructs us to go. He says: when you pray, do not be like the hypocrites who like to stand on street corners and marketplaces. Rather, when you pray, go into your inner room, close the door, and your Father who hears in secret will reward you. Of course, Christ can be referring to your actual inner room — the place that is cut off from everyone else — but he is also referring to the inner room of your heart. And you go into that place, and God the Father hears you.

Do you know the etymology of the word intimacy? Who has ever heard that "intimacy" means "into me see"? That is a *false etymology*, though a convenient way to explain it. But that is not what intimacy is.

It is a Latin word — *intimus*. This is grammar, so this is hard for engineers to understand. Engineers are notoriously bad writers. It is a superlative: *in*, *interior*, *intimus* — the most in. Intimacy means that when we have intimacy with God, he is most interior to ourselves — the truth we met earlier in the chapter on intimacy.

St. Augustine said he looked for God outside of himself, looking to fill himself up with the things of this world. But at last, he looked into his heart, and he found God there. "Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new! too late I loved Thee! … Thou hast made us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." God envelops us with the mystery of his love. His Trinitarian love spills out to us. We are included in that love. He is more intimate. He is closer to me than I am to myself.

Every year on the feast day of St. Augustine, if you go on Instagram — which I do not ever recommend — you will find that some Catholic person likes to quote this one thing that St. Augustine apparently said: "I looked into my deepest wound, and there I saw your glory, and it dazzled me" [quotation wording unverified]. God rests in those deepest, darkest places in our hearts — those places we want to forget about, places where I have been hurt, where I have not received love. God is already there. As much as we invite him in, as much as we try to make him show up, he is already loving us.

## God Has Already Loved You

And this is love — not that we have loved God, St. John says — not that we have proved to God that we loved him, that we have gone out of our way and made sure that we loved him first. No. "In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the expiation for our sins." God is already loving us. He has taken the initiative. He is not waiting around for us to prove ourselves.

Part of the engineering worldview is that we have to work. We have to have success in order to make any kind of progress. The problem with school is you have to get your good grades. You go into your work, and you have to do what your boss expects of you. You have to live up to some standard of performance. And that way you might be rewarded with some kind of raise. That makes sense. That is the basic reward system that we have. It is the worldview that we have of this age — to not be conformed to this age, but to be transformed by the renewal of your mind. For God has already loved you. You do not have to do anything to prove it. How we live that out, then, is that we receive that love, and we respond.

## The Enchanted World: Liturgy, Gift, and Poverty

Part of living in mystery, part of seeing the world as it truly is — the invisible world underneath everything that is visible — is living in an enchanted world.

When we live in this way as Christians — not mere magic, but the love of God pouring out from that chair there, from that watch, from those blinds, from everything — you can see God's love pouring out. This transforms how we view the world. It reminds us — everything reminds us — of the excess of God's love, of the abundance of God's love. So now when we look up at the night sky, we do not just see stars that get studied under the science of astronomy. Now we can see God's love for us and the generosity of his creation. And it is not wrong to think that the stars care about us in some way. Recall the moon that followed Elizabeth: it truly follows her. God's creation is for us. It is his gift to us. It is an icon yet to be transformed. For Christ came to draw all things to himself, to recapitulate all things in Christ, and to offer himself to God the Father.

One way in which we do this is what we call liturgy. Liturgy — when you hear that word, especially spoken by a priest wearing a cassock, it sounds like: reverence, and pristine movements, and signs of the cross that are crisp, and hands that, as a priest, do not go beyond your shoulders. I do not know about your experience of liturgy at Franciscan. It might be different from that. You might think of someone talking about liturgy as something that is just — you have to exactly follow the rules. That is not liturgy as rubrics. Liturgy — as David Fagerberg put it — is doing the world the way the world was meant to be done. It is actually an answer. Why food? To eat. Why that chair? To sit. Why something rather than nothing? Liturgy.

We are meant to respond to the gift, to the mystery of God. We are meant to respond to him in redeeming us. We are meant to respond — with, through, and in Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity — to God the Father in the Holy Spirit. And the way we do that is through the body of Christ — the body of Christ, his bride, the Church. And when we follow the rules, when we do Mass, we hear those readings that are themselves a prayer offered to God. And when we give ourselves over to God in the Offertory, we give ourselves over to him in Holy Communion — who gives himself so totally over to us — we are caught up in a higher cycle of giving.

Have you studied any systems engineering yet? Do you know feedback loops — positive and negative feedback loops? A negative feedback loop is typically the more desirable one. Negative feedback loops are safe. But in the spiritual life, we actually want to be caught up in a positive feedback loop — the loop we met earlier in this book as the positive feedback loop of grace. We want the output to be fed back into the input and to increase things exponentially. In the liturgy, we are offering ourselves to God, who offers himself to us.

Now, we know at least enough theology of the body to know about the law of the gift, as it is commonly called. The law of the gift means that when you give something away, you do not lose it. You actually gain. It is actually a spiritual life hack.

For if you give of yourself, you end up gaining everything of yourself. It is only in a sincere gift of oneself, the Second Vatican Council says, that man finds himself. So if you give yourself away, so totally over to God, you gain. You feed that output back into the input. You give everything of yourself over to God, and you have already gained so much that you give more back to God — whether that is in the expressions of devotion, whether it is a willingness to suffer, union with Christ crucified, whether it is that friend you bring along the way — you give more back to God. Every day when you offer yourself to God, you make that little act of self-offering — giving your day, giving your work, giving your thoughts, your words, your actions, over to God, whether through a morning offering or the prayer of the Church, or a desire to go to Mass.

What typically happens is we hold back. We do not want to give ourselves. We think it is not safe. It might get out of control. Faith, hope, and love — the greatest of these is love. These three are theological virtues: because they go towards God, they do not admit of excess. Whereas in something like courage, you might say too much courage is foolhardiness. But too much faith — there is no such thing. Too much hope — there is no such thing. Too much love — there is no such thing.

We can get caught up in the mystery of God's love again and again, and completely offer ourselves in our totality, in our capacity to give of ourselves — not in mere contracts and little bits of ourselves, siphoning off just the smallest amounts in order to be safe — when we give our whole selves in our poverty. It is like the woman, the widow, who gave only two small coins in the treasury. And our Lord, on the eve of his passion, said: that woman has given more than everyone else. Everyone else gave of their excess. She gave from her poverty — her whole livelihood. And when we give, we give similarly. We give poorly.

One more thing that challenges the engineering worldview: you have heard the expression, "Whatever is worth doing is worth doing well." But some of you are familiar with G.K. Chesterton. He said, "If a thing is worth doing, it is worth doing badly." I would correct Chesterton here. Anything worth doing is worth doing *poorly*. For if it is worth doing, it is worth doing. It does not matter how you do it. We end up getting ourselves caught up in that aphorism — "whatever is worth doing is worth doing well" — and we do not even do it.

I go back to myself when I was a senior in high school, and I wanted to pray. I had opportunities to pray so much, but instead I listened to music. Instead I would just sit on a couch. Instead I would just fill up my time with whatever I could, in order to not have to face myself. But maybe just praying poorly would have been the move to do at that time. Whatever is worth doing is worth doing poorly.

And in fact, in this setting of gift and mystery, whatever is worth doing is worth doing out of your poverty. Give of yourself so totally that you become poor in the process. The lovely emphasis we have as Franciscans on the poverty of St. Francis — Franciscan spirituality — means that poverty is essential to giving. You make yourself poor, and in making yourself poor, you gain everything.

## The Feed and the Feasts

We have a God who is a loving Father who will not deprive you of what you are meant to have, of what you are meant to be. One thing that gets in the way of this habit of living in mystery — going back to silence and recollection — is what the author of The Soul of the Apostolate calls dissipation. We ought to have the custody of our hearts, to keep out those things that are going to distract us from God. Dissipations are rampant nowadays, because there are so many ways that we can satisfy a dopamine addiction. We can just go on to social media, get caught up in TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, and see what is on our social media feed.

You ever wonder why it is called a feed? What kind of creatures consume a feed? Animals — barnyard animals. Here is your feed.

We are human. We are not animals. We do not have *feed*. We have *feasts*. We are not meant to be reduced to that level. Many of us living this life of dissipation have become like pigs. We are caught up in a feed. Instead of wonder, we doom scroll.

It is safe, however. It has its own sort of negative feedback, because I can do that until I am tired enough to fall asleep. I will scroll in its nice negative feedback loop.

You are not called to that mediocrity. You are not called to be comfortable. As Pope Benedict has said, you are not made for comfort — you are made for greatness. This challenges us as engineering students. This challenges us as any kind of student, or any kind of person living in this age.

Dissipation is the enemy of recollection. Dissipation prevents us from experiencing reality as mystery. It gives us a false, counterfeit sense of control. And yet it controls us. We are enslaved. We are reduced. We have screens. Screens block out the mystery. Unlike screens, veils clothe mystery, they accentuate it; they show what is behind the veil. We do not look at the veils, we look beyond them. Yet so often, we are stuck looking at screens. We are blocked. This dissipation is a great enemy of the interior life.

## Watching for the Christ Already Within

The interior life begins with this knowledge: that God is closer to us than we are to ourselves. And everything that is driving us to obstruct, to screen out, and to dissipate, does not need to be there, because we are already loved. God is actually our Father. He actually cares for us. He has sent Christ, his Son, so that all of us can be sons and daughters in Christ the Son — so we can be joined with him in his offering of himself to God the Father — so we can be ourselves teeming with life eternal, even here and now. This interior life happens in our interiority, in the recognition that God is already within us.

One of the attitudes we have to have as Christians always — it comes up every year in Advent — is an attitude of watching, of waiting for the coming of Christ. So we watch for him. And St. John Henry Newman — himself an Oratorian priest, like myself — said that watching is that feeling you have when you are expecting a friend to come to your house, and for some reason he is delayed, but all of your thoughts are fixed on him, and you are just waiting for him to be there, and you are desiring him to come. That is this attitude of watching.

But recall the friend already home, whom we met in the chapter on intimacy: we are waiting for our friend to show up, and then we come back to our house, and we find that he is already there. His car is parked in the driveway. And we go inside, and he is already sitting on our couch.

What do you say to such a friend? Welcome. I see you made yourself at home.

Christ is already dwelling within us. Welcome him. Speak to him. Be silent with him. Gaze on his face with wonder. Look into his eyes. See how he looks at you. He cares for you. And your sufferings are not far from his sufferings. For his mysteries are your mysteries. Your sufferings are his sufferings, and there is no suffering, no wound, that you have undergone that he has not taken on himself. He has loved you so much. He is sorry when you feel like you cannot trust anyone. He is sorry when you feel like you have to aim for counterfeit things. But he just invites you. The interior life is a response to the invitation that Christ constantly gives us.

## Rest

"Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."

This is where the book ends — not with another thing to optimize, but with an invitation. It is the same rest we met in the chapter on intimacy: Christ sleeps in the boat even as the storm rages, and he is already home.
