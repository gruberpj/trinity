# Chapter 3 — Gift and Liturgy

> *As a deer longs for flowing streams, so longs my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and behold the face of God? My tears have been my food day and night, while men say to me continually, "Where is your God." These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I went with the throng, and led them in procession to the house of God, with glad shouts and songs of thanksgiving, a multitude keeping festival. Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you disquieted within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my savior and my God. My soul is cast down within me, therefore I remember you from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar. Deep calls to deep at the thunder of your cataracts; all your waves and your billows have gone over me. Amen.*

— Psalm 42:1–7

Thirst opens this chapter, and thirst will carry it to its end: deep calling to deep.

St. Paul, in the twelfth chapter of his letter to the Romans, says, "Do not be conformed to this world but be transformed by the renewal of your mind." I propose that at least one way — and I will not say the only way — to be renewed by the transformation of your mind is to see mystery, to encounter realities as mysteries, to see the meaning behind that which presents itself to the senses, that which can be measured, to go from beyond the merely problematical to the actual mystery at hand — and not to be deterred by the enormity of the mysteries, but instead to enter in, to recognize that we are implicated in them. And that is a good thing, because we ourselves are good. This is part of what we call sacramentality.

## The Trinity of love that overflows

Understanding God as mystery — that he is one God, one divine nature, and three divine persons — we see that everything that God does is mysterious. There is a way in which we are implicated in it. We can think of creation itself as mysterious, approaching it as a mystery, for we ourselves are created. The uncreated love created love out of his creativity, as one of the retreatants put it, and according to Joseph Ratzinger. God's love spilled out. He was infinitely perfect within himself. There was no need, in one sense, to create. For God was love within three persons — a communion of love, not some lonely figure off in another world unto himself, but fully loving. God pouring himself out, Father to Son, Son to Father, that love being the Holy Spirit. This Trinity is a Trinity of love. And that love made us.

Can I try to explain this? There is an artist of the twentieth century named M.C. Escher, and he has this depiction — I will go first with the staircase. It is a really mind-bending staircase, where you can go up three levels, but the dimensions are switched around. It is confusing. You are going up the stairs, you are up the stairs, you are off the stairs. You are always going up, in this case just dimensionally. On top of that, he has the M.C. Escher waterfall, with water that seems to be flowing back and forth, going up and falling back down, defying the laws of physics. But this is the idea of this water constantly flowing, going up — though we do not know how — and then falling right back down.

I wanted to try to capture that with the Trinity. I remember how Father Mike explained the Trinity — he would try to draw a picture of it — as three buckets. One bucket would pour out into the other bucket. So I made a picture. This is my attempt, not at the M.C. Escher waterfall, but at the PJ Gruber Trinitarian waterfall. It is imperfect, because it seems to be too directional, but the idea is that the water flows and falls into the pool, and flows and falls into the other pool. There is a constant pouring.

I kind of like to do this, too. If you put your fingers together in front of you, it looks like there is a third finger that appears between them. That is not a great analogy, but I like it — how the Holy Spirit comes between [unclear] and the Father. That love is personal. This is sufficient from all eternity.

Creation is a going forth of the love of God — God making something that is not God, not so that we might be separated from God, but so that we might return to him. In Latin, there is a phrase — two words: *exitus, reditus*. *Exitus* sounds like exit — for often that is how Latin works — and then *reditus*. There is a going out, an exiting, and a return. God creates that which is not God in order that everything he creates might return to him in the way that is fitting for that creation.

## Made to respond

God made creation so that creation could respond. He threw across the sky distant galaxies and stars and nebula, and he created one planet within one solar system that was able to sustain life. And in that one planet, in that one particular place, he created male and female after his own image and likeness. God is relational in himself, and when he created us male and female, he made us relationally, made for relationship — which is to give of ourselves, not just in a way of interacting with the environment and with each other in bits and pieces, but to give of ourselves in our totality. He made us personal.

The three divine persons are, within the Trinity, subsistent relations. We are also, in a similar way but different, individual substances of a rational, relational nature — in a way different from God, but also in a way imaging him. We show forth the Trinity. Every time we make the sign of the cross over ourselves, we recognize that we ourselves are in the image and likeness of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. And in that way, we are made to respond.

Creation is the gift of God. It is a going out. And then all creation responds through us. The response of all creation through humanity, and in particular through Christ, who is true God and true man: we call that response liturgy. Typically, when we hear liturgy, we think perhaps of the rubrics, the rules, about how we are to celebrate Mass and pray the liturgy of the hours. But liturgy, more broadly speaking, is our response to God.

## "Mine"

He wanted Adam and Eve to respond, for they were made capable of responding, in God's image and likeness. Adam and Eve turned in on themselves. They grasped after what was going to be given to them as a gift in due time. They made it their possession. Eve reached out, grasped that fruit. She said, "*Mine*." When she said "Mine," she cut herself off from communion with God and with Adam. And Adam shared in that fall. Thus cut off, humanity waited to be united. All the liturgies of the Old Testament, all the rituals and sacrifices, all the prayers and the psalms, everything pointed to a time when that would be satisfied, when we could make that full response. And it was not until grace entered into creation again, preveniently, in the soul of the Blessed Virgin Mary at the moment of her conception, that God could find within humanity a way to respond.

## Mary's yes

God created. He used the passive tense. "Let us make," he says. "Let there be light." *Fiat lux*, he creates. And there was light. "Let the dry land appear." And there it was, dry land. And God creates one in creation to give some kind of echo of response. And it was not until the Blessed Virgin Mary, as a young woman, responded to God's will: "Let it be to me according to your word." God said — Mary said — and the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. God, who said, "Let there be light," finally got a response from a creature. *Fiat lux, fiat terra, fiat mihi secundum verbum tuum.* Mary's response — that is the liturgical response.

And when Jesus teaches us how to pray, he says, "When you pray, pray this way: Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come, thy will be done — *fiat voluntas tua* — thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven." When we pray that "Our Father," recognizing we pray that with confidence, within and through Christ, who is our insertion into the Holy Trinity, we are echoing God's creation. We are giving that full response in our totality back to God.

We have access to God the Father because Christ has given us that access through his Sacred Heart. Before that, in the rites of the ancient Jews, there was a veil that no one could enter beyond, a veil that stopped anyone from seeing into the Holy of Holies. Only the high priest could go once a year, and he would say the name of God there. But we have access, because we have Christ, and his Heart was that veil that was pierced for us.

As one author, Aidan Kavanagh, said: "The liturgy is doing the world the way the world was meant to be done." We have all these things that are not God, but they show something of God. They are teeming with his life, with his existence. And the whole world aches to give this response. Liturgy is this response. Liturgy is, in effect, a response to a question that we ourselves could have. I might say, why this chair? For sitting. Why food? For eating. Why something rather than nothing? Liturgy.

When we come together and we pray in union with Christ, as members of his mystical body, his bride, the Church, we are making that response. Our response is not lessened because we are part of a whole. We ourselves are wholes within it: we make this full response, for we are as the Church, collectively and individually. We are a spouse to Christ, and we make this prayer to God our Father. We are given the Holy Spirit, who teaches us how to pray, "Abba, Father." Liturgy is how we live now as Catholics.

All liturgy is received. Think about what distinguishes liturgy from even our private prayers. When I pray, just talking to God as a friend, which is a very good thing to do, that is not liturgical. A liturgy is something we receive. There are guidelines, there are words we use, there are instructions that we follow. And so then, it is a gift. Liturgy is itself a gift.

Liturgy engages all the different parts of us — the physicality of our human bodies, being present physically. Liturgy is bodily. It means being physically present, and it means, as best as possible, being mentally present. I do not think people need to accuse themselves more than they already do about being distracted at Mass, because your mind will not be able to be focused the entire time. It is not about being focused. It is about being present — and being present and being focused are different things. Our minds do wander. It just does not matter, in some ways. I am present. I am not necessarily focused. Because sometimes focus is to treat it as a problem, not as the mystery itself. To be present, to respond — the Church emphasizes active participation, which is a way to say actual participation, like your heart is being joined to what is going on. Being intentional — that is all part of our response.

Receiving Holy Communion, in some ways, is not even the big point of going to Mass. The point of going to Mass is the praise and glory of God the Father — which means that if you ate a sandwich right before Mass, it is still good for you to be at Mass, even if you do not receive. And if we can receive Holy Communion, that is great, but that is not the point of why we go to church.

The liturgies of the Church — the rites of the Church that regard the sacraments — sometimes these rites are done within the Mass but are distinct rites; they are liturgical acts. I do not make up the words of the Eucharistic Prayer, for example. That would be wrong. The Church says, no, you have to use these words. You have options, but you can [possibly: only] use these words. But other prayers could be influenced by Scripture, which is also received — and Scripture is meant to be proclaimed liturgically. The Liturgy of the Word is actually a prayer to God the Father first and foremost.

## Baptism: inserted into the Trinity

Understanding this liturgy, we have within it the seven sacraments, the first of which is baptism. Baptism is the sacrament — the mystery itself — by which we are incorporated into Christ, in which we are made beloved sons through Christ, the eternal Son of the Father. He who was adopted into our human nature: through him we receive adoption as sons. We are then truly sons, more by grace than we are sons of our biological fathers by nature. We are inserted into the Trinity. God's life is poured over us. When that water hits our head three times, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, what is enacted through material means is done immaterially, and grace is given.

We are washed clean, original sin being wiped away. We are restored, and we are elevated. Made in the image and likeness of God, but that image, marred by original sin, is now made perfect, and ready for that perfection which Christ invites us to. Baptism is our entrance into divine life. We use the word "grace" — in this case, sanctifying grace — to mean the life of the Blessed Trinity within us. And baptism opens us up to the other sacraments as well: confirmation — we are sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit — and the Holy Eucharist — we are fed with the bread of eternal life. We have the sacraments of vocation — of marriage, of priesthood — and the sacraments of healing — of confession, and anointing of the sick. In all of these sacraments, there is involved a pouring out of God, usually through material means of some kind.

The devotion of our lives — you could say that the living of a daily prayer life is an extension of our baptism, which is itself liturgy, where we are immersed and poured over with water. The deep calls unto deep there. But liturgies could be used in a very broad sense.

## Everything is positive

The sacramental life is how we live as Catholics. But we also have this idea of maybe more broadly sacramentality: not just the seven sacraments, but recognizing that all visible things point to the invisible, all material creation comes with the immaterial — it is a package of something beyond us. There is a mystery to behold — and every leaf that falls from a tree, every blade of grass, every hair on our heads — there is mystery, and there is gift.

Luigi Giussani, one of the great religious thinkers and reformers of the 20th century: everything is positive, to use Giussani's phrase. Everything is positive. Everything is good. And if it does not exist, it is not good. Sin is nothingness. Christ said, "Apart from me you can do nothing." And when we sin, we do nothing. But everything — everything that shares in God — shares in his goodness. Everything has a goodness to it, even sin. Sin is our attempt to grasp after something that is good — but not good for us right now — and instead to insert some nothingness into it. As one twentieth-century author put it — Bruce Marshall — "the young man who rings the bell at the brothel is unconsciously looking for God." There is always goodness, and there is no reason to ever be discouraged. There is no reason to ever despair. And everything of our own lives, everything we encounter, is teeming with that life, is teeming with that mystery, is teeming with that gift underneath it.

St. Thérèse said at the end of her life, before she was about to expire from tuberculosis — a very painful, terrible disease — that everything is grace. God's life is in everything. It is brimming with mystery. For this reason, St. Augustine could see the Trinity in everything, or St. Elizabeth of the Trinity could not help but talk about the Trinity in everything she would see — for the relations between objects and persons, between person and person, everything communicated something of the Trinity. But everything is gift. St. Elizabeth of the Trinity: how can you not contemplate the Trinity? How can you not see it between the fruitfulness of a friendship or the delight of someone seeing you and asking, "Hey, how have you been doing since that thing happened?" Honestly, there is a Trinity right there, spilling over into my life. That is mysterious, in the true Christian mystery sense.

Is a number a mystery? Yes. And so it seems like if we say yes, we are simply applying mystery to anything — and we can recognize that everything could be engaged as mystery, and everything could be engaged as a problem.

This is a fundamentally different way to view all reality. How we see the world is utterly different. We work with totalities. We work with wholes. We work with the mystery coming through, shining through the veil of the material world.

## The enchanted world and the law of the gift

We are living in an enchanted world. Not that it is magic in the sense of the occult, but it is magical and enchanted, because the material world is brimming with the invisible. We need to have the sacramental vision by which we see all things. And when we do that, we will want to respond.

We are surrounded by nature, and I hope you will go outside this afternoon and see that behind every rock there is a life — a whole sort of microcosm of existence. This is a fun time of the year to overturn rocks and find the salamanders that are hiding underneath. But I encourage you to do that, because you see, there is so much more going on behind the surface, and the beauty is that the invisible comes through the visible. This is called, more broadly, sacramentality.

Recall John Paul II's Theology of the Body. He speaks often of the law of the gift, as it is commonly called. In this world, when we give something away, a material thing, I no longer have this. I give this folder over. It is no longer mine. I give it to this person here. Gift is a mystery. You know your intentions behind a gift, but you do not understand how the receiver is also receiving. These two things are joined together. It is too much to go into [unclear], but it is already including us. But in the world of grace, when I give, I do not lose. In fact, I gain. Giving over something, we gain. Our response to all of God's creation, all of God's gifts, every one of God's blessings, is that of thanksgiving and praise.

I have come to think more and more that one of the solutions to a lot of the problems we have in this world is thanksgiving — to recognize the blessings God has poured out for us and to give that response — even that liturgical response, the Thanksgiving, the Eucharist, of our holy sacrifice of the Mass. Thanksgiving is a solution because it recognizes everything as the gift that it actually is.

And everything being good, that means everything is gift. To live in gift — this is a distillation of what it means to live in the Spirit. The gift of God Most High poured out for us — the love between Father and Son proceeding forth from them both, coming down to us, living among us, living within us. To live in the Spirit is to live in that gift and to make a gift of ourselves back to God.

## Two kinds of feedback

And there is something that happens when we give ourselves back, because of the law of the gift. There is something that John Paul II understood, something that John of the Cross certainly understood, and Louis de Montfort understood, in his devotion to Mary. And we give totalities. We do not give just pieces. So it is a total consecration of ourselves. And we give again. Now we have more to give, and then we gain more. And in the world of engineering and signal processing, this is called a positive feedback loop.

There are two kinds of feedback loops. One is called a negative feedback loop. This is the safe kind. This is what — what is it called in a car, when you want to go the same speed and you put on cruise control? Cruise control is a negative feedback loop. If you start going faster than your speed, the car will slow down. If you start going slower, the car speeds up. If there is a negative change in speed, the car reverses it, to get back to that speed of 70 miles per hour. A negative feedback loop establishes equilibrium and balance.

Much of our lives, we think about having balance and equilibrium, and there is a usefulness to it. But a positive feedback loop does not reverse the change when it is fed back into the system. And so we might think of a positive feedback loop in a car. If the car started going faster down a hill, and if it was a positive feedback loop, it would actually rev the engine more to make the car go faster. And because the car is going even faster, the feedback would make the car go even faster than that, and eventually the car is going 180 miles per hour and it cannot be controlled. That is dangerous.

Explosions — chemical explosions. You need heat in order for there to be a chemical reaction. There is a small chemical reaction, and it makes more heat, which causes more chemical reactions, and eventually this results in an explosion. Chain reactions in a nuclear bomb result in a massive explosion. This is a positive feedback loop. This is disastrous, you think, in so many different contexts. And when you have, for example, speakers and a microphone — many of us have had the experience of someone with a microphone walking in front of a speaker — the speaker projects the sound, and it gets picked up by the microphone. The microphone takes that sound and puts it in the speaker and amplifies it, and you get this loud screech. That is a positive feedback loop. It is totally undesirable in so many different contexts. In engineering, a positive feedback loop is destructive. But the opposite is true in our relationship with God.

## The positive feedback loop of grace

God gives of himself totally to us in creation and in redemption, and we respond totally with the gift of ourselves in baptism, in the life of the baptized, in the sacraments, in our devotions, our prayers — we give ourselves back to God, and his mysteries, the mysteries of the rosary, and we gain, and God gives, and we give back, and we gain, and we gain — and this is exponential. This is the positive feedback loop of grace. This is our liturgical life to its completion. And then we end up living within the Trinity.

In the prayer of the liturgy, we make the response of the whole Church together, in unity with Christ, to the Father. We pray the prayer of the Mass. Who is the Mass directed to? The Father. That is right. Who is the prayer directed to? Oh, Jesus. Everything is about Jesus. No. It is the prayer of Jesus to the Father, that we are being included in, that we are being offered now to God the Father. We make that response in the unity of the Holy Spirit — through him, with him, and in him. O God, almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, all glory and honor is yours, forever and ever. We are joining in with the Son's gift back to God the Father. His thanksgiving — his Eucharist — for coming forth from God the Father from eternity, he gives himself back. That itself, within the blessed Trinity, is the Eucharist. And we participate in that same prayer. What Christ did on the cross, when he went and died and gave himself so totally to the Father, he has done from the heart of the blessed Trinity for all eternity, and we participate in that. In the Mass, this is the highest gift. This is the source and summit of our faith.

## Deep calls to deep

We are made for this infinity. We who are infinite abysses of existence cry out, and we pour ourselves out. In Greek, the word is kenosis — to pour out. St. Thérèse says, love is to be poured out into another. Going back to the words of Psalm 42, deep calls unto deep. From the abyss of our hearts, we call out to the abyss of the Holy Trinity — an abyss calling upon an abyss, a communication going between two, between an I and a thou. All your torrents and all your waves washed over me. This is utterly catastrophic in this world. To be under a waterfall would result in a lot of death. To be caught up in the waters of chaos and death — typically a deadly activity — is something an ancient Jew would have had a fear of. They were aquaphobic. They did not like water. And so this fear of water was present from the beginning — this chaos that order needed to be instilled upon, this water. But in our prayer, deep calls unto deep. This happens within the heart of the Trinity, and this happens with us. We find our meaning in receiving from the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and we call upon him, and he calls upon us. In the life of the Trinity, we are meant to be washed over, continually swept up — God's pouring out of himself and his triune grace. And we say, let this happen. Let deep call out unto deep. Let me make this prayer of hope.

To hope is to affirm being, says Gabriel Marcel. To despair is to negate being, to give up on all existence. But to hope in God — this is what we are being called into, in the life of the Trinity that we live. We who are other Christs, we who are Christians, are inserted into that holy mystery of the sacred Trinity. We make that full response of ourselves. It is unceasing. It enters into the way we live our lives, even in our work — to use the world well, so we might make that response of the world to God.

This is pointing us towards that heavenly liturgy — the rivers of water that we would be partaking of, the waters of love that wash over us, the torrents and the waves that overwhelm us. This is what is happening in the holy liturgy. We are part of something so much greater — not just the mere sacramentality of the visible and the invisible world being entwined, but the love of God poured out into us through the unity of the Holy Spirit, through and in God the Son, to God the Father. And this is what gives purpose to our lives. This is why we are created. It is all to participate in the Trinity. The Rublev icon — when you look at it, it forms that chalice, and Jesus is there in the center. We, who participate in the bread of life that is Jesus' body, blood, soul, and divinity — that is our participation in the Trinity. This is why this matters so much. There is so much going on here, and everything from our life now has meaning because of this. We who receive Jesus in the Eucharist now know that we are loved, that we are known, that we have a purpose, that we are joined to something greater. And we who struggle to find a meaning within suffering, we who have attempted to despair and have many times despaired and repented — now we can affirm Being, capital B. We can affirm the blessed Trinity, in which we are participating here and now. This should overflow for us.

In his house, there are many rooms. There is welcome for the sinner. There is a place that is set aside for us. We return to the Father — like the prodigal son — joined with Christ in the Paschal mystery, where he who has come forth from the Father has done his own pouring out to enter into creation, and to take on our sins, our sufferings — to die, to enter into hell [unclear], to rise from the dead, and to say, "I will arise and go to my father." He goes — like the prodigal son, but never having sinned — to God the Father. He joins us with himself, and we make that full return through Christ and his passion, death, resurrection, and ascension. And we are called into that heavenly liturgy — the worship of God in heaven — which we participate in now by following God's will for us, to enter into that most sacred celebration here and now.

## Give thanks

This is how we live the Trinitarian life in all aspects — not segmenting it off into some part that we call our faith life. We live our life; our life is inherently Trinitarian, and our life is Trinitarian through the shape of the cross that is traced over us again and again. Living this mystery means living utterly differently, and it needs to be expressed. This is the summit — everything that flows down now seeks out that lowest place. God's mercy finds that lowest place within us and in our fallen world. To live this way is to live entirely transformed — but it is not going to involve a depletion of our resources, for we are not living in the world of the problematic but the world of mystery. We have been made for mystery. We are meant for love. And our whole existence, our whole lives, is for love. This we carry out in liturgy, pouring ourselves out. This is our return to God the Father. And what I recommend is to live this life of thanksgiving, to be thankful before God at all times, in all places. Saint Paul says, "Give thanks."
