# Chapter 4 — Relationship

## Two Kinds of Knowledge

There are two kinds of knowledge. The first knowledge I think we are very familiar with. I alluded to it in the earlier conferences, talking about how we deal with problems. It's the knowledge that comes to us through dissecting things that we see, things that we measure, things that we understand. All the different kinds of ologies — biology, philology, geology — all of these kinds of sciences work through understanding the parts and dissecting them, getting down deep into it, throwing your minds into these areas and absorbing as much as you can, and then putting them back together to form a cohesive whole. And this is how we go about much of our life. It is the realm of the technical, the problematical, and we dissect, we divide, we disjoin. Much of our knowledge comes this way.

St. Thomas Aquinas would quote Aristotle, who said, "Knowledge begins with the senses" — things we can touch and feel. And from that we derive things we can measure, things we experience on the superficial level, although as if it was apart from ourselves, as we are not implicated in it.

The other kind of knowledge is the one that most accords with mystery. It is to treat things not as pieces, pieces of a puzzle, something to put together, but as a whole, in its totality. This is the kind of knowledge that we have as Christians, given to us — the mysteries of our faith, the mysteries of Christ, the events of his life, his death, his resurrection, the mysteries of the rosary, the mysteries of the teachings of the faith. Everything in theology that we understand can be engaged with as mystery, because we are recipients of it. We are participants in our faith. So we already engage with this on some level.

## Knowing from the Inside

Those that write and read poetry might be aware that there is a unitive awareness in the poetic imagination, the way in which a poet views the world, approaches things not as pieces, but as wholes. And the same might be true with the mystic. The mystic is thrown into the depths of knowledge of God, but not in a knowledge that we typically can relate to. And this is a knowledge involving the totality of their being with the totality of God's being under some aspect.

Someone who goes through a mystical experience, I'm told, is not able to communicate what happened in that experience. St. Paul sort of sheepishly talks about how he knew a man who was lifted up into the ninth [possibly: third] heaven, whatever, some level of heaven. And he said, I don't know which, I don't know. And he can't really tell much about it, because it's hard to tell. What you know in that way can't be easily explained.

This kind of knowledge, St. Thomas Aquinas calls this connatural knowledge. His example that he uses to explain how this knowledge works is that of the virtue of chastity. You can understand chastity by observing it and putting it down to its separate parts, understanding how the intellect and the will work together with the passions or against the passions in order to experience and to give and to receive chaste love in the appropriate context. We can study theology of the body. We can understand these things in all their various ways, and we can understand this virtue well. But someone who lives chastity starts to become like it through their living of it. And they might not decipher it and divide it down into its different parts, but someone who understands chastity from the inside has a knowledge that exceeds that of those that study from the outside.

This is called connatural knowledge by St. Thomas Aquinas, because it is knowledge that has become second nature. Virtues become part of us. All knowledge is through a likeness. If I know something, when I divide into parts and understand the different concepts that go into a field of study, those concepts remain in me. There's a likeness imprinted into my intellect. But when we become like something, we bypass the knowledge that comes to us through concepts. And instead, we have that likeness imprinted directly on us, on our person. And that allows us to know and understand on a level wholly different. And it's to know and understand with our totality something in its totality.

## Marked with the Cross

Mystery is best engaged with this second kind of knowledge, where we are imprinted with the Holy Trinity from our baptisms. We are told we are given a mark. Our baptism marks us. The shape of that mark, of that seal that's placed in our souls, is cruciform. And we reenact that mark every time we make that sign of the cross. It is imprinted on us. It is meant to become so deep that the Trinity is really that by which we understand everything else.

St. Paul says in his letter to the Romans, "Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind." The way in which our minds are renewed is how we come into a unitive awareness, a contemplation of — and most especially — relationship.

## Christ Between Us

Christ says, "Where two or three are gathered in my name, there I am in the midst of them." Christ comes in the midst of us. He comes between us — not to separate, for we often use the phrase "between us" as something that's showing an obstacle, but to actually connect us.

It really wasn't until Christ that we became, again, for the first time since Adam and Eve, capable of real communion. For we have a deep communion in Christ, with Christ, but also with each other. Recall the consequences of the fall, how Adam and Eve were disjointed by their original sin. So Christ comes between us.

## I and Thou

Martin Buber was a Jewish philosopher of the 20th century. His great work is called *I and Thou*. It was written in German, and using thou, which is like the more, you know, the older form of you, was emphasized in the English translation to stress not just reverence towards the other, but we're really talking about a singular person. When you say "I and you," you could be plural. He wants to say it between a first person singular pronoun and a second person singular pronoun. I and thou.

## The Space of the Holy Spirit

In a relationship, there is an I, a thou, a you, and there is a between. There is something that is shared. There is the total gift under the aspect, in a lot of cases, of friendship or other relationships. And in that between, this is the space of the Holy Spirit, He who is between the Father and the Son. And that charity that is in God comes down to us in the theological virtue of charity, that we are meant to become more and more loving, to know more and more what love is from the inside out, and then to love others with that same love. This is expressed in friendship, in any kind of relationship.

I recall Pope Benedict's inaugural homily when he was made Pope in 2005. He said, "It is really so: the purpose of our lives is to reveal God to men. And only where God is seen does life truly begin. Only when we meet the living God in Christ do we know what life is. We are not some casual and meaningless product of evolution. Each of us is the result of a thought of God. Each of us is willed, each of us is loved, each of us is necessary. There is nothing more beautiful than to be surprised by the Gospel, by the encounter with Christ. There is nothing more beautiful than to know Him and to speak to others of our friendship with Him." We are able to really engage in friendship because God has loved us first. He has poured himself out for us when we were not deserving. It is pure gift. And because God has poured himself out, we are able to love each other.

## Every Relationship Says Something

All relationships have something that they say about the Trinity. In some way they express that primordial love, that love that was there before the sun and the stars and before we came into being, that love shared constantly, acting out fully between Father, Son, and Holy Spirit from all eternity. That love is the same love now with which we love each other under different aspects and under different relationships.

We are meant to approach, then, the Trinity under and through these relationships that we have. Motherhood, fatherhood, sonship, even co-workers and others, all these things now have something in common with God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. St. Augustine was not wrong to see the Trinity reflected in all aspects of reality, but most especially in us, who are individually and collectively images of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

## Encounter, Not Merely Understanding

In this way, we do not merely understand the Trinity. We don't just reduce it to a notion. We don't just divide it up. We do — under the aspect of theology. We do have notions of the Trinity. We understand it. We can talk about the theological underpinnings of it, and you can read the Catechism, which actually does a great job doing an overview of a very dense topic. We're not meant to merely understand the Trinity.

We instead are to encounter from the depths of our being an infinite abyss of our existence.

Marriage enshrines the Trinity. Motherhood expresses it. Fatherhood mirrors it. Friendship touches it. And loneliness, when it does not despair, adores it.

So maybe the way to take this with you is not to ask what the Trinity means, but to ask which of these relationships God is asking you to see as Trinitarian this week — your marriage, your motherhood, your fatherhood, a friendship, or a loneliness.
