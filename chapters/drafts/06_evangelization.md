# Chapter 6 — Evangelization

> *Dear Jesus, help me to spread your fragrance everywhere I go. Flood my soul with your Spirit and life. Penetrate and possess my whole being so utterly that my life may only be a radiance of yours. Shine through me and be so in me that every soul I come in contact with may feel your presence in my soul. Let them look up and see no longer me, but only Jesus. Stay with me, and then I shall begin to shine as you shine, so to shine as to be a light to others. The light, O Jesus, will be for all from you. None of it will be mine. It will be you shining on others through me. Let me praise you the way you love best, by shining on those around me. Let me preach you without preaching, not by words but by example, by the catching force of the sympathetic influence of what I do, the evident fullness of the love my heart bears to you. Amen.*

— St. John Henry Newman

## Back to St. Patrick

This final chapter circles back to where we started, talking about St. Patrick and his evangelization of the people of Ireland.

According to tradition, St. Patrick had blessed a fire in the 400s that no attempts by the Druid pagans could extinguish. That fire kept burning and burned brightly — a witness against the pagans, but more importantly, a witness of God's love; the fire was meant to go forth and spread.

The words that send us to evangelize come from our Lord before he ascended into heaven, when he told his apostles: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the close of the age."

There has been an evangelizing impulse from the beginning, from the gift of the Holy Trinity given to us. Because we are baptized into Christ, because we receive the divine life of the blessed three in one, we are impelled to go outwards.

The Church's own canon law recognizes this: all of us, by virtue of baptism and confirmation, are by nature missionary.

This missionary nature derives from our Lord's teaching: the greatest commandment is to love God, and the second is like it — to love your neighbor as yourself. The perfection of that commandment is to love one another as I have loved you. What has been poured into us cannot be contained. If we have now shared in the mystery of the Holy Trinity, the mystery is not ours alone; it is meant to go out and make us evangelize all people.

And going back to the little rectangle on Rublev's Trinity icon — it may represent, as we saw, the four corners of the world. The message of the Trinitarian love of God is meant to go out.

## Owning the mystery

We think about evangelizing those who do not yet know Christianity, or who do not know it well, and we run into an awkward question. We want to tell them about Jesus. We want to tell them that Jesus died for their sins — that he not only died for us, but rose from the dead and ascended to the Father. All of this is well and good, but to anyone hearing it, it is a problem. For — you are saying Jesus is God? Yes. But does not Jesus have a God? Is he just the Son of God? Are there two gods? What is going on with that?

We run into the problem of the Trinity right away, anytime we try to evangelize and catechize as part of that work, and it is an awkward thing. Do we try to work around it? Do we insist there is one God and just say, "Don't worry about that — it's a mystery"?

Some from the outside might say, "How is this altogether different from those people who believe in multiple gods? It seems that you want to have your cake and eat it too." Or maybe, "How is this different from the Mormons, who believe the Father and Son are separate divine beings, and that the faithful may one day become gods? How is this different? For don't you, as Christians, believe that you will become like God? Did not one of your early Christian saints — St. Athanasius — say, 'For the Son of God became man so that we might become God'?"

What do we do with this objection? We need to take ownership of it. We need to acknowledge that the Trinitarian mystery of God is something that actually gives us greater access to God. He is not just transcendent, far away; he is immanent — close to us, within us, the most within us. He is intimate with us.

Once we even begin to understand the Trinity — even with just helpful images — the fact that some things can be one and three, the relationality between beings, between people, between persons, the image of the Trinity expressed in marriage, where husband and wife give themselves so totally to each other that a third person comes forth in fruitfulness — all of this helps us see that there is a Trinitarian structure to our relationships.

## Christ in the poor

It is fitting that the prayer I said at the beginning is from St. John Henry Newman, and it was a favorite prayer of Mother Teresa. As she went out and encountered Christ in the poorest of the poor, she lived out Matthew 25. "How, Lord, when did we see you hungry and give you food, and thirsty and give you drink? When did we see you naked and clothe you, and find you in prison and visit you?" And our Lord said, "Amen. Whatever you did for the least of my brothers, you did for me."

St. Paul was taught by his conversion. "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" "Who are you, Lord?" "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting." St. Paul recognized that in those who suffer, Christ is present. Christ is with us in the poor. He is with us in our poverty.

And when Mother Teresa went out to serve the poorest of the poor, she could not help but see Christ in them. It gives me great edification to know that she loved this prayer of St. John Henry Newman so much that when people hear it — this prayer of "help me to spread your fragrance everywhere I go" — they think it is Mother Teresa's, because she prayed it so often. Yet it actually belongs to a rather intellectual saint of the nineteenth century, an Oratorian priest named John Henry Newman.

## Other Christs

But we are meant for others to see only Jesus in us, so that we might shine out with his light. Mother Teresa understood intuitively that we are to be Christ to Christ. We are to be as Jesus to them. We are other Christs — not just because we belong to Christ and are called Christians, but because we are Christ. We are the extension of Christ in this world.

We, who have been incorporated into his body through the Holy Eucharist, are his hands and his feet going out into the world. We serve others as Christ’s body ourselves.

Here we start to see a positive feedback loop. It is Christ serving Christ. We who give of ourselves so fully to this work of evangelization, this work of the corporal works of mercy, serve Christ. They are built up, and they too are transformed. They might go forth and also love Christ and others. It is a chain reaction. In an atomic bomb, the chain reaction would result in a massive explosion of megatons of TNT. For us, it is evangelization.

We serve Christ, and as Christ, everyone we serve encounters the gospel. They encounter Christ, and they know they are loved. The two great desires of our heart are to be known and to be loved. How could they go forth without loving others with that same love?

## Nothing more beautiful

And so the chain reaction goes into the four corners of the world: this totality of our witness, our own martyrdom, our giving of ourselves so fully over to Christ. The witness is a gift never meant to be extinguished within us.

Who, having a light, puts it under a basket? No, you put it on a lampstand to shine out. "You are the light of the world," Christ says. He also says, "I am the light of the world." Both of these are true: we are the light of the world because we are other Christs. We shine out with the same light so that others might only see him, and from that one light, the light is spread.

Christ came that we might have life and have it abundantly. He came to set the world on fire, and how he wished it were already ablaze!

We remember how St. Ignatius of Loyola would say to his missionaries, the Jesuits of the sixteenth century, as they were sent out into the four corners of the world to spread the gospel to people who had never heard it: "Go, set the world on fire." We burn with that love, that catching force of sympathetic influence, so that others might see Christ in us and be influenced by it.

## The Easter flame

The way we evangelize the love of the Trinity is the same way the light is spread at the Easter Vigil, the mother of all vigils, when the priest blesses the fire, the sacred fire. Lifting it up and saying, "Lumen Christi" — the light of Christ — and from that one flame go forth all the different tapers, all the little candles around the church. The church that was in darkness is now filled with the light. Each of us carries in ourselves the flame of Christ in our hearts, symbolized by the candle that we carry. Now it is kept blazing and is meant not to be confined to that church, but to go out from there.

## Missa est: "It has been sent"

At the end of every Mass, we hear: "Go forth, the Mass is ended." *Ite, missa est.* "Ite" means go. Go out. What happened here is not meant to be confined here. The liturgy is meant to envelop all of creation. It is cosmic in its proportions. Christ comes so that all things might be recapitulated in him, might be united in him — all things. Christ says in the book of Revelation, "Behold, I make all things new."

"The Mass is ended" is actually a questionable translation. "*Missa est*" does not mean "the Mass has ended." According to one older reading — that of the nineteenth-century liturgical scholar, Prosper Guéranger — it means "it has been sent." What has been sent? Sometimes people translate it as "be sent" — "go and be sent." Some priests will explain that "missa est" means "be sent" — "go and be sent, be sent, go out into the world, be sent into the world." However, it does not quite mean that; the grammar of *missa est* means something has been sent.

What has been sent? Who has it been sent to? What has been sent is the offering of Christ, the *Eucharistia* — the thanksgiving, the oblation, the full gift of himself. Who has it been sent to? We might think that the Mass is offered to us. It is not primarily offered to us. The Mass is our offering of Christ to the Father. Christ has been offered to the Father. The Mass is not just Jesus coming down to us in the Eucharist. The Mass is that Jesus comes, and he goes — he goes out from the Father down to us, and he goes back to the Father. What he does in salvation — in becoming incarnate and dying and rising and ascending — he does in the Mass. He has been sent back to the Father.

We, as Christians, as other Christs, are meant to pattern our lives after that heavenly liturgy. We are meant to go forth and be sent, as Christ was sent, to the Father. It has been sent, it has been offered, and we are now to do likewise in the way we live our lives. The liturgy does not end with the last words of the Mass, with the last song we sing. The liturgy goes onwards in our hearts, where we can always pray to the Father in secret and make offerings of ourselves.

What Christ does in the Mass is not just a mystery about the second person of the Holy Trinity; it is a mystery of all three persons. It is through Christ, with Christ, in Christ, to God the almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, to whom all praise and glory belongs forever and ever. That mystery is meant to be lived by us now, and as we go forth. Mass is not a one-time thing, not something we merely do for half an hour or forty-five minutes a day and then leave behind. It is how we live our lives. The source and summit of our lives is also how we live our evangelization.

God's unselfish love, his *agape*, has been poured out, and from it, in its excess, in its generosity, in its abundance, all things were created. That *agape* poured out now finds its response in us in our *Eucharistia*. The liturgy is the pouring out of God — his agape — and our Eucharistia, our thanksgiving in Christ, coming together. And now this response is meant to spread outwards. We are to be the light to the world.

## Living it, not just teaching it

In fact, not just how we teach but how we live needs to be relational. We think of it mostly in terms of teaching, and we think there is right teaching and there is wrong teaching. And those who espouse right teaching — we are in that group, in that same tribe. That group is where we are comfortable, where we are supported. They will say things we will most likely agree with, and therefore we will be around those people. Those people who say false teachings, we will reject. We will separate them. They do not matter. They are problems to be solved — or, more often, problems to be ignored. In this way of focusing only on teaching, we fall into rejecting others and rejecting the mystery — not allowing the story they have, of brokenness and error, to be completed by Christ with the mercy of God.

Do not just teach the Trinity. To live the Trinity means to love them as persons. We are tempted to have that dividing line — there is us and them. But there is no peace in thinking this way; it is the peace the world gives. As Fr. Michael often says, it is the peace that comes from the unity of everyone minus one. It is how we can scapegoat, but it is not how we are to live.

The mystery of the Trinity does embrace all. All things make sense by it. All persons are meant to be included in it — not just by nature, but by grace as well. To live from the love of the Trinity, then, means to love all those — even those who hate us — as our Lord says: "Blessed are you when men revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account." We can share that blessedness if we make sure to give of ourselves and be truly vulnerable.

## Tell everyone

How could we contain this mystery in us? If you have been changed by this retreat, if you have been transformed by the renewal of your mind, you will not let it just stay with you. If the Trinity is the best-kept secret of our faith, as Fr. James Dominic Brent calls it, then tell everyone. The mystery is not meant to be confined, not meant to be held back; it is meant to be the leading point, the arrow of our evangelization.

There is nothing to be ashamed of in the Trinity, and nothing to hide away and say, "I can't understand it, neither can you, but just know that Jesus loves you." Yes, Jesus loves you, but he wants you to be part of him, incorporated into his body, sharing in his very being, and to be part of the Holy Trinity with, through, and in him.

We are made from love. God created everything out of love in order to draw all things to himself, and that drawing is meant to include us. We who are embraced by the very Trinity want others to share in that embrace also. Do not hide this away. The Trinity should be spilling out of our thoughts, out of our words, out of our actions — not just out of the words, as though we reduce everything to words. No, we show it forth.

I love how Catholics — because we understand mystery, because we understand that we are created in God's image and likeness and actually share his being by creation, and by redemption are given his grace, his inner life becoming our life as well — do not merely tell, but show. We show it by the evident force, the catching force of our sympathetic influence, our care for others, because we see Christ in every single person we encounter, so that no one is a mere mortal, as C.S. Lewis would say, but everyone, as St. John Henry Newman says, is "an infinite abyss of existence."

We are not meant to remain empty abysses. We are meant to be filled with the fire of God's love. We are meant to share in his being. Christ chooses to do that through us. What is his favorite way, the way he loves best, to share the glory of God — Father, Son, Holy Spirit — but by us shining on those around us, those he has given for us to love.

## Floodgates

In every single person we can see the mystery of Christ and his redemption, his yearning for us. Some of us are waiting to be activated. Some of us are dead in our sins, waiting for Christ to rise again and go to the Father. Christ has come to us in our brokenness and has forgiven us our sins — our venial sins even in the holy Eucharist, and our mortal sins through the sacrament of confession. All of us, though, are meant to share in that divine life.

Let us not hold that mercy back. Let us open the floodgates of God's mercy and love into this fallen world. Let him love us. Let the waves and the billows, the torrents, all wash over us. In the chaos of this life, let us find Christ within, already resting in our souls, and be impelled by him, especially in his gift of the Holy Eucharist, to go forth.

We are now to spread mercy to everyone else as well, and to go forth and welcome — just as we welcome Christ in our own hearts — those who most reject us, those we would rather protect ourselves from. Welcome, then, with forgiveness; welcome them with compassion, to share in their own sufferings, and then to help draw them. We might be the means by which others encounter the same Trinitarian love. Our baptisms are not meant to terminate within us. They are meant to impel us forward as well, to make disciples of all nations, so that others might be baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.

Let Christ love us. Let him love others through us. May we all be caught up in that love that made the sun and the stars, and be joined with Christ and the Father and the Holy Spirit in the heavenly liturgy to come.
