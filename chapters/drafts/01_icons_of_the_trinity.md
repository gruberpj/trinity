## St. Patrick and the Shamrock

The impetus for this retreat, and why I picked this particular topic, was to recognize another saint that is during the month of March — one, though — even though he's not of the same status as Saint Joseph — gets maybe more celebration, at least in our country and in Ireland. And that is the saint, Saint Patrick. Saint Patrick, who was known for his evangelization of the peoples of Ireland during the 400s — after having been a slave in Ireland, having learned the language and escaping, he came back to evangelize the same place where he was held in slavery. And he was going up against a pagan religion — the religion of Druidism — a pagan religion that saw spirits in nature, a respect for trees and certainly plants, and had its own customs and rituals, high priests, wizards, and very many problems morally. And the biggest problem is they didn't believe in God and needed to be converted. St. Patrick ached to be able to help convert this land, and he came in, and having known the language, he was able to preach to the people of Ireland — many miracles attested to the power of God with him.

And when he preached, he did not shy away from the central mystery of our faith, and that is the Trinity. We have the prayer of St. Patrick, St. Patrick's Breastplate, of which we began this conference. And we also have the story of the shamrock, how the shamrock was used to illustrate to a people that may be caught up with botanical language how something can be both one and three, that it can share the same life, be connected in the same way, but yet be distinct at the same time. How could God be three persons and one God?

It's maybe more understandable to think of a pure monotheistic religion — which we do have, a pure monotheistic religion. We really do. But to say there's one God, one person, one God — and if you were Jewish [possibly: pagan] and you believed in many gods, that would be hard enough to accept. But to say you believe in one God who is three persons — that is a very difficult thing.

St. Patrick led with this because it is what our Lord told us to lead with. "All power in heaven and on earth has been given to me." So St. Patrick did not shy from holding up a shamrock and saying, "Three and one," and to give them something to grip onto in their minds, to have an image around which could later be corrected, modified, where they could enter into a theological understanding of the mystery at hand — but to give them something to begin with. It was so crucial for evangelization for the people of Ireland, at which St. Patrick was incredibly successful.

## Another Icon of the Trinity

I want to turn to another icon of the Trinity. An icon just means image. An icon, in particular — when we talk about icons, we think of the icons of the Eastern Catholic and the Eastern Orthodox. And in particular, I want to look at a Russian icon from Andrei Rublev of the Trinity. This is included in all of your packets, and it would be really helpful if you can take it out and look at it.

It's not usual to depict the Trinity. There are some instances in art, but typically people would shy away from depicting the Father and the Holy Spirit under any kind of personal form. And we would depict Christ, because the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. We can show Christ, and we can show what he looked like, and there's no affront there. But to show the Father and the Holy Spirit — well, this is hard to do.

Andrei Rublev, when he wrote this icon — for icons are not painted, they are written, because the Word became flesh, and the Word is a written word, as well as a spoken word, and a read word. This is written in that same way, to communicate about the saints, or, in this case, these three angels depicted here.

## The Hospitality of Abraham

It recounts the story from Genesis 18. In Genesis 18, there are three visitors sent from God, three angels, that showed up to Abraham and Sarah's tent. Abraham and Sarah quickly, out of an abundance of hospitality, prepared food for their guests, who sat with them and ate. They were near the Oak of Mamre, and there they fed. And it was there that the angels, the three messengers of God, communicated to Abraham that Sarah was to conceive in her old age. And that's where eventually Isaac would be born.

And the scene of these three angels, in retrospect, we would look back and say they are a sort of representation of the Trinity. This is a very normal thing for us to do, having been given the revelation that God is both one and three, through Christ, the incarnate Word, and the sending forth of the Holy Spirit. We now know that God has a triune nature. And we can look back in the Old Testament, where this mystery was still hidden, and see resonances of God's Trinitarian nature.

And so we might say the first chapter of Genesis, when God says, "Let us create man in our own image and likeness" [quotation wording unverified]. We who now know of God as one and three, we think that the "us," the plural, refers to the Trinity. And we are not wrong. Originally, it referred to the angels, the choirs of angels — "Let us create," God would speak to them. But for us, we can retroactively understand that in its fullest sense, God creates from the Trinity. Likewise, when three angels show up to Abraham and Sarah, we can say this is an Old Testament representation of the Trinity.

## Reading the Icon: One Divinity

The icon has a lot of meanings in there, and it's actually very helpful for us to look at art. It engages our imagination in a way that's very helpful, especially for a mystery that often confounds us. The mystery of the Trinity does confound us, and there are reasons for that.

But looking at this icon — if everyone can take a look at it — we see there's three angels around a table, and the other settings, other parts of it, are hard to see, but I will explain them as we go. These three angels, they all are dressed in one color that is common to all three of them, and that color is blue. The one on the left, you see a little bit of blue. The one in the middle has a lot of blue, and then the one on the right has a good bit of blue that's visible. Blue, in the iconographic tradition, represents divinity. So they all share the same divinity.

## The Holy Spirit and the Mountain

Likewise, all three of them have this scepter, this stave — in their, I believe that's the word, stave? We'll go with that — and their left hands. They all have the same power. They all share the same divinity. And they all have the same face. It's the same face, same hairstyle. I don't think there's any differences in the wings or anything else about them in their physical appearance. Where we notice differences are in the colors that each one of them wears, starting with the angel on the right. See, that angel is wearing green over top of the blue, the blue tunic underneath. The green represents life. In this time of spring, there will soon be green everywhere to replace the brown. Green represents the new life. It is associated with the Holy Spirit, that breath of life that comes over the world every spring. In the East, they don't wear red for the Holy Spirit, for Pentecost. They wear green, because the new life that comes from the Holy Spirit. So the angel on the right represents the Holy Spirit, and behind him, in the distance, that figure is that of a mountain.

A mountain is associated with the Holy Spirit, especially in the Old Testament. We recall how many good things happen on a mountain. Mountains symbolize the Holy Spirit, prophecy. And we go to the New Testament — and where do we find mountains? Mountains, we might think of Mount Tabor, where he went with Peter, James, and John, and they were all enveloped in the cloud — the cloud that symbolized the Holy Spirit — and then the Father's voice was heard. And there you have a Trinitarian exposition in the New Testament. And then of course the mountain — or really rather the hill of Calvary — where Christ gave forth the Spirit. The Holy Spirit would be connected with mountains, especially in the icon tradition.

## The Son in the Middle, the Father on the Left

The central figure in this icon in the middle, in addition to the blue on the outside, he's wearing this purplish, reddish, maybe more brownish color. And the brownish red — we have the red of — it's kind of in between. I don't know what color to call that; my color identification is poor. Some would say it's more of a brownish red, and that could symbolize the Word that went forth, that went down to the earth and walked on the dirt of this earth — the red representing the blood of Christ poured out for our salvation, or even the purple representing the kingly power. And with that kingly power, too, it's symbolized in the gold stripe along his right shoulder. That symbolized his kingly power.

Looking at his right hand, his fingers are extended, two fingers extended out. This was a typical posture used when raising your hand in blessing in the East. The two fingers represented the humanity and divinity of Christ — what we call the hypostatic union, where God is united with us in our human nature: true God and true man in Christ, fully human and fully divine. The other three fingers representing the Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

And then behind him, there is the tree — the tree, which makes sense in the context of the story of the Oak of Mamre. But we also think back to that original tree where original sin happened, when Eve plucked that fruit from the tree of knowledge of good and evil. And by a tree, humanity fell. We also look forward to that tree of life that is the cross, when Christ goes up on that cross and dies for us. So the tree behind him goes along with the identification of the middle angel as the second person of the Holy Trinity.

On the left, obviously, would have to be the Father. Now, his outer garment, it's hard to identify. In this picture, it looks pinkish, purplish, with some blue hints to it. It's original — when it was originally written, the colors were meant to be hard to identify, sort of ephemeral and beyond our ability to really name. And this represents the Father.

And behind him is a house. Now, the house of Abraham and Sarah, sure. Sure, but also we recall our Lord's words in John 14, "In my Father's house are many rooms." Or we can go back to Luke 2, "Do you not know I must be about my Father's business and my Father's house" [quotation wording unverified], that is, the temple. The temple in Jerusalem being the house of the Father, especially for Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity incarnate. So there's the Father's house there, symbolized by the house of Abraham and Sarah.

## The Postures, the Chalice, and the Empty Place

Now, the postures of all three of them: you see the Father, his head is up, but the other two, the heads are actually pointed towards — they are looking at the Father. This deference to the Father recognizes that both the Son and the Holy Spirit proceed from the Father. And so that is symbolized in that.

The whole image, if you kind of step back, you see sort of a kind of an hourglass, the top of an hourglass — look between the figures on the right and the left: sort of the top of a chalice. And in the middle of that sort of visual chalice, we have Christ, whose blood is poured out for us. Of course, they're all around a table, and they all are sitting on thrones — that's equal dignity there, of course, as well. It's noted also that not only the heads of the Son and the Holy Spirit pointed towards the Father, but even the mountain is as if it was bowing towards the Father, so that the Father might be glorified in all things.

And then we have the chalice, or the bowl, in the center on the table, and symbolizing the food of heaven, the bread of life, the chalice of salvation, which we partake in the New Testament. And then, oddly, there is this rectangle with a little inner rectangle in the corner on this altar as well. And I had trouble trying to figure out what that was. I read somewhere that that rectangle represents the four corners of the world, that the Trinity is necessarily meant for evangelization, to spread the love of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, out of the four corners. But there's another meaning of that rectangle. In the altars of the East, there was a compartment in the altar where the relics of martyrs were placed.

And this is the part of the table where all three persons of the Trinity are not occupying, and we, as the viewer, are now part of this icon. We are meant, in some ways, to be invited into this feast, to share in the hospitality of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. And how do we do so? And it's very poignant: the way in which we share in that hospitality is through martyrdom, through the witness of our lives. "Unless a seed fall to the ground and dies, it remains but a seed. But if it dies, it produces much fruit" [quotation wording unverified]. Our insertion into the Holy Trinity through Christ is through a sharing in his sufferings and his death on the cross.

There are probably other parts of this image — there could be fruit drawn from it. And with our own prayer, I recommend taking this to the chapel, or in your own time of silent recollection, to look at this image. If you come up with other possible meanings, do feel free to share either with me during the times of conferences or afterwards, when we are able to talk.

## The Third Icon: The Sign of the Cross

But there's one more important image of the Trinity I want to draw attention to. And that's not the shamrock, and that's not the icon of Andrei Rublev. And that's the icon of the sign of the cross.

Catholics, we are very used to beginning prayer and ending prayer, bulk [possibly: both] ending it with the sign of the cross. The sign of the cross consists of words: "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Amen." Those words are first uttered over us — water poured three times over our heads — when we are baptized. Our baptism is that insertion into the Trinity where we share in the life of God. The Holy Trinity has a dwelling within us, and those words are important. Each one of them is important. "In the name of" — in the name, not names, although we name Father, Son, and Holy Spirit separately — to say they have one name, it emphasizes the unity of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and their equality in the Trinity.

And of course, this is not just something we made up. It is Christ's words himself, baptizing them — he says, "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." This is from our Lord in Matthew 28, his final words in Matthew's gospel before he ascends to heaven.

These words matter. Every word matters — even the "and" of thus [possibly: these]. And it's good for us, when we pray, to pray with a reverence that we might say, "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," and not to do the sloppy "Father, Son, Holy Spirit." The words matter.

## The Motions: Father and Son

Not only that, the actions matter. When we lift our hands towards our foreheads, this upward motion of our hands goes along with "In the name of the Father." That upward motion is this pilgrimage all of us are making to the embrace of God the Father. We all are ascending. We who have come forth from the Father are now meant to go towards him in our prayer. This is that simple glance directed towards heaven that St. Thérèse talks about when she says, "What is prayer but a simple glance directed towards heaven?" [quotation wording unverified] In the name of the Father.

And then our hand goes down, passing over our heart, and that downward motion, we say, "and of the Son." The downward motion is that of Christ, the second person of the Holy Trinity, descending, going forth from the Father and entering into our humanity. Entering not only into our humanity, but taking all of our sufferings onto himself, all of our misery, all of our sin — the descent of Christ into the depths of human misery, so that all human suffering is taken on him. He undergoes that out of love for us. He binds us to himself so we might be lifted up to the Father. And of the Son — crossing over our hearts in the process.

## The Spirit's Crossing — and the Amen

And then we go to "and of the Holy Spirit," going from left to right. In the Western tradition, it's left to right. In the Coptic tradition, it's left to right. In the Eastern tradition, it's right to left. And there are reasons why it's done differently. But the emphasis there is this Holy Spirit that is now among us. This is the Holy Spirit that has been sent among us. Pour it out for the forgiveness of sins. This descent of the Holy Spirit is now with us. We are living in the age of the Holy Spirit among us, and we cross over, going from shoulder to shoulder — we cross over our heart.

The last word of the sign of the cross is no less important. Amen. We think of "amen," we're like, "What does amen mean?" "Oh, it means I believe. It means I'm committing to this." And it does mean that. When we say "Amen," we are saying, "I am staking my life on this. It will not be moved. This is my stability. I am grounded in this truth. You will not move me. No storms can disturb me, because I am putting — this is my stability, my surety. You cannot take this from me."

This "amen" is no less important than the other words we pray, because this is the most central mystery of our faith, especially when we pray "Amen" in regards to the sign of the cross.

## Who Are We Crossing? Ourselves

And let's not forget the central image, the central icon of the sign of the cross. Who are we crossing? Ourselves. We are images of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We are made in his image and likeness. We have the Trinity in us, inscribed in our very natures, and we have the Trinity in us through baptism. We are temples of the Holy Spirit, intimacy with Christ, the inner room from which we pray to the Father in secret. We are all of these things.

And the relationality within the Trinity, the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — we are made for relationship. We are made to pour ourselves out and to receive, to give, and to receive, to reciprocate. We are inscribed in our very natures, so that when God looked in creation all the other days, he said, "It is good" [quotation wording unverified]. We [possibly: He] looked upon us created in his image and likeness, male and female, meant to enshrine that image of the Holy Trinity in their very bodies. He looked at us and said, "You are very good" [quotation wording unverified], for we are images of God's triune love.

What that means, how that is lived out, how this changes not just how we approach our faith but everything in our life, we will discuss in the future conferences.
