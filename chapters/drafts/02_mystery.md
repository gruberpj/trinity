# Chapter 2 — Mystery

> *O my God, Trinity whom I adore, help me forget myself entirely so to establish myself in you, unmovable and peaceful as if my soul were already in eternity. May nothing be able to trouble my peace or make me leave you, O my unchanging God, but may each minute bring me more deeply into your mystery! Grant my soul peace. Make it your heaven, your beloved dwelling and the place of your rest. May I never abandon you there, but may I be there, whole and entire, completely vigilant in my faith, entirely adoring, and wholly given over to your creative action.*

— Prayer of St. Elizabeth of the Trinity

## The Graveyard of Heresies

The Trinity is the most central mystery in our faith, and it matters greatly. It also matters what we mean by the word mystery. We think of the Trinity as something that we cannot possibly understand, and it confounds us.

I am sure that many of you have heard homilies on Trinity Sunday — the one Sunday a year when priests feel compelled to at least mention the Trinity — homilies that cannot possibly say very much about the Trinity, because if you talk for more than ten minutes about the Trinity, you are likely to fall into heresy. 

We look over the early history of Christianity, and — as Joseph Ratzinger observes in Introduction to Christianity — we see "a graveyard of heresies." We want out of that graveyard!

We do not even have our minds wrapped around what the problems were for the early Christians — except for a few things here and there, like "Arianism is wrong." We do not want to go anywhere near Arianism or Subordinationism or Modalism, so we conclude, "The Trinity is a mystery, we cannot understand it," and we stop there, even though we read in the Catechism that the Trinity is the central Christian mystery.

I once heard a speaker at a FOCUS conference tell about a conversation he had when he was a Protestant. His Catholic friend persuaded him to meet up with a Jesuit priest, and the priest asked him, "What is the central mystery of our faith?" The man, since he was a Protestant, answered, trying to be edgy: "Justification by faith alone." Somewhat distressed by his response, the priest gently responded, "It is the Trinity." The man said sheepishly, "Oh yeah, that’s right." 

It is the Trinity: the love that made the sun and the stars — the love shared by the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, overflowing into all creation and, by God’s mercy, extended to us. The whole Trinity acts to redeem us and draw us back to God: the Father of mercies, the Son in his death and resurrection, and the Holy Spirit poured out among us for the forgiveness of sins. The Trinity stands at the heart of the Christian faith. Through Christ, we will behold the triune God — and share in his divine life — in the beatific vision, where God gives us his very self and enables us to know him. The Trinity is the central mystery of the faith.

However, we may object, the Trinity cannot be understood. The Trinity is a mystery. We cannot seem to understand it. It is a huge part of our faith, we admit, but we tell ourselves that we simply cannot come close to it at all. Although we invoke the Trinity at every sign of the cross, at every Glory Be, and every time at Mass in the doxology, we do not let it have any meaning for us. We are inoculated against the mystery of the Trinity. It is something we hear, something we practice, but not something that we really grasp at all.

## Mystery Novels and Jigsaw Puzzles

When we hear the word mystery, one thing that comes to mind is mystery novels — Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, Father Brown. Those are mystery stories. Mysteries, in that case, are something that need to be solved. There is something we do not know. Who committed the murder? How did it happen? What were the motivations? Who needs to be arrested? The mystery is meant to be solved in a mystery novel.

There are pieces of information that are not there, or if they are present, they require us to deduce from the material given to arrive at the conclusion. Like a jigsaw puzzle, we put it together and we see the picture, and there you go. Or it is like a problem in physics: you come up with the answer through calculating, putting in the different measurements, and you arrive at your answer. Or a problem in engineering: you need to optimize something, using the materials and the designs we have on hand — and within the design limitations, you optimize. We reduce much of what we do in our life to problems that need to be solved.

## What We Mean by Christian Mystery

Seeing mystery as a problem to be solved is not what we mean by Christian mystery. When we think about the mystery of faith — the central mystery of our faith being the Trinity — we also might think of the Holy Eucharist. We might know that in the Eastern Catholic tradition the sacraments are called mysteries, and the Blessed Sacrament is called “the Mysteries,” very simply.

When we say mystery as Christians, we do not mean a problem to be solved, with an answer that we do not yet know. We mean something so knowable that it overwhelms our ability to know.

## The Sun

If you were to go outside on a sunny day and try to gaze at the sun — are you able to see the sun? In one sense, yes, because it is so much light; in another sense, no. You cannot really see the sun. You cannot see its outline. All you see is blinding light.

So you would not say the sun is invisible. You would say the sun is so visible that it overwhelms your ability to see. Your retina cannot handle that sun, but at the same time, the sun is useful in analogy, because the sun is that by which we see everything else.

A mystery is likewise: as Jacques Maritain said — he was a Thomist philosopher in the twentieth-century — mysteries are "pregnant with intelligibility." They are so full of things we can know that it is too much. We are finite intellects in the midst of something inexhaustible, and it overwhelms us.

So what do we do? We can try to get away from it, treat it like a problem, go nowhere near it — or enter into it.

## Mysteries Implicate the Self

Another twentieth-century philosopher, Gabriel Marcel, said that a mystery is "a problem which encroaches upon its own data, invading them, as it were, and thereby transcending itself as a simple problem." In other words, a mystery is not simply something in front of me. It is something I am already inside of. Mysteries implicate the self. You are involved in the mystery. When we approach something as a problem, we approach it as separate from ourselves. It is outside of us. It can be measured. It can be analyzed. If it remains unchanged, we remain unchanged, and it can be solved.

## Persons Are Not Problems

St. John Henry Newman said that each of us "has a depth within him unfathomable, an infinite abyss of existence." That is to say, each of us is a mystery, so knowable that it overwhelms anyone's ability to know. We are even a mystery unto ourselves. The more we come to know ourselves, the more that knowledge overwhelms us. Self-knowledge and knowledge of God are brought together, and we might call that union the virtue of humility — when we know ourselves and know God, and when we see the discrepancy between the two, we act accordingly. To treat another person as a problem, to use them as part of an equation, is to dismiss them at their very core.

Gabriel Marcel said that many of the problems of the twentieth century came from treating mysteries as problems. We have seen the consequences. The so-called “solution” to the so-called “Jewish problem” in Germany in the 1930s and 1940s was the murder of millions of persons. A whole people — an accumulation of mysteries, of persons made to be known and loved — was reduced to a technical problem requiring a solution. When persons are treated as problems, the solution can become death. It was an extension of living in a technical world — optimizing everything, having control over everything. Persons are not problems. They themselves are mysteries — not to be rejected, not to be cast out, not to be solved, but to be known and to be loved.

That was a terrible time in our history. We have certainly learned from that time, but we have intensified now in the 21st century our movement into the matters of the technical. Everything is reduced to a problem, solvable by emerging technologies.

Many make science central to their worldview — almost a god that protects them from otherwise untamable forces — because they believe everything can be measured, analyzed, and optimized. That worldview is fundamentally an attitude of fear that causes us to reduce the mysteries, the persons around us, into problems, leaving us to deal with them on the merely technical level. One of the reasons why we become so anxious all the time is that we live in a world of problems.

## The Two Aspects of Mystery

The two aspects are these: first, mystery is so knowable that it overwhelms us, and second, it necessarily involves us. We are implicated in it. 

We use the word "implicated" to talk about being implicated in a crime. I buy a bike from a friend at a really good deal and recognize that the friend stole the bike from someone else. I am implicated in that crime. Similarly, we are implicated in the mystery of God — not as accomplices in a crime, of course, but because God is already involved with us and has drawn us into relationship with him.

## Elisabeth and the Moon

I will tell a story to illustrate this point about mystery involving the self. Several years ago, my sister's family was returning from a visit to my brother-in-law's family in eastern Pennsylvania. Afterwards I talked to my niece Elisabeth, who was five years old at the time. I just asked her, "Elisabeth, how was your trip?" She excitedly said to me, "Uncle PJ" — because she calls me Uncle PJ — "Did you know that, when I go places in the car, the moon follows me? It really does!"

And my heart sank. I felt bad for her; in my mind, I am thinking, this belief is like one of those Santa Claus moments. Elisabeth, you are five years old — it’s time you learn something about reality. The moon does not follow you. It is so far away that, while traveling at sixty or seventy miles per hour, you cannot perceive much change in its position relative to the surrounding landscape. It only looks like it is following you because of the great distance it is away from you — it seems stuck in the sky. As you travel, it looks like it is also going with you, but that is not the case.

And I am thinking, how can I prove it to her? Maybe I could apply the principle of non-contradiction, or some variant of it. What if we put you, Elisabeth, and your sister, Mary Margaret, in separate cars and drive in separate directions, and both of you observe the moon? It cannot be following both of you. You must look for another explanation.

Quickly, I said to Elisabeth, "Elisabeth, that is not true. The moon does not follow you. It is so far away. It only looks like it is following you." And before I could explain my idea for an experiment that would disprove her notion of lunar motion association, she interrupted me: "No, Uncle PJ, the moon really follows me." And with such opposition, I abandoned my attempt to scientifically disprove her childish perception.

Elisabeth was right, in a sense. She was wrong about the movement of the moon, but she was right to experience it as mystery. Its movement was not separate from hers. She was involved in it, and it was involved in her experience of the world.

She saw the moon as if it had a personality, as if it had a care for her. In my own fear — not wanting to think something cared for me, only to find out that it does not — I thought it was necessary to dissuade her from that childish notion. But she would not let me.

I had an explanation, and the explanation was true. But she had wonder. The moon was a mystery to her. She was implicated in it.

## Augustine's Boy

God is a mystery. He overwhelms us. There is so much to know. We cannot possibly contain God, or get our mind around him, just as you cannot hug a mountain. It is beyond you.

We remember the old legend of St. Augustine walking along the beach and finding a boy digging a hole in the sand. The boy was carrying pails of water from the ocean and pouring them into the hole. "What are you doing, boy?" "I am pouring the ocean into this hole." "Well, you cannot do that. The ocean will not fit. All the water you pour in will just seep into the sand and return to the ocean." And the boy says, "It is easier for me to pour the ocean into this hole than for you to understand the Trinity."

## How Far Analysis Goes

The Trinity overwhelms us. We can analyze it to an extent. We can divide it up. We can distinguish the persons, their relations, and their personal properties. We can speak of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, each a subsistent relation within the one divine nature. We can speak of the missions of the Son and the Holy Spirit, through which the triune God draws us into his own life.

We can talk about the Trinity in history. We can talk about the Trinity in theology. We can apply the dictums of philosophy to the Trinity. But, as Maritain said, "a philosophy unaware of mystery would not be a philosophy." We can expand our mind and try to grasp as much as we can, but we still find ourselves unable to. The first step is to understand that we are involved in it already, that we are implicated in that very mystery.

It is not wrong to study the Trinity — in fact, there are many things to study about it, and that is why we actually have official Church teaching called dogma. Flannery O'Connor said: "Dogma is the guardian of mystery. The doctrines are spiritually significant in ways that we cannot fathom." So it is not wrong to study dogmas, and it is not wrong to bring our questions, distinctions, and careful analysis to the Trinity — but we must recognize that the Trinity can never be merely a problem to be solved. The Trinity is a mystery to be worshiped.

## A Way of Approaching All Reality

Mystery is not just a category that we can put things in. It is a way of approaching reality. I can approach this table as a mystery. I am involved in it. I have a history with it, a story to tell, a way in which I am connected to it. It is not just a horizontal plane elevated with finite width and length. Now this table is something that separates me from you, but it also is something that allows me to be here with you. We recognize that we have a part to play in it.

Even this lamp here can be approached as a mystery. I cannot look at the light itself, but by that light, I see other things. At first, an engineered product might seem like the opposite of a mystery: it was designed to solve a problem. But this lamp has memories for me. It has been here since my first retreat at Rednal, the Oratory’s retreat house. I replaced the light bulb in it myself during a hard time in my priesthood, when this place was for me a place of solace. Now every light bulb speaks to me of God providing for me during a very difficult time. Now there is a story attached to it. Even there, an engineered product can be engaged as a story, as a mystery brimming with the invisible.

Even more so than this table or lamp, every person is a mystery to me in some way — which is another way of saying that every person is someone to be loved.

The engagement with our world, all creation, as mystery, is preparation for engaging God as mystery.

In the ancient world, engaging the cosmos as mystery was not so uncommon: even grown men would look up at the sky at night, see the stars, draw pictures in the sky, and connect them to their maps of meaning through the myths that they would tell, the stories that they would share with their children and with others in society. The whole cosmos was a mystery — they engaged with the sky, with the stars, as if it involved them.

For them, as for Elisabeth, the moon really did follow. The stars and celestial bodies really do belong to a creation that speaks to us and concerns us — in the sense that we are made for relationship and live in relation to everything else. 

When I observe the stars at night, I do not look up there and see constellations — even if I might be able to identify a few. When I think of the stars, I know what they are. I know they are tremendous balls of plasma, many light-years away — but actually, to me, that knowledge moves me more towards wonder. When I know more about what it is, it is even more interesting and amazing. Astronomy is one of those sciences in which greater knowledge can lead to greater wonder. The more I understand the immense scale, beauty, and order of creation, the more astonishing it becomes.

The problematic need not be opposed to mystery and wonder. Think of the fact that we can actually travel to the moon and land on it, and how many thousands of problems had to be solved to accomplish that feat six times. It was amazing for those astronauts there. The first time anyone went around the moon — before they even landed on it — they were the first human beings ever to see an earthrise. These astronauts looking back on the Earth and seeing it all as one planet moved them towards the idea of a greater unity. The earthrise was a wonder — and a wonder that was transmitted back to the world; years later, the Apollo 17 astronauts took the "blue marble" photograph, the whole Earth in a single frame. It was a movement towards a wonder, seeing our place in the whole.

## The Proper Response

When we talk about the Trinity as mystery going forward on this retreat, we need to hold in our minds what we mean by mystery. We must not use the word mystery as an excuse to say that the Trinity is inaccessible to us, because that is exactly the wrong approach. To approach mystery is to access it, to enter in, to be enveloped by it, to see that we ourselves are included and caught up in a relationship.

We can treat the Trinity as so far away, so transcendent, and so separate from us that we have nothing to do with it. We can analyze it from afar, apply theology and Christian history to it, and say, “Well, that is Modalism; that is Patripassianism; that is Subordinationism.” Or we can understand that we are already living in it, that the Trinity involves us. We have our origin in the love of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, spilling over into creation; each of us is the result of a thought of God, willed by him and made for him. We want to say, "No. Somehow we are separate. Somehow everything is determined. Somehow everything is just coming forth from matter, from different material causes — maybe even randomly — in order to produce us, where we are right now." No, none of us is meaningless. None of us is random. Each one of us is willed. Because all of us come forth from the Trinity, we are made for the triune love. We are made to be inserted, through Christ, into the love of God: Father, Son, Holy Spirit. We are implicated in the Holy Trinity.

How do we practice this? Perhaps simply by recognizing — just seeing that there is mystery here, that in some way the love of God is spilling out here. We hear the voice of the Father: “This is my beloved Son.” Those words belong uniquely to Christ, the eternal Son, but through him they are extended to us by adoption. As Christians — as other Christs — we receive the Spirit through whom we cry, “Abba, Father.” By grace, we are joined at the level of our beings to this love. We cannot dismiss this mystery: it is absolutely central to our faith, absolutely central to our prayer, absolutely central to our very selves.

That Trinity — the love that moves the sun, the stars, and the moon that follows us as we travel — that Trinity is within us, by grace. God has come to dwell in our hearts. We have been baptized into Christ and given the indwelling Trinity; we have become a home for the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, even as we ache to be at home in the Father’s house in heaven.

The Triune God is so beyond our finite grasp that he encompasses us in his infinite embrace. 

The proper Christian response to this mystery is to love, adore, and worship.
